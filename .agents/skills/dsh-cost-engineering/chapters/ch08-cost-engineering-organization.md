# Chapter 8 — Cost Engineering Organization

## Core Idea

"Cost engineering efforts at most companies can only be described, at best, as sporadic, inconsistent, and mostly ineffective." Resources get placed in engineering, purchasing, finance or operations but rarely in all of them simultaneously, and rarely across the whole product life cycle. The intensity and scope is "very person-dependent" — which means it disappears when the person or the sponsor does.

The chapter prescribes: a **cost engineering organization reporting to the CFO** with experts matrixed into every function; a **culture** enforced through the product development gates; a **capability development plan** including a career path; a specific hiring profile; and the software category that is changing the practice.

## Frameworks Introduced

**The benchmark organization** (Figure 8.1). Head of cost engineering reports directly to the **CFO**. Cost optimization experts are embedded in four functions and report in a matrix to both the head of cost engineering and their functional/product-line head:

| Embedded in | Responsibility |
|---|---|
| Engineering | Work with designers and engineers to optimize designs for cost, especially early in development |
| Purchasing | Work with suppliers to achieve optimal cost including logistics |
| Operations | Work with in-house manufacturing engineers on optimal processing cost |
| Finance | Bring costing discipline and control to other functions and product lines; consolidate cost data into business cases and customer quotes |

The head of cost engineering also keeps a central staff who set **standards for processes and procedures** and who are **deployable experts** in estimating and optimization techniques.

**Why the CFO.** Cost engineering is a **cost controlling function** — structurally analogous to an accounting controller charged with cost and profit reporting discipline — and it ties naturally to the customer quoting function (business controlling), also under Finance, which drives competitive quotes. The chapter also entertains the alternatives: reporting to the **CEO** ("since cost management is such a critical function, just as much as engineering and purchasing, why not give it an equal status?"); reporting to **Purchasing or Engineering** is rejected because of "the risk of being pulled into their core activities."

**The three-part capability plan**: (1) recruit and develop early — internship/apprenticeship, then a career path rotating through engineering, manufacturing, purchasing and finance; (2) build a full internal training course with mentorship, since universities do not teach this — "it would not be a stretch to expect this training material to span an equivalent of two elective courses in college"; (3) provide advancement — junior → senior cost engineer → supervisor → manager → director → VP or chief executive — and treat cost engineers as candidates for roles in other functions.

## Key Concepts

- **The lone-cost-engineer failure sequence.** A head of engineering hires one person with cost optimization experience → that person has no authority inside or outside engineering, works within processes not designed to support optimization, and nobody else has aligned objectives → engineers keep optimizing only for "a product that works" → some wins accumulate → a few more cost engineers are hired → the head of engineering leaves → the new head reallocates resources to core design → the cost optimization engineers are transferred or fired → "that is usually the end of the company's cost optimization efforts." The same story plays out identically in purchasing, finance or operations.
- **Why the trend is improving.** Globalization is intensifying competition and forcing cost focus. Japanese firms' continuous-improvement culture and cost planning techniques (target costing) are spreading; their advantage is cost discipline **throughout** development, which prevents late overruns and, when overruns happen, forces the time and effort to optimize back to budget. That discipline typically comes from a dedicated cost planning group inside finance reporting to the CFO.
- **Culture is what happens, not what is posted.** "Culture is not a poster in a conference room that states what the management wants the culture to be; it is what actually happens in the conference room." Management must give cultural direction and then drive it daily.
- **How the culture is actually enforced**: the CEO and management stress cost engineering's importance; it is **built into the product development process**; the process is followed by insisting every step is completed correctly; cost engineering reviews sit at each critical gate from concept design to launch; at each gate the cross-functional team reviews cost versus target and develops a gap-closure plan; a list of cost reduction ideas with an implementation plan is produced; and **clear roles and responsibilities are assigned**.
- **Openness to change is the second cultural pillar.** Larger companies impose systems and procedures that inhibit change — "sometimes even mimicking country governments in its bureaucracy." Those rules exist to prevent execution failures and satisfy quality certifications, but they restrict variance and drive creativity out, even though creativity is essential to innovation. (Recommended: *Switch: How to Change Things When Change Is Hard*, Chip and Dan Heath.)
- **Talent is scarce and untrained.** Demand is rising across industries; universities, colleges and vocational schools do not teach cost engineering because it is not acknowledged as a core function; most companies have no training or apprenticeship. "Cost engineering is usually self-taught or trained on the job."
- **It is treated as a dead-end job — wrongly.** Core functions do not see cost engineers as having enough core-function experience, "despite the fact that individuals engaged in cost engineering actually acquire much more knowledge about company operations, its suppliers, products, and manufacturing processes than almost anyone else in the company."
- **The knowledge a cost engineer actually accumulates**: manufacturing processes, factory organization and plant finances (from time at own and supplier plants); product development, design down to GD&T level, testing and launch (from working with engineering); commercial negotiation and supplier relationships (from purchasing); cost structures, cost allocation and financial reporting (from finance/accounting, in-house and at suppliers). "No buyer or engineer will ever gain this kind of cross-functional knowledge working only in their functions."
- **The argument for fast-tracking.** A CEO's development path deliberately gathers experience across core functions; a cost engineer gathers exactly that, so "cost engineering professionals should be fast tracked or prioritized for promotions."
- **Software is changing the practice.** Two generations: (a) estimating/allocation/target costing/business case platforms — **FACTON, Siemens, aPriori**, plus tooling cost estimation from **Schmale, Siemens, aPriori** — carrying thousands of raw material, labor and machine rates by region, saving enormous data collection effort; (b) real-time estimating at the engineer's fingertips — **aPriori, Costimator, Munro** — taking 3D models and estimating part cost with no input from cost estimators or suppliers, turning days or weeks into immediate feedback on design decisions. The author's expected next step: software that suggests design solutions from functional analysis or TRIZ concepts automatically, replacing some brainstorming workshops.

## Mental Models

- **Person-dependent means temporary.** If cost optimization survives only while one sponsor stays, it is not a capability.
- **Authority follows reporting line.** Embedding without a matrix report to a CFO-level owner gives the embedded person no leverage over the function they must challenge.
- **Cost engineering is cost *controlling*, not cost *accounting*.** Accounting looks at the past or short-term future; cost estimating and optimization look mid- to long-term.
- **Hire for curiosity.** The single most important trait, above analytical mindset and attention to detail.

## Anti-patterns

- **A single cost optimization engineer inside one function**, with no authority, no aligned objectives, and no process support.
- **Reporting cost engineering into Purchasing or Engineering** — the function gets absorbed into their core activities.
- **A culture poster instead of gate reviews.**
- **Bureaucracy that eliminates variance** and with it creativity, while claiming to protect quality.
- **Treating cost engineering as a terminal specialism** with no career ladder — the only people who stay are those content to remain a cost engineer for life; "those who want to be leaders and influencers will eventually leave cost engineering or never get into it in the first place."
- **Assuming cost engineering skills are too unique to transfer** to other functions — "This is a mistake and the opposite is true."
- **Hiring purely for analytical skill.** Necessary but not sufficient.

## Reference Tables

**Organizational design options weighed in the chapter**

| Reporting line | Argument for | Argument against |
|---|---|---|
| **CFO** (the benchmark) | Cost engineering is a controlling function, analogous to the accounting controller; ties to business controlling / customer quoting; authority and objectives align across functions | Accounting focus is past/short-term while cost engineering is mid/long-term |
| **CEO** | Cost management is as critical as engineering and purchasing — give it equal status | Presented as a future possibility, not the current benchmark |
| **Purchasing or Engineering** | Proximity to where much of the work happens | Risk of being pulled into their core activities outweighs proximity |
| **None / embedded individual** | Cheap | Not sustainable; ends with the sponsor |

**The cost engineer hiring profile**

| Trait | Rank | How to probe it in an interview or résumé |
|---|---|---|
| **Curiosity** | **Most critical** — "curiosity to understand what is behind the assumptions and calculations will drive the most success" | Look for interests that showcase it, e.g. travel and history |
| **Change agent / challenges the status quo** | Second, especially for cost optimization roles | Ask whether they have proposed changes and how they drove them to completion; whether they were part of an organization seeking change in society or the community; whether they can speak to large groups and sell things or concepts |
| Analytical mindset | Important but commonly over-weighted | Standard assessment |
| Attention to detail | Important but commonly over-weighted | Standard assessment |

The chapter's framing: not everyone can be a good cost engineer, and the traits are unique to the function — but they also transfer well outward, reinforcing the fast-track argument.

## Worked Example

**Building the organization from the failure state — the chapter's own before/after.**

*Before (the state most companies are in).* A head of engineering is tasked with improving the cost of the company's designs. He adds one person with cost optimization experience to his staff and charges them with leading all cost optimization initiatives. Trace what that person actually has:
- **No cooperation** — their work is out of scope of the engineering process.
- **No authority** — none inside engineering, none outside it.
- **No supporting process** — established processes and procedures were not designed to support cost optimization.
- **No aligned objectives anywhere else** — engineers continue to focus on their single objective, designing a product that works.

"The probability of successfully engaging the entire organization in cost optimization efforts are therefore very limited to none at all." Some opportunities get found; some get implemented by persuasion; occasionally the success is large enough that a few more cost optimization engineers are hired. Then the head of engineering leaves, the new head decides more resources are needed in core design, and the cost optimization engineers are transferred — if not fired — to the design group. End of the effort. The chapter stresses this outcome is the same whether the group sat in engineering, purchasing, finance or operations.

*After (the benchmark structure).* Reconstruct the same activity with the four structural changes:
1. **A head of cost engineering reporting to the CFO** — establishing cost engineering as a controlling function with the same standing as accounting control, tied to the quoting/business-controlling function.
2. **Experts embedded in engineering, purchasing, operations and finance**, each with the specific remit listed in the Frameworks table, reporting in a **matrix** to both their function/product line and the head of cost engineering. "With this organizational structure, the whole company is engaged in the cost optimization effort."
3. **A central staff** under the head of cost engineering owning standards for processes and procedures, plus deployable estimating and optimization experts who can be sent to any project.
4. **Objectives aligned** across the functions, so cost is not somebody else's problem.

*And the culture that keeps it alive.* Cost engineering reviews at every critical gate of the product development process, from concept design to launch. At each gate the cross-functional team reviews cost against target, develops a gap-closure plan, produces a list of cost reduction ideas with implementation plans, and assigns clear roles and responsibilities. Management, starting with the CEO, insists every step is completed correctly. "Over time, the discipline of enforcing the cost engineering process and continuous improvement mindset will lead to culture change" — employees stop improvising around their own past experience and preferences, and the results the discipline produces earn acceptance.

*And the pipeline that staffs it.* Recruit early (internship or apprenticeship), rotate through engineering, manufacturing, purchasing and finance to build the required knowledge base, run an internal training course with mentorship spanning roughly two college electives' worth of material, provide a ladder from junior cost engineer to VP or chief executive, and treat cost engineers as strong candidates for roles in other functions — arguably fast-tracked, because their cross-functional knowledge exceeds that of any single-function peer.

## Key Takeaways

1. Person-dependent cost optimization always ends; only an organization survives.
2. The benchmark structure is a head of cost engineering reporting to the CFO with matrixed experts in engineering, purchasing, operations and finance, plus a central standards-and-expertise staff.
3. Reporting to Purchasing or Engineering risks absorption into their core activities; reporting to the CEO is a defensible alternative.
4. Culture is enforced through gate reviews with targets, gap plans, idea lists and named owners — not through statements of intent.
5. Openness to change is a required cultural pillar; large-company bureaucracy actively works against it.
6. Cost engineering talent is scarce and untrained; build your own training (~two college electives) and mentorship.
7. Give cost engineers a career ladder and treat them as candidates for other functions — arguably fast-track them.
8. Hire for **curiosity** first, then change-agent ability; analytical mindset and attention to detail are necessary but insufficient.
9. Costing software (FACTON, Siemens, aPriori; Schmale for tooling; aPriori, Costimator, Munro for real-time 3D-model estimating) removes rate-collection effort and pushes cost feedback to the moment of design.
