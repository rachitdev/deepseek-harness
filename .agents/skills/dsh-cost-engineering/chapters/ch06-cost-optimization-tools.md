# Chapter 6 — Cost Optimization Tools

## Core Idea

Once cost is understood, the first move is **prioritization by size of spend**, not by convenience. "There are many companies that spend an inordinate amount of time optimizing the cost of things that contribute only a small percentage to the overall cost, because they have no idea what their cost is and how to prioritize it."

Then choose the tool that fits the cost type: lean for the manufacturing process, value stream mapping for the supply chain, functional analysis and/or TRIZ combined with benchmarking for design.

## Frameworks Introduced

**Prioritize by cost share.** The assembler's arithmetic: assembly factories are 5%–10% of overall cost while purchased components are up to 80%. A resource investment yielding 10% improvement returns 1% of total cost if aimed at the factory and 8% if aimed at the supply base. Companies that do recognize this often stop at a "lean event" at the supplier's factory — which again addresses only the supplier's process and ignores the rest of the value chain, even when that supplier is itself an assembler.

**Tool-to-problem mapping**

| Problem type | Tool |
|---|---|
| Manufacturing process efficiency | Lean manufacturing |
| Full supply chain / value chain | Value stream mapping (VSM) |
| Competitiveness of the design | Benchmarking / teardown |
| Function delivered per dollar | Functional analysis (VA/VE) |
| Stuck for alternatives during functional analysis | TRIZ |
| All of the above, concentrated | Cost reduction workshop |

**Lean's three levers**: cycle time, OEE, and inventory (incoming, in-process, outgoing, plus inventory between customer and supplier factories).

**Value = function ÷ cost** — functional analysis asks, for each function: can it be delivered cheaper, or can more function be delivered at the same cost?

**TRIZ 40 principles** (Figure 6.2) — Altshuller's patterns from analysis of thousands of patents, used to break brainstorming deadlock.

## Key Concepts

- **Lean pays most where lean is new.** For companies already practising lean, further lean work yields little. A machining company finding 10 s on a 500 s cycle has saved nothing unless a laborer is released or another product fills the freed time; savings materialize only after incremental improvements accumulate into usable open capacity.
- **Lean can be exported rather than achieved.** If the customer factory is extremely lean but the supply chain is not, suppliers carry the inventory the customer refused. "If suppliers are carrying one month's worth of inventory to allow for its customer to be lean, the suppliers will eventually pass on the cost to the customer and the cost savings will be minimal or non-existent." Kanban and JIT are the target state, but only across the whole chain.
- **What a VSM records.** Movement of material end to end: customer order → raw material supplier → processing/assembly → delivery. For each process: number of operators, cycle times, uptime, inventory levels at each step, shipping time, and the places material physically travels. "Having this information is a great cost optimization idea generator since it makes any inefficiency very obvious."
- **What VSMs typically expose in a first-time company**: the same part transported multiple times (sometimes across an ocean), excessive inventory across the chain, bottlenecks preventing optimal equipment utilization.
- **Benchmarking works only with costs attached.** Tear down competitor products and cost every component individually. Participants often have zero costing background, so without costed components they generate "improvements" that are actually cost increases — e.g. adopting a competitor's seal style that costs more than your own.
- **Benchmarking's real obstacle is engineering pride.** Engineers grow attached to their designs, focus on part performance, and prefer not to touch what works. This makes the exercise ineffective unless driven by top management and run by well-trained facilitators.
- **Functional analysis at macro and micro scale.** Macro: a car's function is moving people from A to B — asking for the same function at lower cost is how alternative powertrains were born. Micro: asking for the same or better battery life at smaller size drove new raw materials and optimized electrical routings; over two decades batteries went from large packs holding a few miles of charge to half-size packs holding four times the charge at one-tenth the cost.
- **TRIZ examples given.** Principle #22 "converting harm into benefit" → regenerative braking charging an EV battery. Principle #28 "substituting a mechanical system with an electromagnetic one" → a motor replacing an engine.
- **Cost reduction workshop ingredients** (all required): top-leadership support and attendee dedication; the right people with cross-functional product knowledge; challenging and motivating cost reduction targets; a proper process and a skilled facilitator; the right tools to open minds; the right facilities; and enough time — **3–5 days**.
- **Two powers to harness in a workshop.** Creative power, from cross-functional perspectives (or outside consultants); and **the power of ignorance** — deliberately including people who lack product knowledge and will ask the "dumb" questions that spark ideas.
- **Keep the method flexible.** SAVE International's guidelines are good but can be restrictive about process and tools. A facilitator may insist on functional analysis plus Alex Osborne brainstorming when teardown plus TRIZ would fit better — especially when workshops are new to the company.

## Mental Models

- **Aim at the 80%, not the 10%.** Where the money is, not where the access is.
- **A lean event at one supplier is not supply-chain optimization.**
- **Cost-blind idea generation generates cost increases.** Cost the components before the workshop, not after.
- **Ignorance is a resource.** Deliberately seat someone who does not know why it has always been done that way.
- **Product performance always wins the day job.** Cross-functional teams never find time for cost optimization inside normal work, which is exactly why a dedicated, time-boxed workshop produces results that "would normally be obvious."

## Anti-patterns

- **Optimizing the 5%–10% you control while ignoring the 80% you buy.**
- **Celebrating cycle-time reductions that release no resource.**
- **Being lean at your own factory by pushing inventory upstream.**
- **Benchmarking without component-level cost estimates.**
- **Letting design attachment kill teardown**; running it without executive sponsorship or trained facilitation.
- **Over-prescribing workshop methodology** when the problem calls for a different tool.
- **Running a workshop without targets, without the right people, or in too little time** (under 3 days).

**Cost reduction workshop checklist** (from the chapter's list of "key ingredients")

| Ingredient | Test |
|---|---|
| Top leadership support | Is there a named executive sponsor and a mandate? |
| Attendee dedication | Are attendees released from day jobs for the duration? |
| Right people | Cross-functional product knowledge present? Suppliers invited? Someone deliberately ignorant of the product? |
| Targets | Challenging and motivating cost reduction targets set in advance? |
| Process + facilitator | Defined process; skilled facilitator who can motivate a team to find *and implement* ideas |
| Tools | Functional analysis, teardown, TRIZ, VSM available — and matched to the problem, not prescribed |
| Facilities | Dedicated space (co-location if the effort is large) |
| Time | 3–5 days minimum |

## Worked Example

**The connector cost reduction workshop.** A cost engineer convenes a cross-functional team to brainstorm on the six-pin over-molded connector from Chapters 2 and 3, working through the tools in sequence. What each tool produced:

1. **Value stream mapping.** The map showed that although final assembly happens in **Mexico**, the pins are stamped in **California**, the wires are made in **China**, and the resin comes from **Texas**. Idea: localize all three in Mexico, yielding at a minimum logistics and inventory savings. (This is the same localization lever that Chapter 3's negotiation table lists under packaging/logistics.)
2. **Benchmarking / teardown.** Competitors use **nickel plating on the pins instead of silver** — a large saving. Chapter 4's Table 4.7 quantifies the mechanism: silver is ~$15–$20/ounce while nickel is ~$5.00–$7.50/lb. The teardown also showed competitors' resin probably has **less glass fiber (GF15 vs the current GF30)** — another saving, since additives raise resin price. That in turn sparked a third idea: **allow regrind and reuse of scrapped resin during molding**, which Chapter 4's Table 4.8 notes is worth 10%–60% of shot weight (Chapter 2's estimate explicitly assumed *no* regrind for simplicity, so this is real money left on the table).
3. **Functional analysis.** Costing each connector function revealed that **one of the six wires can be eliminated** — directly removing one purchased wire at $0.04/part and shortening assembly content.
4. **TRIZ.** Pushing further, the team realized **all the connector's functions could be replaced by wireless communication** — a complete elimination of the part. The team judged it not feasible at this time, but recorded it.

The author's comment on the result is the transferable lesson: "Most of the generated cost reduction ideas are not drastic and would normally be obvious. Nevertheless, these opportunities were missed or forgotten as the team rushed to develop and launch a working product. Since product performance always takes priority, members of a cross-functional team often do not have time to think about cost optimization."

**Real-Life Anecdote — the co-located commodity teams.** An automotive OEM's CEO directed the creation of **cross-functional teams for every component/commodity purchased from suppliers**, gave each a **30% cost reduction objective over five years** for its spend, and pushed the same objective into **every function's performance objectives**. Faced with that, teams built strategies spanning technology improvements, design changes, commercial negotiations and logistics improvements. Rather than meeting occasionally, the teams were **co-located for three months outside their normal office space — effectively one very long workshop**. Result: "a drastic decrease in cost with most teams reaching their 30% reduction targets." The three structural elements: CEO-level directive, a hard numerical target, and aligned objectives across every function.

## Key Takeaways

1. Prioritize optimization by where the cost actually is — usually purchased components, not your own factory.
2. Match the tool to the cost type: lean (process), VSM (chain), benchmarking (competitiveness), functional analysis and TRIZ (design).
3. Lean returns most where lean is new; savings are real only when a resource is released or freed capacity is filled.
4. Do not achieve leanness by pushing inventory onto suppliers — it comes back in price.
5. A first VSM almost always exposes redundant transport, excess inventory and bottlenecks, and it prioritizes the biggest cost drivers for you.
6. Benchmark with costed components and name the specific best practice behind each lower cost.
7. Workshops need sponsorship, dedication, targets, facilitation, tools, facilities and 3–5 days — and they need both cross-functional expertise and deliberate ignorance.
8. Directive from the top plus a hard target plus aligned functional objectives is what makes cost reduction stick.
