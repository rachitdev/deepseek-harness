# Chapter XIII — Your First Ultralearning Project

> "The beginning is always today." — Mary Shelley

## Core Idea

This is the operational chapter: a five-step pipeline from research to what you do after the project ends, plus the nine-principle self-audit you run *while* executing. Young's framing point is that the first project matters disproportionately — "A solid, well-researched, well-executed plan can give you the confidence to face harder challenges in the future. A bungled attempt is not a disaster, but it may make you reluctant to pursue future projects of a similar nature." He also closes the book's argument by naming the two legitimate alternatives to ultralearning and when each beats it.

## Frameworks Introduced

**Step 1 — Do your research.** "Research is a bit like packing a suitcase for a long voyage. You may not bring the right items, or you may forget something and need to buy it on the road. However, thinking ahead and packing your bags correctly will prevent a lot of fumbling later." The minimum checklist:

1. **What topic you're going to learn and its approximate scope.** If the goal is instrumental — start a business, get a promotion, do research for an article — figuring out *what you need to learn* determines how wide and deep to go. **"I suggest starting with rather a narrow scope, which can expand as you proceed."** His contrast: "Learning enough Mandarin Chinese to hold a fifteen-minute conversation on simple topics" is far more constrained than "Learn Chinese," which may include reading, writing, studying history, and more.
2. **The primary resources you're going to use.** Books, videos, classes, tutorials, guides, and people serving as mentors, coaches, and peers. Examples he gives: "I'm going to read and complete the exercises in a book on Python programming for beginners"; "I'm going to learn Spanish through online tutoring via italki.com"; "I'm going to practice drawing by making sketches." In some subjects static materials determine how you proceed; in others they support your practice. **Either way they "should be identified, purchased, borrowed, or enrolled in before you begin."**
3. **A benchmark for how others have successfully learned this skill.** "Almost any popular skill has online forums where those who have learned the skill previously can share their approaches.... This doesn't mean you need to follow exactly in their footsteps, but it will prevent you from completely missing something important." The Expert Interview Method from Chapter IV is the tool.
4. **Direct practice activities.** "Every skill and subject you're learning will be used somewhere eventually, even if it's as simple as using it to learn something else. Thinking about how you might use the skill can enable you to start finding opportunities to practice it as early as possible. If direct practice is impossible, you should nonetheless identify opportunities for practice that mimic the mental requirements of using the skill."
5. **Backup materials and drills.** "Backup materials are often good if you recognize that a certain tool or set of materials might be useful but you don't want to be overwhelmed in the beginning."

**Step 2 — Schedule your time.** Two reasons to plan the schedule in advance: "this way you subconsciously prioritize your project by setting it down on your calendar ahead of other things," and "learning is often frustrating and it is almost always easier to click over to Facebook, Twitter, or Netflix." Three decisions:

- **How much time you'll commit.** Often dictated by circumstance — a one-month employment gap, or a full schedule permitting a few hours a week. "Whatever time you can commit, decide on it in advance."
- **When you'll learn.** A few hours on Sunday? Waking an hour earlier? Evenings? Lunch breaks? **"I recommend setting a consistent schedule that is the same every week, rather than trying to fit in learning when you can. Consistency breeds good habits, reducing the effort required to study. If you have absolutely no choice, an ad hoc schedule is better than none, but it will require more discipline to sustain."**
  - *Optimizing the shape.* "Shorter, spaced time chunks are better for memory than crammed chunks are. However, some types of tasks, such as writing and programming, have a long warm-up time that may benefit from longer uninterrupted time chunks. The best way to find out what is best for you is to practice; if you find it takes a long time to warm up, opt for longer spaces in your schedule. If you find you can get to work within a few minutes of starting, shorter chunks of time spread out will be helpful for long-term retention."
- **The length of the project.** "I generally prefer shorter commitments to longer ones because they are easier to stick with. An intensive project that lasts a month has fewer potential interruptions from life or from your motivation changing and waning. If you have a big goal you want to accomplish that can't be done in a short time frame, I suggest breaking it up into multiple smaller ones of a few months each."
- **Then put it in the calendar.** Logistically this surfaces conflicts from vacations, work, and family events. Psychologically it beats a plan "written on a piece of paper tucked into a desk drawer," and "the act of scheduling demonstrates your seriousness." Young's own case: before the MIT Challenge he wrote a hypothetical schedule with study from 7 a.m. to 6 p.m. and only a short lunch break. "Although my actual schedule, in practice, rarely reached that ideal (even in my most intensive early days, I almost never got in eleven hours straight), the mere act of writing down the schedule helped prepare me psychologically." **The tell: "If you're unwilling to put time into your calendar, you're almost certainly unwilling to put in time to study. If you're waffling at this stage, that's a good sign your heart isn't really in the right place to get started."**
- **Bonus: the pilot week.** For projects of six months or more, "test your schedule for one week before you commit to it. This will give you firsthand knowledge of how difficult it will be and prevents overconfidence. If you already feel burned out after the first week, you may need to make adjustments. There's no shame in going back and retooling your plan to make it fit your life better. Making this kind of adjustment is a lot better than giving up midway because your plan was doomed from the start."

**Step 3 — Execute your plan, auditing against the nine principles.** No plan is perfect; expect to notice that you rely too much on passive reading, that your practice is "a winding detour away from where you will actually want to use it," or that you're memorizing without understanding. "That's okay. In some cases, you won't be able to have the perfect learning approach because the resources to do so don't exist. However, becoming sensitive to how the way you're learning isn't aligned with the principles is a good way to feel out changes you can make."

The nine self-audit question sets, verbatim in substance:

1. **Metalearning.** Have I researched the typical ways of learning this subject or skill? Have I interviewed successful learners about resources and advice? Have I spent about 10 percent of the total time preparing?
2. **Focus.** Am I focused, or multitasking and distracted? Am I skipping sessions or procrastinating? How long before I'm in a good flow? How long can I sustain focus before my mind wanders? How sharp is my attention? Should it be more concentrated for intensity or more diffuse for creativity?
3. **Directness.** Am I learning the skill in the way I'll eventually use it? If not, what mental processes are missing from my practice that exist in the real environment? How can I practice transferring what I learn from book/class/video to real life?
4. **Drill.** Am I focusing on the weakest points of my performance? What is the rate-limiting step holding me back? Does my learning feel like it's slowing down with too many components to master? If so, how can I split the complex skill into smaller, more manageable components?
5. **Retrieval.** Am I mostly reading and reviewing, or solving problems and recalling from memory without notes? Do I have some way of testing myself, or do I just assume I'll remember? Can I successfully explain what I learned yesterday, last week, a year ago? How do I know if I can?
6. **Feedback.** Am I getting honest feedback early, or dodging the punches and avoiding criticism? Do I know what I'm learning well and what I'm not? Am I using feedback correctly, or overreacting to noisy data?
7. **Retention.** Do I have a plan to remember this long term? Am I spacing my exposure? Am I turning factual knowledge into procedures I'll retain? Am I overlearning the most critical aspects?
8. **Intuition.** Do I deeply understand, or am I just memorizing? Could I teach these ideas and procedures to someone else? Is it clear *why* what I'm learning is true, or does it seem arbitrary and unrelated?
9. **Experimentation.** Am I getting stuck with my current resources and techniques? Do I need to branch out? How can I go beyond mastering the basics and create a unique style to solve problems creatively?

"Together these principles serve as **directions, not destinations**.... Do you need to switch resources? Do you need to stick to the same resources but spend more time on a different kind of practice? Should you seek out new environments for feedback, directness, or immersion? These are all subtle adjustments you can make along the way."

**Step 4 — Review your results.** After the project finishes (or if you pause it), analyze: "What went right? What went wrong? What should you do next time to avoid making those same mistakes?" Crucially, "Although the tendency is to blame willpower and motivation, very often the problems with projects can be traced back to their conception."

- *Young's own failure case.* A project to improve his Korean after the trip, at five hours per week. "It wasn't as successful as I had hoped because I didn't invest enough time in focusing on immersive, direct practice from the get-go. Instead, my study method depended a lot on textbook exercises, which were boring and didn't transfer too well to the real world. If I had thought a little more about it, I would have spent a week or two ahead of time trying to find places to practice, instead of trying to pivot midway, when I was already losing some motivation." The lesson he draws is uncomfortable and deliberate: "**mastering the principles is a lifelong process. Even after many experiences learning languages, and knowing what works well, I slipped into a less effective approach because I didn't plan my project adequately.**"
- *A project that mutated usefully.* He started a project to learn cognitive science more deeply from a book list; "eventually, however, a lot of that project morphed into a desire to do research for this book, which put me into contact with a lot of science, now combined with an outlet for a more direct way to apply it."
- *Analyze successes too.* "They can often tell you more than your failures because the reasons a successful project succeeded are the very elements you want to retain and replicate.... With ultralearning, as with all self-education, the goal isn't merely to learn one skill or subject but to hone and enhance your overall learning process."

**Step 5 — Choose to maintain, relearn, or master.** "With no plan in place, most knowledge eventually decays.... the best time to make a choice about how you're going to handle that is right after you learn something."

- **Option 1: Maintenance.** Enough practice to sustain the skill without pushing it further, usually via a habit of regular, possibly minimal, practice. Young's language schedule: thirty minutes a week per language in the first year, thirty minutes a month per language in the second. Alternative: integrate the skill into life — "This is how I maintain my programming skills, where I write Python scripts to handle work tasks that would otherwise be cumbersome or annoying.... This kind of lightweight usage is far from the deep math and algorithms I learned from my MIT coursework, but it is enough to keep a foot in the door if I want to embark on a bigger project at a later time." And because forgetting decays exponentially, "maintenance practice, too, can fall off on a decaying rate, so that the bulk of the knowledge you've acquired will be preserved."
- **Option 2: Relearning.** "For many skills the costs of relearning the skill later are smaller than the costs of keeping it continuously sharp." Two reasons. First, you probably learned more than you need, "so if some of that knowledge selectively decays due to disuse, it is automatically going to be the less important knowledge." His example: keeping the ability to prove modal logic theorems current has only marginal value; "Knowing what modal logic is and where I might apply it in case I want to learn something that requires it is probably enough." Second, relearning is easier than first-time learning — performance drops dramatically but the knowledge is "likely inaccessible rather than completely forgotten," so "doing a refresher course or practice series can be enough to reactivate most of it in a fraction of the time." Best for subjects used infrequently where the need won't arrive without warning. **"Often, recognizing that a certain domain of knowledge is helpful for a particular problem type is more important than the details of solving the problem, since the latter can be relearned but forgetting the former will cut you off from solving those problems."**
- **Option 3: Mastery.** Continue at a lighter pace, or launch another ultralearning project. "A common pattern I've noticed in my own learning is that an initial project covers a wider territory and some basics and exposes new avenues for learning that were previously obscured." You might follow up a subtopic, or transfer the skill to a new domain — after China, Young wanted to learn to *read* Chinese better, which had been only incidental while traveling. "Mastery is a long road that extends far beyond a single project. Sometimes the barriers you overcome in your initial effort are enough to clear the way for a slow process of accumulation.... On the other hand, some projects will get stuck, and you will need to spend time unlearning and push through your frustrations again."

**The two alternatives to ultralearning.**

- **Alternative 1: Low-intensity habits.** "Low-intensity habits work well when engaging in learning is spontaneous, your frustration level is low, and learning is automatically rewarding. In these cases, when the barriers to learning are fairly low, all you need to do is show up. No fancy project, principles, or effort is required." Examples: once you reach conversational level in a language, living where it's spoken accumulates vocabulary steadily; once you're good enough at programming to use it professionally, the job pushes you along; once you can read denser books on a topic, reading them "is mostly a matter of putting in time, not developing ingenious learning strategies." There's a spectrum, and most habits sit in the middle — you may know enough Excel to write spreadsheet macros but need a push to practice, or speak well but still need guts to go on stage.
  - **The decision rule:** "Habits tend to work best when the act of learning is mostly a process of **accumulation**, adding new skills and knowledge. Ultralearning and more deliberate efforts are better suited to when improvement in a field requires **unlearning** ineffective behaviors or skills. Increasing your vocabulary in a foreign language is often a slow process of accumulation.... Improving your pronunciation, on the other hand, is an act of unlearning. You're training yourself to use different muscular movements that aren't natural to you. Ultralearning also tends to be better for areas in which learning has greater frustration barriers and psychological obstacles."
  - **The enjoyment argument.** There's a real trade-off between effective and enjoyable, "However, in my own experience, I've noticed that enjoyment tends to come from being good at things. Once you feel competent in a skill, it starts to get a lot more fun. Therefore, although a tension between the two can exist in the short term, I think pursuing aggressive ultralearning projects is often the surer way to enjoy learning more, as you're more likely to reach a level where learning automatically becomes fun."
  - Note that even the exemplars mix strategies: "Benny Lewis, for example, does do intensive learning projects for languages, but he has learned most of his languages over repeat visits to the countries they are spoken in, digging deeper into languages he previously established in intense bursts. Roger Craig did learn aggressively to win at *Jeopardy!*, but he also engaged in more leisurely acquisition of trivia when his appearance on the game show wasn't imminent."
- **Alternative 2: Formal, structured education.** "There is no contradiction in pursuing ultralearning within a school or university. That might be the best way to learn the skills you want to acquire. Just treat it like any other resource." Reasons to choose formal education: **credentials** ("If those are necessary or recommended for your chosen line of work, you might need to be satisfied with making sacrifices for your learning in order to acquire them"); and the **learning environment** — "Design and art schools often function like apprenticeships. Some programs allow for team projects that are difficult to start on your own. Finally, the postgraduate levels of academia create communities where immersion is possible, so that you acquire not only the ideas that are written down in books and papers but those that are communicated indirectly between experts in their fields." **"The message of this book isn't that you should drop out of school to learn on your own but that you should take control over your own learning, wherever that may be."** And: "The correct mindset to cultivate isn't a rejection of anything slower or standardized but a recognition that the possibilities for learning anything are considerably broader than they might first appear."

## Key Concepts

- **The real obstacle.** "The biggest obstacle to ultralearning is simply that most people don't care enough about their own self-education to get started."
- **Why the first project is different.** "One successful project tends to lead to others. It's usually the first project that requires the most thought and care."
- **The book's closing statement of purpose.** "The goal of ultralearning is to expand the opportunities available to you, not narrow them. It is to create new avenues for learning and to push yourself to pursue them aggressively rather than timidly waiting by the sidelines. This is not going to be a method suitable for everyone, but for those who feel inspired to use it, I hope it provides a start."

## Mental Models

- **Conception failures masquerade as willpower failures.** When a project stalls, look first at how it was designed, not at your discipline.
- **Narrow scope, then expand.** A constrained first target ("fifteen-minute conversation on simple topics") is easier to plan, schedule, and finish than a category ("learn Chinese").
- **The calendar as a commitment test.** Unwillingness to schedule is a reliable early signal of unwillingness to do the work.
- **Warm-up time determines chunk size.** Long-warm-up tasks (writing, programming) want long blocks despite the spacing evidence; short-warm-up tasks want spread-out chunks.
- **Forgetting the *existence* of a technique is worse than forgetting its details.** Details relearn quickly; missing recognition cuts you off entirely.

## Anti-patterns

- Beginning without resources identified, purchased, borrowed, or enrolled in.
- Hoping to find the time later rather than deciding in advance.
- An ad hoc schedule when a consistent weekly one is possible.
- Committing to a six-month schedule with no pilot week, then discovering in month two that it was never survivable.
- Depending on textbook exercises with poor transfer — Young's own Korean mistake.
- Pivoting a project's method midway, when motivation has already started to erode.
- Reviewing only failed projects and never successful ones.
- Ending a project with no maintenance, relearning, or mastery decision.
- Reading the book as an argument to drop out or to reject slower, standardized instruction.

## Reference Tables

**The five-step pipeline**

| Step | Output |
|---|---|
| 1. Research | Scope, primary resources, benchmark, direct practice activities, backup materials and drills |
| 2. Schedule | How much time, when, how long, entered in the calendar; pilot week if ≥6 months |
| 3. Execute | Continuous nine-principle audit and mid-course adjustment |
| 4. Review | What went right, what went wrong, what to change — for successes as well as failures |
| 5. Decide | Maintenance / relearning / mastery |

**Strategy selection**

| Situation | Strategy |
|---|---|
| Learning is accumulation, spontaneous, low-frustration, self-rewarding | Low-intensity habit |
| Improvement requires unlearning (pronunciation, stale programming habits) | Ultralearning |
| High frustration barriers or psychological obstacles to any practice | Ultralearning |
| Credentials legally required or strongly recommended | Formal education |
| Apprenticeship-style studio, team projects, or research-community immersion needed | Formal education |
| Any of the above | Self-direction still applies — treat the institution as a resource |

**Post-project decision**

| Option | Use when | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Maintenance | You want the skill available continuously | A recurring, decaying habit (Young: 30 min/week → 30 min/month) |
| Relearning | The skill is used infrequently and its need is foreseeable | Refresher time later; risk of losing recognition of when it applies |
| Mastery | The first project exposed avenues worth pursuing | Another project, or sustained lighter practice |

## Worked Example

**Young's Korean maintenance project, as a diagnosed failure.**

The chapter's most instructive worked case is one that didn't work, and it is worth walking through the pipeline to see exactly where it broke.

| Step | What he did | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Research | Went straight to textbook exercises as the primary resource. No benchmark against how others maintain a language; no direct practice activities identified in advance. | **Failed.** Checklist items 3 and 4 skipped. |
| 2. Schedule | Five hours per week. | Fine — the time was there. |
| 3. Execute | Discovered mid-project that the practice was indirect and boring, and attempted to pivot. | Pivot attempted too late: "when I was already losing some motivation." |
| 4. Review | Diagnosed the cause as conception, not willpower: "If I had thought a little more about it, I would have spent a week or two ahead of time trying to find places to practice." | The review is the salvage. |

The failing item is checklist item 4 — *direct practice activities* — which is the one item that requires you to answer, before you start, the question: where will this skill actually be used? For a maintenance project on a conversational language, the answer is "in conversation," and the practice should have been conversation. Textbook exercises fail the directness audit in Step 3 ("what mental processes are missing from my practice that exist in the real environment?") — but by then he was already inside the project with declining motivation.

Compare the successful version of the same problem, from Chapter X: for the four languages he wanted to *keep*, he scheduled thirty minutes of conversation per week via italki over Skype — direct practice, identified in advance, entered as a recurring commitment. Same person, same languages, same year; the difference is entirely in Step 1.

**The general lesson Young extracts:** "This struggle illustrates that mastering the principles is a lifelong process."

## Key Takeaways

- Five steps: research, schedule, execute with the nine-principle audit, review, then choose maintenance / relearning / mastery.
- Start narrow. Have every resource in hand before day one. Identify direct practice activities *in the research phase*.
- Put the schedule in the calendar; unwillingness to schedule is a reliable predictor of unwillingness to study.
- Run a pilot week for anything six months or longer.
- The nine principles are directions, not destinations — audit continuously and make subtle adjustments.
- Blame conception before willpower when a project fails; review your successes too.
- Habits fit accumulation; ultralearning fits unlearning and high frustration barriers; formal education fits credentials and genuine apprenticeship or immersion environments.
