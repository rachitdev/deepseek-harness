# Chapter XIV — An Unconventional Education

> "Give me a dozen healthy infants, well-formed, and my own specified world to bring them up in and I'll guarantee to take any one at random and train him to become any type of specialist I might select—doctor, lawyer, artist, merchant-chief and, yes, even beggar-man and thief." — John Watson

## Core Idea

Everything up to here has been about the individual who *chooses* to ultralearn. This chapter asks whether ultralearning can be *fostered* in someone else — a child, a student, an employee — without producing the misery that externally imposed intensity usually causes. Laszlo Polgar's deliberate experiment in raising geniuses is Young's loophole case: three daughters raised on chess from early childhood, all three reaching world-class level, and none of them apparently harmed. Young audits the Polgar method against all nine principles, then offers three cautious suggestions for home, school, and workplace — while being candid that the experiment proves less than it appears to.

## Frameworks Introduced

**Laszlo Polgar's five-step method** from his book *Raise a Genius!* (originally *Nevelj zsenit!*; Young credits the blogger Scott Alexander and his readers for sourcing an English translation).

1. **Start early.** "The child's education should begin no later than three, and specialization should begin no later than six." Young's hedge: "Although the exact extent to which learning becomes harder as we age is unclear, there is evidence from fields such as music and languages that children's brains are more plastic and flexible when younger. Laszlo took this idea to the extreme."
2. **Specialize.** The girls learned languages, mathematics, sports and other subjects, but the focus was always chess. Laszlo: "starting from 4-5 they played chess 5 or 6 hours a day." Two roles: it exploited any flexibility young children possess, and it let them reach proficiency at a much younger age. "Winning against older and more experienced opponents in chess built their confidence and competitive spirit, so that they actively wanted to practice more.... Had the girls spread their intellectual appetites too broadly, they might not have developed the self-confidence that leads to intense practice."
3. **Make practice into play.** Chess is naturally suited, but Laszlo insisted on introducing *all* subjects as forms of play. "When the girls would become distracted or get up and wander around while playing a game, they weren't punished but encouraged to let their minds wander as they sought a solution." Crucially, he did not mean play *instead of* work: "play is not the opposite of work" and "a child does not need play separate from work, but meaningful action," adding "learning presents them with more enjoyment than a sterile game." Play and work combined "with no rigid boundary between them."
4. **Create positive reinforcement.** "Failure, suffering, and fearfulness decrease achievement. Following a number of successive failures, even a damaging inhibitory complex can be created." Both directions harm enthusiasm — losing, confusion, frustration against a stronger opponent, *and* facing an opponent so easy the win carries no satisfaction. So while he was still stronger than his daughters, Laszlo adjusted his own play "so that they would be challenged but still win enough of the time to find the game enjoyable." In his words: "We should make sure not to always win against the child; we should let them win sometimes so that they feel they are also capable of thinking," and "At the start it is most important to awake interest.... We should get the child to love what they do—to such a degree that they do it almost obsessively."
5. **Never coerce.** "Self-discipline, motivation, and commitment, he felt, must come from the girls themselves." Laszlo: "One thing is certain: one can never achieve serious pedagogical results, especially at a high level, through coercion." And: "one of the most important educational tasks is to teach self-education." This mattered most because the girls quickly outpaced their father — "Had they not been encouraged to develop their own abilities to teach themselves and adjust their learning, they might have become decent chess players but certainly not grand masters."

Beyond the five steps, Laszlo and Klara built the infrastructure: "cultivating a database of more than **two hundred thousand matches**, buying every chess textbook they could find, and recruiting chess tutors for their daughters.... The Polgar house, with diagrams of chess positions hanging on the walls, became a temple devoted to the practice of the ancient game." Both parents worked at this full-time, assembling resources and schooling the girls at home.

**Three suggestions for fostering ultralearning in others.** Young frames these carefully: "These suggestions aren't rules, but they can be seen as starting points."

1. **Create an inspiring goal** — "Better yet, allow people to design their own learning goals that inspire them. Inspiration is an essential starting point.... There must be something very compelling for a person to summon up the energy and self-discipline needed to learn." Sometimes the compelling thing is career: coding boot camps push students at a brutal pace, "sometimes approaching eighty hours a week," and the goal justifies it. Sometimes it's intrinsic interest *amplified*: Young's own interest in computer science wouldn't have produced a structured project until the idea of a compressed degree made it "a passionate commitment"; Craig was always interested in trivia competitions, and it was the chance of appearing on the show that "made his interest become an obsession"; Barone expanded a childhood love of a game into an effort to build a better version. **"Seeking out people's natural interests for ultralearning means encouraging the sparks that already exist, rather than merely imposing on them the topics you feel would be most beneficial."** Note the sequencing that worked for de Montebello: "started with the idea of ultralearning and only later chose to craft a public speaking project around it."
2. **Be careful with competition.** "You don't need to feel as though you're good at something to invest energy into learning. After all, becoming good at something is what learning is. However, you need to feel that you *could* be good at it." The failure mode: "People tend to make their perceptions of inadequacy into immutable destinies: 'I'm no good at math,' 'I can't draw anything but stick figures,' 'I don't have the language gene.'" These aren't completely false — real differences in innate ability exist — but they ignore motivation, and "Feel as though you're lousy at doing something, and you're robbed of the motivation to change."
   - **The reference group is the lever.** Young observes that "many, but not all, ultralearners aimed at projects that were so unusual that they made it hard to compare them to a normal reference group." De Montebello competed against excellent speakers, which could have created inferiority — "except that de Montebello could always explain to himself any perceived deficits as coming from trying such an ambitious project with so little prior experience. Had it been the case that instead of an individual ultralearning project, he had been pitted against a dozen other competitors with exactly the same prior experience, he might instead have rationalized any perceived inadequacy as his simply not being good enough."
   - **The twofold rule.** "If a person in whom you want to encourage an ultralearning spirit has a natural aptitude, competition is probably good.... For a person who either is of moderate ability or is behind other people, such as learning a skill in a domain in which he or she has no experience, or who is starting to learn a new skill later in life, you should make an effort to make the project **unique**. This will encourage the person to frame his or her progress by comparing to his or her past self, not due to competition with others." And a staging pattern: "Sometimes a project can start out being unique, thus sheltered from the harsh light of unfavorable comparison, and move to a more competitive environment once confidence has been established. For example, you might start learning programming by creating a game that is hard to compare to others but enter coding competitions as you begin to feel more competent."
   - The Polgars sit on the favorable side of this: "Because the girls' training started unusually young, they were always seen as precocious and the competitive environment enhanced their motivation. Had they started late or been put into a school where they weren't guaranteed to be star performers, their motivation might have been sapped."
3. **Make learning a priority.** "Outside school, learning is usually seen as a by-product of doing a job, not the core goal. Though organizations often give lip service to ongoing training and education, it's usually in the form of workshops or seminars that one sits through passively before getting back to the real job at hand. Ultralearning, by encouraging direct, intensive practice, provides the opportunity for a kind of **fusion project**—one that accomplishes real objectives but is also designed to teach something new."
   - **The inversion.** "The normal protocol for assigning a project is to find the best person for the job and give them the task. A learning-driven approach would suggest instead that people who are *not yet capable* of doing the task might be assigned to the project. An ultralearning-driven work environment might consist of employees' spending a majority of their time on projects that are within or near their competency levels but devoting a specific fraction of their time to working on projects that are a leap above their current abilities."
   - Young labels this explicitly hypothetical and names two imagined benefits: "First, it will create a culture of learning within an organization where people are always willing to try to solve problems they don't yet know how to solve, instead of expecting someone else to know the answer already. Second, it will help reveal talent by giving people challenges they can rise to. If mentorship opportunities and difficult projects are assigned only on a whim by managers, they will likely miss a lot of people who may have the ability to succeed in difficult positions but are never given the opportunity."
   - The ceiling: "At the highest levels, an ultralearning-driven culture also allows learning to go into areas where perhaps nobody else has a particular skill. Although going between established levels of skill is important, it is when one learns to do something that nobody else can do that learning becomes truly valuable."

## Key Concepts

- **Judit Polgar.** Won her first game against a chess master at seven — while blindfolded. Ranked **fifty-fifth of all chess players in the world by FIDE at twelve**. Became the **youngest-ever grand master at fifteen**, beating Bobby Fischer's record by one month. Peaked at **eighth in the world** and competed in the World Chess Championship, the only woman ever to have done so.
- **The prejudice she competed against.** Grand master Edmar Mednis on facing her: "Grandmasters don't like to lose to 10-year-old girls, because then we make the front page of all the papers." Some competitors were admiring — Nigel Short said she might be one of the "three or four great chess prodigies in history"; former world champion Mikhail Tal suggested at twelve that she might eventually contend for world champion. **Garry Kasparov** was not: "She has fantastic chess talent, but she is, after all, a woman. It all comes down to the imperfections of the feminine psyche. No woman can sustain a prolonged battle."
- **The Linares incident.** Polgar, seventeen, playing Kasparov in a tournament in Linares, Spain. On **move thirty-four** he placed his knight, briefly lifted his fingers from the piece, changed his mind, and moved it to a better square. By the rules, once a player stops touching a piece the move is done. She glanced at the referee, who did not challenge him. She lost the game. Why she didn't protest: "I was playing the World Champion and didn't want to cause unpleasantness during my first invitation to such an important event. I was also afraid that if my complaint was overruled I would be penalized on the clock when we were in time pressure." Afterward she confronted him in the hotel bar — "How could you do this to me?" Kasparov's defense: "She publicly accused me of cheating.... I think a girl of her age should be taught some good manners." It would be years before they spoke again.
- **The 2002 rematch.** Eight years later, at the **Russia Versus the Rest of the World** match in Moscow, rapid chess with **twenty-five minutes** per player. Judit as white played the **Ruy Lopez** (the Spanish game, named for the sixteenth-century Spanish bishop and chess strategist), a common opening moving the knight and bishop out on the second and third moves. Kasparov countered with the **Berlin Defense**, developing a second knight while ignoring the potential danger from her bishop — "known to be a solid counter, often leading to draws. Kasparov wasn't taking any chances." After a flurry of exchanges, positions were close: Judit had castled her king into safety; Kasparov, losing that opportunity, had retained both light-square and dark-square bishops, "a powerful combination that can often be decisive." She "calmly pushed forward, cornering one of Kasparov's bishops and neutralizing his advantage," accumulated small positional advantages through the midgame, and **two pawns down and facing looming threats of checkmate, Kasparov resigned.**
- **Kasparov's revision.** "The Polgars showed that there are no inherent limitations to their aptitude—an idea that many male players refused to accept until they had unceremoniously been crushed by a twelve-year-old with a ponytail."
- **The origin of the experiment.** Laszlo Polgar decided to raise a genius *before having children or even a wife*. "A genius is not born but educated and trained," he maintained. Studying the biographies of hundreds of great intellectuals, he was convinced genius could be manufactured: "[W]hen I looked at the stories of geniuses, I found the same thing.... They all started at a young age and studied intensively." He first corresponded with **Klara**, a Ukrainian foreign-language teacher, by explaining the idea; she agreed, they met and married in the Soviet Union, and moved to his native Hungary. Three daughters: **Zsuzsa, Zsófia, Judit** — all three became world-class players, with Zsuzsa also becoming a grand master and Zsófia reaching international master.
- **Why chess.** They considered many subjects, from foreign languages to mathematics, and settled on chess "because it was objective and progress in it was easy to measure. No doubt the preeminence of chess intellectually in the socialist countries in those years added weight." All three girls also learned foreign languages (Zsuzsa learned eight), mathematics, table tennis, swimming and other subjects — the single-subject focus across all three daughters was a *practical* decision: "given the intense devotion both parents would need to have, in both resources and time, spreading their effort over three different disciplines would have been more than their budget or schedule could bear."
- **Momentum between siblings.** Zsuzsa started at four; six months later she was going with her father to Budapest's smoke-filled chess clubs, playing elderly men — and winning. By the time it was Judit's turn she was already motivated: "Zsuzsa and Zsófia played chess in a small room Laszlo had devoted to the game, and she didn't want to be left out." The three travelled as a team against much older, usually male players, and the shared mission "created camaraderie rather than jealousy."
- **Fighting the women-only rule.** The Hungarian Chess Federation's policy was that women compete in women-only events. Laszlo was strongly against it: "Women are able to achieve results similar, in fields of intellectual activities, to that of men. Chess is a form of intellectual activity, so this applies to chess. Accordingly, we reject any kind of discrimination in this respect." Discrimination had already prevented Zsuzsa from obtaining the grand master title at fifteen; as the youngest, Judit found the barriers already partly broken down and could skip the women-only tournaments.
- **The three girls were not identical.** Zsófia was the weakest of the three; despite reaching international master she later retired from chess for art and family. Zsuzsa specialized a little less from an early age, learning eight languages, "which her father admits might have distracted her from reaching her maximum chess potential." Judit started more slowly, according to Zsuzsa, "but she had the strongest work ethic, being 'obsessed' with chess to a degree unusual even for her family."

**Why this is a weak experiment — Young's own list.** "As far as experiments go, Laszlo's has plenty of holes that keep it from becoming a model of scientific purity."

- **No control group.** All three daughters got the same education; there was no fourth sister who went to school normally.
- **No randomization.** "Laszlo didn't adopt a random child to raise in his unusual system but taught his own. This also means that the influence of genetics can't be ignored. The success of all three children may be owing to hereditary, rather than acquired, talents."
- **No blinding.** "All the Polgars knew they were part of something special, a unique mission that set them apart from other families."
- Conclusion: "the success of the Polgar sisters might be suggestive of the role that unorthodox education can play, but it is far from definitive."
- **But not harmful, either.** "Although Laszlo's methods were strange, it does not seem as though the girls suffered, either in their broader education or in their emotional well-being. They were self-confident and happy and grew up to be successful, emotionally stable adults with loving families of their own." Asked whether the methods robbed his girls of a normal childhood, Laszlo argued the opposite — "that it is a normal, mediocre education that often leads to unhappiness." Interviewer Endre Farkas, who worked with Polgar on *Raise a Genius!*: "Have they been educated too narrowly, deprived of a carefree childhood? I was able to observe the Polgars.... One can clearly see that they are happy."
- **The loophole this represents.** Young's prior belief was that ultralearning couldn't be taught: intensity plus external imposition equals misery. Psychologists recognize a large difference between goals pursued **intrinsically** and those pursued **extrinsically**, "pushed by overbearing parents, punishing curricula, or demanding employers. The latter type, because the motivation for conforming to them comes primarily from outside social pressures, are the cause of much misery. Tales of depression, anxiety, and even suicide are distressingly common in environments where the pressure to perform on standardized tests is ratcheted up." The Polgars are unusual because the girls were trained from a very early age and worked incredibly hard yet "don't seem to have been harmed psychologically.... In contrast to the stereotypical 'tiger' parent, their parents encouraged their unusual specialization through play and positive feedback, not authority and punishment." The obsession "was clearly something fostered, rather than imposed." But Young keeps the honest caveat: "At the same time, their participation in the experiment wasn't exactly voluntary. Laszlo had dreamed up his goal of raising geniuses before knowing whether his children would consent."

## Mental Models

- **Fostered, not imposed.** The distinction that makes intensity survivable is whether the drive was cultivated in the learner or applied from outside.
- **Confidence is upstream of practice intensity.** Early wins create the self-confidence that produces the obsessive practice, which produces further wins — hence positive reinforcement and carefully chosen opponents at the start.
- **Reference group management.** Where you can't guarantee favourable comparison, make the project unique so the comparison is to the learner's past self.
- **Teach self-education, because you will be outgrown.** The step that mattered most for the Polgar sisters was the one that let them keep improving after their teacher couldn't help.

## Anti-patterns

- The "tiger parent" model: authority and punishment instead of play and positive reinforcement.
- Extrinsically imposed intensity — punishing curricula, standardized-test pressure — which the chapter links to depression, anxiety, and suicide.
- Always beating the learner. Laszlo deliberately did not.
- Also: always letting them win. Too-easy opponents rob the win of satisfaction and reduce enthusiasm just as failure does.
- Putting a late starter or a novice into direct competition with a matched reference group, which converts perceived deficits into "I'm simply not good enough."
- Imposing the topics you think would be most beneficial instead of amplifying existing sparks.
- Passive workshops and seminars as the organizational learning strategy.
- Assigning every project to the person already best able to do it.
- Reading the Polgar case as proof. It has no control, no randomization, and no blinding.

## Worked Example

**The nine-principle audit of the Polgar method** — Young's own, and the book's demonstration that the framework describes real pedagogy rather than only self-directed projects.

1. **Metalearning.** "Polgar devoted himself full-time to understanding how people learn chess and under what conditions his daughters would thrive. He created a huge library of chess positions, strategies, and lists of games, which, in the days before widespread internet, was no small feat. He also articulated a plan for coaching the girls ... starting by teaching them first to name the squares on the board and later to know how the pieces moved. That slow progression allowed the girls to pick up the game even before their other cognitive abilities had developed."
2. **Focus.** Laszlo considered "the ability to handle monotony, the capability to sustain interest and persistent attention" key traits to instill. "The girls twice took part in **twenty-four-hour chess marathons** when they were fifteen, nine, and eight years old, respectively, needing to complete **one hundred matches** in the time period. Chess is a game not merely of flickers of brilliance but of endurance and stamina."
3. **Directness.** He took them to games against men from as young as four. "The girls played many, many games of chess, which formed the backbone of their abilities. That allowed them to learn not only how to play the game well but also to deal with variables such as time pressure and the psychological insecurities of playing against older, more intimidating opponents. **By using chess timers even for casual games, the girls practiced in an environment that more closely matched the one they would face in tournaments.**"
4. **Drill.** Varied approaches: memorizing the names of the squares first, then the movements of key pieces. "Chess puzzles, hanging from the walls of the house, became the girls' homework, as they had to solve the different tactical positions and come up with creative solutions. **Blitz and blindfolded games** allowed the girls to get better at thinking more quickly and mentally simulating the game."
5. **Retrieval.** Laszlo: "We should not tell them everything; we should try to get the child to say something!" Using what he called the "Socratic method" for chess — "posing questions his girls must answer instead of telling them to remember a presolved solution." Blindfolded games again: "By practicing without looking at the board, it forced them to cultivate the ability to follow positions in their head."
6. **Feedback.** He encouraged play with real opponents but was careful to select "suitable partners, who have a generally similar playing ability." Feedback was deliberately controlled in *both* directions — enough challenge (hence insisting on men's tournaments) but not too much while abilities were nascent. "Cultivating positive feedback was important early on, and Laszlo was ever ready to adjust the flow of the game."
7. **Retention.** "Laszlo focused on having the girls recall chess patterns from memory and increased the speed of games to make elements of their play more automatic and less susceptible to forgetting" — i.e. proceduralization plus spaced practice, "aided both by spaced practice and through specialized drills such as blitz and blindfolded games."
8. **Intuition.** "Mirroring the Feynman Technique, Laszlo encouraged his girls to **write articles about chess**, explaining, 'If one writes an article, one considers a matter more deeply than without a goal, thinking alone or speaking with someone about it.'" Play in the unstructured, goalless sense was part of the teaching strategy: "Coming up with interesting solutions and challenging the girls to think of tricks and new insights allowed them to explore outside what could be offered by memorizing past results."
9. **Experimentation.** "As the Polgar sisters eclipsed their father in chess ability, their impetus to continue mastering the game increasingly had to come from within themselves. Each of the girls had to cultivate her own unique style." Judit chose to focus on tricks and tactics: "opening preparation was not at all important at that time. This may be a reason why, even today, my strongest area remains the middlegame."

Laszlo's own framing of the whole enterprise, which Young quotes as the summary: "In my opinion, we should disseminate the idea of intensive learning in every field."

## Key Takeaways

- The Polgar case shows intensity from childhood need not produce misery — when the drive is fostered through play and positive reinforcement rather than imposed by authority and punishment.
- It is nonetheless a weak experiment: no control group, no randomization, no blinding, and genetics cannot be excluded.
- Laszlo's five steps: start early, specialize, make practice play, create positive reinforcement, never coerce — and above all teach self-education, because the teacher gets outgrown.
- All nine ultralearning principles are visible in the method, including a striking parallel to the Feynman Technique (writing chess articles) and a directness detail worth stealing (chess timers even in casual games).
- To foster ultralearning in others: amplify existing sparks rather than imposing topics; use competition only where aptitude makes comparison favourable, otherwise make the project unique; and structure work so a defined fraction of time sits above current ability.
- The highest value of a learning-driven culture is reached "when one learns to do something that nobody else can do."
