# Chapter XI — Principle 8: Intuition — Dig Deep Before Building Up

> "Do not ask whether a statement is true until you know what it means." — Errett Bishop, mathematician

## Core Idea

Richard Feynman was called a magician — a genius whose process remains dark even after you understand what he did. Young's move is to take each of the famous feats apart and show the machinery: a large library of memorized patterns, an insistence on re-deriving results rather than following them, and a habit of constructing a concrete example for every abstraction. Intuition isn't a gift you have or lack; it is "the product of a large volume of organized experience dealing with the problem." The chapter's deliverable is the **Feynman Technique**, an explicit procedure for breaking the *illusion of explanatory depth* — the near-universal condition of thinking you understand something you don't.

## Frameworks Introduced

**Four rules for building intuition.**

**Rule 1: Don't give up on hard problems easily.** Feynman worked stubbornly on a problem until it yielded, from childhood radios onward: "If [he] had said, 'Never mind, it's too much work,' I'd have blown my top, because I want to beat this damn thing, as long as I've gone this far." In mathematics and physics he'd eschew easier methods, such as the Lagrangian technique, "forcing himself to painstakingly calculate all the forces by hand, simply because with the latter method he came to understand it better." Young's implementable version: **the struggle timer.** "When you feel like giving up and that you can't possibly figure out the solution to a difficult problem, try setting a timer for another ten minutes to push yourself a bit further." Two advantages: very often the problem yields to enough thinking; and even in failure "you'll be much more likely to remember the way to arrive at the solution when you encounter it" — the retrieval-difficulty effect from Chapter VIII, which primes memory "even when the difficulty is caused by the information not being there."

**Rule 2: Prove things to understand them.** Feynman on Lee and Yang's papers: "I can't understand these things that Lee and Yang are saying. It's all so complicated." His sister teased him that the problem wasn't that he couldn't understand it but that he hadn't invented it. He then read the papers meticulously and found they weren't so difficult — he had simply been afraid to go through them. The general point: "Feynman didn't master things by following along with other people's results. Instead, it was by the process of mentally trying to re-create those results that he became so good at physics." A disadvantage — it made him repeat work and reinvent existing processes — but the source of his intuitive depth. Einstein did the same: as a child, one of his earliest mathematical forays was trying to prove the Pythagorean Theorem on the basis of similar triangles. "Both men had a tendency to dig much deeper before they considered something to be 'understood.'"

**Rule 3: Always start with a concrete example.** "Human beings don't learn things very well in the abstract. As the research on transfer demonstrates, most people learn abstract, general rules only after being exposed to many concrete examples." Feynman supplied examples even when none was given, working through an explicit example in his mind's eye to see what the math was demonstrating.

- *The levels-of-processing effect.* It isn't how much time you spend attending to information that determines retention, but *how you think about it while attending*. In one study, participants reviewed a list of words; half were told there would be a test (motivated) and half were not. Within each group, half used a shallow orienting technique — notice whether the word contains the letter *e* — and half a deep one — judge whether the word is pleasant. **Motivation made no difference.** The orienting technique made a large one: deep processors "remembered almost twice as much as those who simply scanned their spelling."
- *Examples as feedback.* "When it's not possible to imagine an appropriate example, that's evidence that you don't understand something well enough and would benefit from going back a few steps."

**Rule 4: Don't fool yourself.** Feynman's aphorism — "Don't fool yourself," to which he added "and you're the easiest person to fool." He was deeply skeptical of his own understanding and "presaged the current replication crisis in psychology, attacking what he perceived as many social scientists fooling themselves." Young suspects this came from having cultivated rigorous standards for what counted as knowing.

- *The Dunning-Kruger effect.* Someone with inadequate understanding believes they know more than the people who actually do, because lacking knowledge also means lacking the ability to assess your own abilities. "It is true that the more you learn about a subject, the more questions arise. The reverse also seems to be true, in that the fewer questions you ask, the more likely you are to know less about the subject."
- *The fix: ask lots of "dumb" questions.* Feynman: "Some people think in the beginning that I'm kind of slow and I don't understand the problem, because I ask a lot of these 'dumb' questions: 'Is a cathode plus or minus? Is an an-ion this way, or that way?'" Young: "How many of us lack the confidence to ask 'dumb' questions? Feynman knew he was smart and had no problem asking them. The irony is that by asking questions with seemingly obvious answers, he also noticed the not-so-obvious implications." The counter-case from Brazil: Feynman's students complained when he asked simple questions they supposedly already knew the answers to. He eventually realized "they didn't know the answers but didn't want to admit it in front of everyone else in the class, wrongly assuming that they were the only ones who didn't know it."

**The Feynman Technique.** Young invented and named it while trying to turn these observations into something he could apply, and used it extensively during the MIT Challenge. Purpose: "to help develop intuition about the ideas you are learning. It can be used when you don't understand an idea at all or simply when you understand something a little but really want to turn it into a deep intuition."

1. Write down the concept or problem you want to understand at the top of a piece of paper.
2. In the space below, explain the idea as if you had to teach it to someone else.
   a. If it's a **concept**, ask yourself how you would convey the idea to someone who has never heard of it before.
   b. If it's a **problem**, explain how to solve it and — crucially — **why that solution procedure makes sense to you**.
3. When you get stuck — meaning your understanding fails to provide a clear answer — go back to your book, notes, teacher, or reference material to find the answer.

"The crux of this method is that it tries to dispel the illusion of explanatory depth. Since many of our understandings are never articulated, it's easy to think you understand something you don't.... Just as drawing a bicycle quickly confirms whether you have a basic grasp of how it is put together, using this technique will quickly reveal how much you really understand."

Young footnotes an honest caveat: "Calling this the Feynman Technique was possibly unwise. It's unclear if Feynman ever used this exact method, so I may have inadvertently given the technique an illustrious history it doesn't possess." (He also notes the wordplay hazard: Feynman's great contribution included "Feynman Diagrams," and the technique can produce diagrams, though not necessarily *those*.)

**Three applications of the technique.**

- **Application 1 — for things you don't understand at all.** Work with the book in hand, going back and forth between your explanation and the book's. "This lacks the benefits of retrieval practice, but it can often be essential when the explanation you've been given baffles you." Feynman's version, on encountering what he saw as philosophical gobbledygook: "I had this uneasy feeling of 'I'm not adequate,' until finally I said to myself, 'I'm going to stop, and read one sentence slowly, so I can figure out what the hell it means.' So I stopped—at random—and read the next sentence very carefully.... it was very close to this: 'The individual member of the social community often receives his information via visual, symbolic channels.' I went back and forth over it, and translated. You know what it means? 'People read.'"
- **Application 2 — for problems you can't seem to solve.** "It's very important to go through the problem step by step alongside the explanation you generate, rather than simply summarizing it. Summarizing may end up skipping over the core difficulties." Going deeper takes time "but it can help you get a strong grasp over a new method in one go, rather than needing numerous repetitions to memorize the steps."
- **Application 3 — for expanding your intuition.** For ideas important enough that a great intuition would really help. Instead of explaining every detail or following the source, "focus on generating illustrative examples, analogies, or visualizations that would make the idea comprehensible to someone who has learned far less than you have. Imagine that instead of trying to teach the idea, you are being paid to write a magazine article explaining the idea. What visual intuitions would you use to pin down the abstractions? Which examples would flesh out a general principle? How could you make something confusing feel obvious?"

## Key Concepts

- **Mark Kac's two kinds of genius.** *Ordinary geniuses*: "Once we understand what they have done we feel certain that we, too, could have done it." *Magicians*: "Even after we understand what they have done, the process by which they have done it is completely dark." Feynman, by Kac's reckoning, was "a magician of the highest caliber."
- **The feats.** In high school math tournaments he "would often get the correct answer while the problem was still being stated" — competitors just beginning to compute while Feynman had the answer circled. He won the **Putnam Mathematics Competition** (whose scholarship prize was to Harvard), a competition so difficult "some examination sessions have a median score of zero." He walked out early, scored first place, and his fraternity brothers were later amazed at the gap between his score and the next four. During the Manhattan Project, Niels Bohr asked to speak with Feynman directly before the other physicists — "He's the only guy who's not afraid of me.... [He] will say when I've got a crazy idea."
- **Feynman was mortal.** He excelled in math and physics but "was abysmal in the humanities." College grades: history in the bottom fifth of his class, literature in the bottom sixth, fine arts worse than 93 percent of fellow students. At one point he resorted to cheating on a test to pass. His measured intelligence in school scored **125**; the average college graduate scores 115, "which puts Feynman only modestly higher." Young allows the score may have failed to capture him or been poorly administered, but keeps it in as a reminder.
- **The mental arithmetic, demystified.** Against an abacus salesman in Brazil, asked for the cube root of **1,729.03**, Feynman produced **12.002** to more decimal places than the salesman could reach 12. His explanation: "I happened to know that a cubic foot contains 1728 cubic inches, so the answer is a tiny bit more than 12. The excess, 1.03, is only one part in nearly 2000, and I had learned in calculus that for small fractions, the cube root's excess is one-third of the number's excess. So all I had to do was find the fraction 1/1728, and multiply by 4." For *e* to a power: "because of radioactivity (mean-life and half-life), I knew the log of 2 to the base e, which is .69315 (so I also knew that e to the power of .7 is nearly equal to 2)" — then multiply that number against itself for higher powers. His own verdict: "sheer luck." Young's: "The secret was his impressive memory for certain arithmetic results and an intuition with numbers that enabled him to interpolate."
- **The safecracking, demystified.** He obsessed over how combination locks worked and realized that by fiddling with a lock while it was *open* he could determine the last two numbers, note them after leaving the office, sneak back, and crack the remaining number with patience. One military official, shown the technique, didn't fix the security flaw but warned everyone to keep Feynman away from their safes. A professional locksmith later greeted him: "God! You're Feynman—the great safecracker!"
- **The physics intuition, demystified.** In his own words: "I had a scheme, which I still use today when somebody is explaining something that I'm trying to understand: I keep making up examples." Rather than following an equation, he'd imagine the situation it described, work it through on his example, and see mistakes as they arose. "As they're telling me the conditions of the theorem, I construct something which fits all the conditions. You know, you have a set (one ball)—disjoint (two balls). Then the balls turn colors, grow hairs, or whatever, in my head as they put more conditions on. Finally they state the theorem, which is some dumb thing about the ball which isn't true for my hairy green ball thing, so I say, 'False!'"
- **Principles versus surface features.** In a famous study, advanced PhDs and undergraduate physics students sorted physics problems into categories. Beginners sorted by surface features — pulleys, inclined planes. Experts sorted by deep principle — "Ah, so it's a conservation of energy problem." The expert approach is more successful "because it gets to the core of how the problems work. The surface features of a problem don't always relate to the correct procedure needed to solve it." Why don't students start there? "The simple answer may be that they *can't*. Only by developing enough experience with problem solving can you build up a deep mental model of how other problems work."
- **The chess chunking study.** Masters and beginners were shown a chess position and asked to reproduce it on an empty board. Masters recalled far more; beginners placed pieces one by one and often failed. Masters remembered in larger **chunks**, several pieces corresponding to a recognizable pattern placed at once. "Psychologists theorize that the difference between grand masters and novices is not that grand masters can compute many more moves ahead but that they have built up huge libraries of mental representations that come from playing actual games. Researchers have estimated that having around **50,000** of these mental 'chunks' stored in long-term memory is necessary to reach expert status." **And the limit:** give both groups a *randomized* board that couldn't arise from normal play, and the expert advantage disappears — they revert to remembering piece by piece. (Footnote: not all researchers accept the chunking model; Ericsson prefers "Long-Term Working Memory," but both point to expertise gained through extensive context-specific practice.)
- **Feynman's intuition had the same boundary.** His mathematician friends tested him on counterintuitive theorems where "properties of the procedure (such as that an object can be cut into infinitely small pieces) defied the normal physical limitations that aided his intuition elsewhere" — and his intuition failed.
- **Brittle learning.** Feynman once tricked classmates into believing a French curve (a device for drawing curved lines) was special because, no matter how you hold it, the bottom is tangent to a horizontal line — which is true of *any* smooth shape and an elementary fact of calculus his classmates should have recognized. He saw this as "a particularly 'brittle' way of learning things, since students didn't really think about relating what they had learned to problems outside the textbook."
- **The illusion of explanatory depth (Rebecca Lawson).** "We judge our own learning competency, not directly but through various signals. Assessing whether or not we know a factual matter, such as what is the capital of France, is quite easy—either the word 'Paris' comes up in your mind, or it doesn't. Asking whether you understand a concept is a lot harder because you may understand it a little, but not enough for the purposes at hand." Lawson's study asked participants to draw a bicycle — seat, handles, tires, pedals, and chain in the right places. "Most participants had no idea how the machines were assembled, even though they used them all the time and believed they understood them quite well." Young invites the reader to try it, and then to try a harder one: "Try the exercise again, except this time with a can opener. Can you explain how it works? How many gears are there? How does it cut the lid open? This one is much harder, yet most of us would say we understand can openers!"

## Mental Models

- **Magic is well-practiced technique.** Every Feynman feat in the chapter dissolves into memorized patterns plus a method. "Magic, perhaps, Feynman did not possess, but an incredible intuition for numbers and physics he certainly did."
- **But demystification is not deflation.** "Even knowing the logic behind Feynman's sleight of hand, I'm certain I wouldn't have been able to calculate the numbers he did so effortlessly.... This explanation doesn't provide the satisfying 'Aha!' that it would have had the magician's trick been revealed as something trivial."
- **Effort hides behind play.** "In his playful style and rebellious impulses, he may seem to defy the stereotype that learning requires hard work." But he and a friend raced ahead of their classmates through the early quantum mechanics books; he made "a meticulous timetable to allocate hours to his many intellectual pursuits"; and even his lock picking was systematic: "I got it down to an absolute rhythm so I could try the 400 possible back numbers in less than half an hour. That meant I could open a safe in a maximum of eight hours—with an average time of four hours." Young's conclusion: "perhaps his greatest [gift] was his ability to merge tenacious practice and play."

## Anti-patterns

- Nodding along while reading and counting it as understanding.
- Summarizing a problem instead of walking it step by step (which skips the actual difficulty).
- Memorizing textbook solutions without relating them to problems outside the textbook — "brittle" learning.
- Avoiding "dumb" questions to appear knowledgeable, as Feynman's Brazilian students did.
- Sorting problems by surface features and being surprised when the method doesn't fit.
- Assuming you understand a familiar object or idea because you use it constantly (bicycles, can openers).

## Worked Example

**Application 3, worked in full: what is voltage?**

The chapter's fullest demonstration of the technique. Young's starting state, in an early electromagnetism class during the MIT Challenge: "Though I was comfortable using the concept in problems, I didn't feel that I had a good intuition of what it was." Note the diagnosis — he could compute with it, which is exactly the condition the illusion of explanatory depth thrives in.

*The obstacle.* "It's obviously not energy, electrons, or flows of things. Still, it was hard to get a mental image of an abstract concept on a wire."

*The move.* Rather than re-reading the definition, he compared the **equations for voltage against the equations for gravity** — looking for a structural analogy to an intuition he already had. The result: **"voltage is to the electrical force as height is to the gravitational force."**

*The picture that fell out of the analogy:*

| Electrical concept | Water/gravity image |
|---|---|
| Wires at different voltages | Troughs of water at different heights |
| Batteries | Pumps, moving the water up |
| Resistors | Hoses dropping down, of various widths, impeding the flow of water draining down |

*The honest evaluation.* "Although this picture of troughs and hoses wasn't necessary for solving the equations, it stuck with me and helped me reason my way out of new situations more easily than if voltage had just been an abstract quantity."

That last sentence is the point of the whole principle. The intuition bought nothing on the problems he could already do. It bought transfer to situations he hadn't seen — which is precisely what the physics-sorting study says separates experts from novices, and precisely what Chapter VI says is hardest to get.

*The other two applications, briefly.* For **photogrammetry** — determining an object's 3D shape from a series of 2D pictures taken under different lighting conditions — he didn't understand it at all, so he wrote a few pages of notes with the textbook at his side, "trying to sketch out the broad strokes of the idea so I could get the general gist of it." For **grid acceleration** — speeding up ray-traced rendering by skipping objects that "obviously" won't appear on the part of the screen being drawn — he walked the problem step by step, "drawing a little snowman that I imagined rendering, with lines shooting out of an eyeball representing the camera." Note that in Application 2 the concrete example is *drawn*, not summarized.

## Key Takeaways

- Intuition is a large library of organized, context-specific experience — the chess-chunk finding generalizes.
- Experts sort problems by deep principle; novices by surface feature, and they can't do otherwise until the library exists.
- Four rules: don't quit early (use a struggle timer), prove things yourself, always construct a concrete example, and don't fool yourself.
- The levels-of-processing study: *how* you think about material beats *how motivated* you are to remember it.
- The illusion of explanatory depth is universal — most people cannot draw a working bicycle.
- The Feynman Technique — write it at the top, explain it as if teaching, go to the source where you get stuck — exists to expose that illusion, in three variants for three different deficits.
