# Chapter XII — Principle 9: Experimentation — Explore Outside Your Comfort Zone

> "Results? Why, I have gotten lots of results! I know several thousand things that won't work." — Thomas Edison

## Core Idea

The first eight principles are starting points. Beyond the basics, following someone else's path stops working: fewer people can teach you, your skill profile diverges from theirs, improvement turns from accumulation into *unlearning*, and originality itself becomes part of what's rewarded. Van Gogh — who started at twenty-six, couldn't draw, alienated nearly everyone who could have taught him, and became one of the most recognizable painters in history — is Young's model for the pattern that replaces imitation: **hypothesis, experiment, results, repeat**, executed with enough intensity that each experiment produces a real body of work.

## Frameworks Introduced

**Why experimentation becomes essential as you advance — three reasons.**

1. **The path thins out.** "The early part of learning a skill tends to be the best trodden and supported, as everyone begins at the same place." As you develop, there are fewer teachers, fewer peers, and a smaller market for books, classes, and instructors — *and* you diverge from the people you were learning from. "Whereas two complete novices have quite similar knowledge and skills, two experts might have quite different sets of skills that they've already acquired, thus making improving those skills an increasingly personalized and idiosyncratic adventure."
2. **Improvement turns into unlearning.** "Learning in the early phases of a skill is an act of accumulation.... Getting better, however, increasingly becomes an act of unlearning; not only must you learn to solve problems you couldn't before, you must unlearn stale and ineffective approaches." His example: "The difference between a novice programmer and a master isn't usually that the novice cannot solve certain problems. Rather, it's that the master knows the best way to solve a problem, which will be the most efficient and clean and cause the fewest headaches later on. As mastery becomes a process of unlearning over accumulation, experimentation becomes synonymous with learning."
3. **Many skills reward originality, not just proficiency.** "A great mathematician is one who can solve problems others cannot, not merely a person who can solve previously solved problems easily. Successful business leaders are those who can spot opportunities others cannot.... As creativity becomes valuable, experimentation becomes essential."

**Three levels of experimentation**, each broadening the option space exponentially:

1. **Experimenting with learning resources.** Methods, materials, and resources — what van Gogh did at the start of his career: home-study courses, watching fellow artists, sketching from life and in the studio. "This kind of experimentation is useful in helping you discover the guides and resources that work best for you." The critical caveat: "It's important, however, that your impulse to experiment be matched with a drive to do the necessary work. Although van Gogh tried many different approaches when he first started teaching himself to draw and paint, he also produced an enormous quantity of work based on each of those methods." **The strategy: "pick a resource (maybe a book, class, or method of learning) and apply it rigorously for a predetermined period of time. Once you apply yourself aggressively to that new method, you can step back and evaluate."**
2. **Experimenting with technique.** Later the question shifts from "How can I learn this?" to **"What should I learn next?"** Languages are the clearest case: beginner resources all cover the same vocabulary and phrases, but as you improve the possibilities multiply — literature, professional conversation, comic books, business discussions — each with its own specialized vocabulary, phrases, and cultural knowledge. "Pick some subtopic within the skill you're trying to cultivate, spend some time learning it aggressively, and then evaluate your progress.... There's no 'right' answer here, but there are answers that will be more useful to the specific skill you're trying to master."
3. **Experimenting with style.** "Although there are some skills that have one and only one 'correct' way of doing things, this is not true of most. Writing, design, leadership, music, art, and research all involve developing certain styles, which have different trade-offs." Van Gogh tried styles from traditional painters like Millet to Japanese woodblock prints, and studied the techniques of artist friends like Gauguin and Rappard. **"The key to experimenting with different styles is to be aware of all the different styles that exist."** Van Gogh spent enormous time studying and discussing other artists' work, giving him a large library of styles to adapt. "Similarly, you might want to identify masters in your own line of study and dissect what makes their styles successful."

The tension across all three levels — explore versus commit — "often resolves itself as you cycle through exploring a new avenue in learning and then buckling down to learn it deeply before moving on to something else."

**The mindset.** Young connects it to Carol Dweck's **growth mindset**. In a *fixed* mindset, learners believe their traits are innate and there's no point trying to improve them; in a *growth* mindset, capacity for learning is something that can be actively improved. "In some ways, these two types of mindsets become self-fulfilling prophecies." The parallel: "Experimenting is based on the belief that improvements are possible in how you approach your work. If you think your learning styles are fixed ... you won't be able to experiment at all." But he positions experimentation as a step beyond: **"whereas the growth mindset encourages you to see opportunities and potential for improvement, experimentation enacts a plan to reach those improvements. The experimental mindset doesn't just assume that growth is possible but creates an active strategy for exploring all the possible ways to reach it.... Exploration, not dogmatism, is the key."**

**Five tactics.**

1. **Copy, Then Create.** Van Gogh is best known for original work but "also spent a lot of time copying drawings and paintings he liked by other artists." Copying simplifies experimentation by giving you a starting point for decisions — the possibility space of what to paint and how is otherwise too vast to choose within. "However, if you start by emulating another artist, you can use that foothold to venture further in your own creative directions." A second benefit: "In attempting to emulate or copy an example you appreciate, you must deconstruct it to understand why it works. This can often highlight things that the other person does exceptionally well that weren't obvious from the beginning. It may also dispel illusions you may have had about an aspect of the work you thought was important but upon emulating the other person's work you realize was not."
2. **Compare Methods Side-By-Side.** Borrow the scientific method: control conditions so the difference between two situations is limited to the variable being studied. "By applying two different approaches side by side, you can often quickly get information not only about what works best but about which methods are better suited to your personal style." Two advantages: better information (as in real experiments), and breadth — "by solving a problem multiple ways or applying multiple solution styles to it, you will increase your breadth of expertise. Forcing yourself to try different approaches encourages experimentation outside your comfort zone."
3. **Introduce New Constraints.** "The challenge of learning in the beginning is that you don't know what to do. The challenge of learning in the end is that you think you already know what to do." That latter difficulty makes you rerun old routines out of habit rather than because they're best. "A powerful technique for pushing out of those grooves of routine is by introducing new constraints that make the old methods impossible to use. It's practically an axiom of design that the best innovations come from working within constraints. Give a designer unlimited freedom, and the solution is usually a mess."
4. **Find Your Superpower in the Hybrid of Unrelated Skills.** The traditional path is to take one well-defined skill and practice it relentlessly — the athlete's path, decades perfecting a shot, jump, kick, or throw. "However, for many areas of creative or professional skills, another, more accessible, path is to combine two skills that don't necessarily overlap to bring about a distinct advantage that those who specialize in only one of those skills do not have." His example: an engineer who becomes really good at public speaking. "You may not be the best possible engineer or the best possible presenter, but combining those two skills could make you the best person to present on engineering topics for your company at conferences." **Scott Adams**, creator of *Dilbert*, "likened his own success to following this strategy by combining his background as an engineer with an MBA and a cartoonist." Young's own case: after the MIT Challenge he applied his programming knowledge to write scripts that automatically generated flash cards for learning Chinese. "This level of experimentation often plays out over multiple ultralearning projects."
5. **Explore the Extremes.** Van Gogh's work "pushed well outside normal conventions along many dimensions": thick impasto application far from the thin glazes of Renaissance masters; far more rapid brushwork than other painters; bold, often garish colors instead of subtle. Charted against other painters he would lie at the extreme on many dimensions.
   - *The geometric argument.* "As you get to higher and higher dimensions, most of the volume of a higher-dimensional sphere lies near its surface. For instance, in two dimensions (a circle), just under **20 percent** of its mass lies in the outer shell described by a tenth of the radius. In three dimensions (a sphere), that number rises to almost **30 percent**. In ten dimensions, almost **three-quarters** of the mass is in that outermost layer." Imagine learning a complex subject as finding an optimal point in a high-dimensional space, where the dimensions are qualitative aspects of the work — van Gogh's complementarity of colors, application of paint, and so on. **"The more complicated a domain of skill is (i.e., the more dimensions it contains), the more space will be taken up by applications of that skill that are extreme across at least one of those dimensions. This suggests that for many skills, the best option is going to be extreme in some way, since so many more of the possibilities are themselves extreme. Sticking to the middle and playing it safe isn't the correct approach because that allows you to explore only a small subset of the total possibilities."**
   - *The move.* "Pushing out to an extreme in some aspect of the skill you're cultivating, even if you eventually decide to pull it back to something more moderate, is often a good exploration strategy."

## Key Concepts

- **Van Gogh's handicaps.** He started at **twenty-six**, late in a field of precocity: Picasso's cubism grew out of already painting realistically as a child ("four years to paint as Rafael, but a lifetime to paint like a child"); Leonardo was apprenticed in his teens; Dalí had his first exhibit before turning fourteen. Van Gogh took up the brush only after failing as an art dealer and as a minister. The art seller and family friend **H. G. Tersteeg** told him: "You started too late.... Of one thing I am sure, you are no artist.... This painting of yours will be like all the other things you started, it will come to nothing."
- **He genuinely couldn't draw.** "His drafting was crude and childlike." Getting anything resembling a likeness took many attempts — and convincing models to sit was itself hard, given his famously difficult personality. During a brief stint at a Parisian atelier he studied next to future Post-Impressionist leaders such as Henri de Toulouse-Lautrec; where Toulouse-Lautrec captured a scene "with a few flicks of his wrist," van Gogh struggled. A classmate: "We considered his work too unskillful.... His drawings had nothing remarkable about them." He left the studio after less than three months.
- **Temperament as an obstacle to teaching.** "Nearly everyone who entered his life would eventually reject him, as his manic enthusiasm and fraternal solidarity would inevitably sour into bitter fights." Near the end he was routinely placed in mental asylums, variously diagnosed from "acute mania with generalized delirium" to "a kind of epilepsy." His outbursts — "attacks," as he called them — "alienated him from people who could potentially serve as his peers, mentors, and teachers. As a result, despite having attempted formal schooling, van Gogh was largely self-taught."
- **His death.** At thirty-seven, of a bullet wound to the stomach. Suspected suicide, but biographers Steven Naifeh and Gregory White Smith consider accident or foul play more likely — "he may possibly even have been shot by one of the village youths who played pranks on him and called him the *fou roux*, or 'crazed redhead.'"
- **The outcome.** *The Starry Night*, *Irises*, and *Vase with Fifteen Sunflowers* became icons. "On four separate occasions, a van Gogh piece has become the most expensive painting ever sold, including his *Portrait of Dr. Gachet*, which was sold for more than **$82 million**."
- **The pattern that produced it.** "First, he would identify a learning resource, method, or style and pursue it with incredible vigor, creating dozens, if not hundreds, of works in that direction. After this burst of intensity, aware of his still-existing deficiencies, he would apply himself to a new resource, method, or style and start again. Although there's no evidence van Gogh thought of the connection, I see a parallel between this pattern and the one used by successful scientists: hypothesis, experiment, results, repeat."
- **The specific experiments.**
  - *Home-study courses.* Charles Bargue's **Exercices au fusain** (Charcoal Exercises) and **Cours de dessin** (Drawing Course), and Armand Cassagne's **Guide de l'alphabet du dessin** (Guide to the ABCs of Drawing) — thick books of graduated exercises. His biographers: he "devoured these big books ... page by page, over and over." Van Gogh to his brother Theo: "I have now finished all sixty sheets," adding "I worked almost a whole fortnight, from early morning until night."
  - *Copying.* Jean-François Millet's **The Sower** was a favorite, copied again and again. Copying continued late into his career.
  - *Advice from other artists.* **Anthon van Rappard** convinced him to try reed pen and ink and adopt short, fast strokes. **Anton Mauve** persuaded him to try charcoal and chalk, watercolor, and Conté crayon. **Paul Gauguin**, during their stay together at the house where van Gogh would later cut off his ear, pushed him to paint from memory, mute his colors, and adopt new materials.
  - *Failed experiments.* Gauguin's suggestions "didn't work for van Gogh, whose weaknesses in drafting were exacerbated by not having the scene directly in front of him, and the different materials went against the style that would later make him famous." Young's verdict: **"Experiments, however, needn't always be successful to have value."**
  - *Experimenting with philosophy, not just materials.* Though famous for vibrant color, that wasn't his initial intention. Originally he favored muted, greyer tones, as in *The Potato Eaters*: "Scarcely any color is not gray. In nature one really sees nothing else but those tones or shades." He was fully convinced and based his work on it — then switched to the exact opposite: bright, complementary colors, "often imposed on a scene rather than being brought out from nature." His stance on movements moved too: at first he preferred traditional painting to Impressionism, later shifting to the avant-garde, "opting for bold forms rather than verisimilitude."
- **The two things Young says made it work.** *Variation* — "Since he struggled with many aspects of painting, I believe that variation was important to his eventually finding a style that would work for him—one that would take advantage of his strengths and diminish the significance of his weaknesses. Although virtuoso talents might be able to latch on to the first style of instruction they are presented with and follow it to completion, others require a great deal of experimentation before the right method sticks." And *intensity* — "Despite receiving much negative feedback and discouragement, he pursued his art relentlessly, sometimes producing as much as a new painting every day."

## Mental Models

- **Learning is experimental twice over.** "First, the act of learning itself is a kind of trial and error. Practicing directly, getting feedback, and trying to summon up the right answers to problems are all ways of adjusting the knowledge and skills you have in your head to the real world. Second, the act of experimenting also lies in the process of trying out your learning methods."
- **The principles are guidelines, not iron rules.** "Only by experimenting will you be able to find the right trade-offs between different principles—for instance, when directness is more important and when you should focus on drills or whether retention or intuition is the main obstacle to learning."
- **There is no single right answer to the method questions.** "Are you better off learning a language through practicing speaking or engaging in lots of input through movies and books? Are you better off learning programming by building your own game or working on open-source projects? These questions don't have a single correct answer, and people have achieved success using a wide variety of different methods."
- **Experimentation prunes as well as generates.** "Not only does it make you try new things and think hard about how to solve specific learning challenges, it also encourages you to be ruthless in discarding methods that don't work. Careful experimentation not only brings out your best potential, it also eliminates bad habits and superstitions by putting them to the test of real-world results."

## Anti-patterns

- A flurry of random activity mistaken for experimentation. "In order to work, experimenting requires understanding what learning problems you're facing and coming up with possible ways to resolve them."
- Trying many methods without producing a real body of work under any of them.
- Sticking to the same narrow set of methods for everything: "As a result, there are a lot of things they struggle to learn because they don't know the best way to do so."
- Playing it safe in the middle of every dimension of a complex skill.
- Assuming your learning style is fixed, which makes experimentation impossible by definition.
- Continuing to follow exemplars past the point where their skill profile still resembles yours.

## Worked Example

**Young's mnemonics split test — a full instance of Tactic 2.**

*The question.* "I wasn't sure how effective mnemonics would be" for French vocabulary. Chapter X's verdict on mnemonics is genuinely mixed — powerful but slow to recall and expensive to build — so this is exactly the kind of question that can't be settled by reading.

*The design.* For **one month**, every day:

- Find **fifty new words**, "put together from my regular reading or random encounters with the language."
- For **half**, simply look them over with their dictionary translations.
- For the other **half**, "make an effort to use a visual mnemonic to link the two meanings."
- Later, test on words **picked randomly from each side** and compare how many were remembered.

Note the controls: same day, same source, same person, same total exposure, one variable. This is what distinguishes a side-by-side test from generalized tinkering.

*The result.* "I remembered the words I used mnemonics for at almost **twice the rate** of those I didn't. That showed that even if creating the mnemonics took a bit more time, they were worth it." Young flags that this is "something you would probably expect after reading the chapters on retrieval and retention" — which is the point: the theory predicted it, and the test confirmed it *for him*, in his language, at his level.

**The failures, which the chapter insists on reporting.** "Embedded in my successes are many failures — times where I thought I could get something to work and it ended up failing miserably." The named one: "Early on in learning Chinese, I thought I could use some kind of mnemonic system to remember the words, with colors for tones and memorized symbols for the syllables. That was because my normal sounds-like method for visual mnemonics wasn't working with the words, which all sounded so different from English. **The result was a total failure, and it didn't work at all!**" Note this is the *same* technique that succeeded in French, failing in Chinese — the strongest possible argument for testing rather than importing conclusions.

**And the portrait challenge as a compressed experimental sequence** — thirty days, three methods: "starting by doing sketches and, when my progress using that approach slowed, trying to do sketches even faster to get more feedback. When that, too, had reached its limits, I spent some time learning a different technique altogether to achieve greater accuracy." Each transition was triggered by metafeedback (Chapter IX): a stalling learning rate.

## Key Takeaways

- Experimentation becomes essential as you advance because the path thins, improvement turns into unlearning, and originality starts to count.
- Three levels, in order: resources, technique, style — each with exponentially more options than the last.
- Pair every experiment with enough aggressive work to produce a real body of output; otherwise you're sampling, not testing.
- The experimental mindset extends the growth mindset: it doesn't just believe improvement is possible, it plans how to find it.
- Five tactics: copy then create, side-by-side comparison, new constraints, hybrid skills, and pushing to extremes.
- The high-dimensional geometry argument: in complex skills most of the possibility space is extreme along some dimension, so the safe middle is a small search.
- Report and keep your failures. Young's Chinese mnemonic system failed completely; the same idea in French roughly doubled retention.
