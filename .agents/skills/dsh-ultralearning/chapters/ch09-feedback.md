# Chapter IX — Principle 6: Feedback — Don't Dodge the Punches

> "Everybody has a plan until they get punched in the mouth." — Mike Tyson

## Core Idea

Feedback is the most consistent tactic across ultralearners — and one of the few areas where the research says *more is not always better*. In over 38 percent of studies feedback made performance worse, almost always when it addressed the person rather than the task. So the skill is twofold: get far more feedback than is comfortable, far earlier than feels reasonable; and then filter it — knowing which of the three grades of feedback you're actually receiving, refusing to fake an upgrade, and deliberately ignoring the parts that carry no information. Chris Rock walking unannounced onto a small Greenwich Village stage to bomb on purpose is the chapter's image of what that looks like.

## Frameworks Introduced

**The three types of feedback**, in ascending value and descending availability:

| Type | Question it answers | Availability | Example from the chapter |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Outcome** | Are you doing it wrong? | Most common, often the only kind | Applause or crickets after de Montebello's speech; a grade; a product's sales |
| **Informational** | *What* are you doing wrong? | Fairly common | A native speaker's confused stare; compiler errors; a joke that doesn't land |
| **Corrective** | *How* do you fix what you're doing wrong? | Hardest to find | A coach; MIT assignment solution keys; flash card answers |

- **Outcome feedback** is "the least granular." It comes as a grade, or as an aggregate over many simultaneous decisions. Every entrepreneur gets it when a product hits the market: "It may sell wildly well or abysmally, but that feedback comes in bulk, not directly decomposable into the various aspects of the product. Did the product cost too much? Was the marketing message not clear enough? Was the packaging unappealing?" Research shows even this helps: in one study feedback on a visual acuity task facilitated learning "even when it was delivered in blocks that were too large to get any meaningful information about which responses were correct." Barone's development blog is the model — it couldn't tell him exactly what to change, "but his simply being immersed in an environment that provided feedback at all was helpful." Two mechanisms: a motivational benchmark against your goal, and a read on the relative merits of the methods you're trying — stick with what's moving, change what's stalled.
- **Informational feedback** tells you what's wrong but not how to fix it. Easy to obtain when you have real-time access to a feedback source. A programmer who doesn't understand her error messages can still use whether errors increase or diminish as a signal. Self-provided informational feedback is ubiquitous and in some pursuits almost as good as external: looking at your own painting tells you whether the brushstrokes are helping. "Because this kind of feedback often comes from direct interaction with the environment, it often pairs well with the third principle, directness."
- **Corrective feedback** shows both the error and the fix, and "is often available only through a coach, mentor, or teacher." But the right materials can automate it: during the MIT Challenge Young worked back and forth between assignments and their solutions, so finishing a problem revealed "not only whether I had gotten it right or wrong but exactly how my answer differed from the correct one." Flash cards and other active recall do the same. Maria Araceli Ruiz-Primo and Susan M. Brookhart: "The best feedback is informative and usable by the student(s) who receive it. Optimal feedback indicates the difference between the current state and the desired learning state AND helps students to take a step to improve their learning."

**Do not fake an upgrade.** Two failure modes Young flags explicitly:

- *Outcome → informational.* Works only if you can genuinely elicit feedback per element. "If instead the feedback is being provided as a holistic assessment of everything you're doing, trying to turn it into informational feedback can backfire. Game designers know to watch out for this, because asking play testers what they don't like about a game can often return spurious results: for example, they don't like the color of the character or the background music. The truth is, the players are evaluating the game holistically ... asking for greater specificity may lead to guesses from those giving feedback."
- *Informational → corrective.* Requires a genuinely correct answer or a recognized expert. Without one, "trying to turn informational feedback into corrective feedback can work against you when the wrong change is suggested as an improvement."
- De Montebello's workaround for the second case: "the advice most people gave him wasn't terribly useful, but the *consistency* of it was. If his speech elicited wildly different reactions each time, he knew there was still a lot of work to do. When the speech started to get much more consistent comments, he knew he was onto something."

**The four feedback tactics.**

1. **Noise Cancellation.** Every feedback stream contains signal (useful information) and noise (random factors you shouldn't overreact to). Young's example: articles posted online mostly get little attention, and when they do "it's often because of factors outside of your control; for example, just the right person happens to share it." Quality drives those factors, "but there's enough randomness that you need to be careful not to change your entire approach based on one data point." Borrow the audio-engineering technique of **filtering**: human speech occupies a particular frequency range while white noise is spread across the spectrum, so boost the speech frequencies and quiet the rest. In learning, look for **proxy signals** — measures that don't exactly equal success but eliminate noisy data. For blog writing: tracking what percentage of readers reach the end of an article. "This doesn't prove your writing is good, but it's a lot less noisy than raw traffic data."
2. **Hitting the Difficulty Sweet Spot.** "A scientific measure of information is based on how easily you can predict what message it will contain. If you know that success is guaranteed, the feedback itself provides no information.... Good feedback does the opposite. It is very hard to predict and thus gives more information each time you receive it." Most people intuitively avoid constant failure, "However, the opposite problem, of being too successful, is more pervasive." The rule: "Ultralearners carefully adjust their environment so that they're not able to predict whether they'll succeed or fail. If they fail too often, they simplify the problem so they can start noticing when they're doing things right. If they fail too little, they'll make the task harder or their standards stricter.... Basically, you should try to avoid situations that always make you feel good (or bad) about your performance."
3. **Metafeedback.** Not feedback on your performance but on **the strategy you're using to learn**. The key metric is your **learning rate** — how fast you're improving. Chess players track Elo growth; LSAT studiers track mock exam improvements; language learners track vocabulary learned or errors made when writing or speaking. Two uses: (a) decide when to keep going and when to experiment — "If your learning rate is slowing to a trickle, that might mean you're hitting diminishing returns with your current approach and could benefit from different kinds of drills, difficulties, or environments." (b) Compare two study methods head to head. During the MIT Challenge, Young "would often split up questions from different subtopics before testing myself on an exam and try different approaches side by side. Does it work better to dive straight into trying to answer questions or to spend a little time to try to see that you understand the main concepts first? The only way you can know is to test your own learning rates."
4. **High-Intensity, Rapid Feedback.** Sometimes the fix is simply far more of it, far more often — "particularly true when the default mode of learning involves little or infrequent feedback." De Montebello got far more stage exposure than most speakers; Lewis's immersion exposes his pronunciation "at a point when most students still haven't uttered a word." The advantage is informational but "more often the advantage is emotional, too. Fear of receiving feedback can often hold you back more than anything. By throwing yourself into a high-intensity, rapid feedback situation, you may initially feel uncomfortable, but you'll get over that initial aversion much faster than if you wait months or years." And "Knowing that your work will be evaluated is an incredible motivator to do your best. This motivational angle ... may end up outweighing the informational advantage it provides."

**Timing.** The literature is superficially mixed. James A. Kulik and Chen-Lin C. Kulik, reviewing feedback timing: "Applied studies using actual classroom quizzes and real learning materials have usually found immediate feedback to be more effective than delay." Ericsson agrees, favoring immediate feedback when it helps identify and correct mistakes and lets you execute a corrected version. Laboratory studies, however, tend to favour delaying presentation of the correct response alongside the original task. Young's reading: "The simplest explanation of this result is that presenting the question and answer again offers a second, spaced exposure to the information. If this explanation were correct, all it would mean is that immediate feedback is best paired with delayed review (or further testing)."

**His recommendation: generally faster feedback** — with one serious caveat. "There's a possible risk that this recommendation might backslide into getting feedback before you've tried your best." Early studies showing neutral or negative effects of immediate feedback often let subjects see the correct answer *before finishing the prompt*, so they could copy rather than retrieve. "**Feedback too soon may turn your retrieval practice effectively into passive review.**" His fix: "For hard problems, I suggest setting yourself a timer to encourage you to think hard on difficult problems before giving up to look at the correct answer."

## Key Concepts

- **Chris Rock at the Comedy Cellar.** Rock has sold-out shows and HBO specials; his performances "feel like a rock concert," with a delivery so precise "you get the feeling he would be able to make anything funny. And that's exactly the problem. When everything you do is funny, how do you know what really makes a joke good?" So he enters from a narrow back staircase onto a modest brick-backed stage in Greenwich Village, holding scraps of cards scribbled with bits of phrases — a trick learned from his grandfather, a cab driver who preached on weekends — and slumps against the back wall instead of using his signature aggressive style. "This is his laboratory, and he's going to perform comedy with the precision of an experiment." He warns the crowd: "It's not going to be that good.... Not at these prices. At these prices, I could leave right now!" He imagines the review: "Chris came out and he left. It was good! He didn't tell any jokes—but it was good!" His stated purpose: "They'll give you about six minutes because you're famous ... then you're back to square one." **He wants to know what's funny when he's not trying to be funny.**
- **Not unique to Rock.** The Comedy Cellar is famous for big-name drop-ins — Dave Chappelle, Jon Stewart, Amy Schumer — testing material in front of small crowds before prime-time specials and concert-scale gigs.
- **Ericsson and deliberate practice.** Feedback features prominently in the research: Ericsson found that immediate feedback on one's performance is essential to reaching expert levels. "No feedback, and the result is often stagnation—long periods of time when you continue to use a skill but don't get any better at it. Sometimes the lack of feedback can even result in declining abilities. Many medical practitioners get worse with more experience as their accumulated knowledge from medical school begins to fade and the accuracy of their diagnoses is not given the rapid feedback that would normally promote further learning."
- **Kluger and DeNisi's meta-analysis.** Hundreds of studies. Overall effect positive, "but it's important to note that in over 38 percent of cases, feedback actually had a negative impact." Their explanation is the *type*: feedback works when it provides useful information guiding future learning, and backfires when aimed at the ego. "Praise, a common type of feedback that teachers often use (and students enjoy), is usually harmful to further learning. When feedback steers into evaluations of you as an individual (e.g., 'You're so smart!' or 'You're lazy'), it usually has a negative impact." Even informative feedback must be processed correctly; some negative results came from subjects who "rejected the feedback, lowered the standards they expect from themselves, or given up on the learning task altogether." And who delivers it matters — feedback from a peer or teacher carries social dynamics beyond information.
- **Two guardrails for the ultralearner.** (1) *Don't overreact* to feedback — positive or negative — that carries no specific improvement information. "Eric Barone, for instance, did not attend to every comment and critique on early drafts of his game. In many cases he ignored them, when the feedback conflicted with his vision." (2) *Watch motivation in both directions*: "Not only can overly negative feedback lower your motivation, but so can overly positive feedback." This is the research basis for Rock's strategy — "disregarding the positive feedback that his celebrity automatically generates."
- **Why feedback is a comparative advantage.** "Feedback is uncomfortable. It can be harsh and discouraging.... Standing up on a stage in a comedy club to deliver jokes is probably one of the best ways to get better at stand-up comedy. But the act itself can be terrifying, as an awkward silence cuts deep." And crucially: "**Fear of feedback often feels more uncomfortable than experiencing the feedback itself.** As a result, it is not so much negative feedback on its own that can impede progress but the fear of hearing criticism that causes us to shut down." Which yields the counterintuitive move: "Sometimes the best action is just to dive straight into the hardest environment, since even if the feedback is very negative initially, it can reduce your fears of getting started."
- **De Montebello's immersion.** He could have taken the normal Toastmasters route — carefully prepare a script, deliver once every month or two. "Instead he dove straight in, speaking several times per week, jumping among different clubs to gather different perspectives on his performance. This deep dive into feedback was uncomfortable, but the rapid immersion also desensitized him to a lot of the anxiety that being onstage can create."
- **On solitude.** "The self-directed nature of ultralearning shouldn't convince you that learning is best done as an entirely solitary pursuit." Paying for a tutor can be worth it precisely because they can identify the exact mistake and correct it with less struggle on your part.

## Mental Models

- **Ego vs. task.** The single best predictor of whether feedback helps is whether it is about the work or about you.
- **Signal and noise.** Treat any single data point as mostly noise; design proxy measures that strip randomness before you act.
- **Information = unpredictability.** Feedback you could have predicted teaches you nothing. Being reliably successful is as uninformative as reliably failing.
- **Take the punches early.** "It's better to get in and take the punches early so that they don't put you down for the count. Though short-term feedback can be stressful, once you get into the habit of receiving it, it becomes easier to process without overreacting emotionally."
- **Feedback is not maximization.** "Ultralearning isn't simply about maximizing feedback but also knowing when to selectively ignore elements of it to extract the useful information."

## Anti-patterns

- Avoiding feedback until you think it will be favourable — the same avoidance pattern as delaying self-testing.
- Praise-seeking, and building an environment that reliably makes you feel good.
- Asking for element-level critique when the assessment is genuinely holistic.
- Treating a non-expert's suggested fix as corrective feedback.
- Changing your whole approach on one data point.
- Peeking at the answer before you've genuinely attempted the problem — converting retrieval into review.
- Long-cycle practice: preparing carefully and performing rarely, when frequent rough performances would teach more.

## Reference Tables

| Symptom | Likely feedback problem | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Plateau despite continued use of the skill | No feedback at all — stagnation | Add any outcome feedback (a blog, an audience, a score) |
| Know you're wrong, don't know why | Only outcome feedback | Find per-element signal, or an expert |
| Know what's wrong, can't fix it | Only informational feedback | Coach, mentor, tutor, or solution keys |
| Wildly inconsistent reactions | Not yet good enough for signal to emerge | Keep iterating; consistency is the tell |
| Always succeeding | Difficulty too low, no information | Raise difficulty or tighten standards |
| Always failing | Difficulty too high | Simplify until you can notice what works |
| Wide swings from one data point | Noise | Build a proxy signal |
| Learning rate collapsing | Method exhausted | Metafeedback → experiment |

## Worked Example

**Chris Rock's Comedy Cellar experiment, as a feedback design.**

The problem Rock is solving is not "how do I get more feedback?" — as a famous comedian he gets vast amounts. It's that his feedback is **saturated at the top of the scale**, which by the information rule means it carries almost no signal: when the audience laughs at everything, laughter stops distinguishing good jokes from bad ones.

His countermeasures, each mapping to a tactic in this chapter:

| Move | Tactic it implements |
|---|---|
| Small unannounced club instead of a concert hall | Hitting the difficulty sweet spot — remove the guaranteed-success condition |
| Slumping against the back wall instead of his signature aggressive delivery | Noise cancellation — strip out the performance style that makes anything funny |
| Reading from scraps of cards rather than a polished set | Same — remove the polish so the material is what's being tested |
| Telling the crowd it won't be good, and undercutting the ticket price | Deflating the celebrity premium that generates automatic positive feedback |
| Knowing "they'll give you about six minutes because you're famous ... then you're back to square one" | Explicit model of where the signal starts |
| Doing this repeatedly, in a club known for drop-ins | High-intensity, rapid feedback |

The feedback he collects is **informational**: a joke lands or it doesn't. It is deliberately not corrective — "they can't tell him what to do to make it funnier—he's the comedian, not them" — which is the correct read, and precisely the upgrade Young warns against faking.

Contrast with de Montebello, who *did* have access to corrective feedback through Michael Gendler, and used it "to spot subtle weaknesses in his presentations that would have gone unnoticed by himself or by a less experienced audience member." Same domain family, different feedback grade available, different design.

## Key Takeaways

- Feedback is essential for expertise and negative in over a third of studies; the discriminator is task-directed information versus ego evaluation.
- Three grades: outcome, informational, corrective. Know which you have; never manufacture a fake upgrade.
- Praise and reliable success are as useless as no feedback, because they are predictable.
- Filter aggressively: proxy signals, ignoring what conflicts with a clear vision, and reading consistency rather than content when advice conflicts.
- Metafeedback — your learning rate — tells you when to change strategy and lets you A/B test study methods.
- Prefer fast feedback, but not before you've genuinely attempted the retrieval. Use a timer on hard problems.
- The fear of feedback costs more than the feedback. Dive into the hardest environment early and desensitize.
