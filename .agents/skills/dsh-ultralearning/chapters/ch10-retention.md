# Chapter X — Principle 7: Retention — Don't Fill a Leaky Bucket

> "Memory is the residue of thought." — Daniel Willingham, cognitive psychologist

## Core Idea

Forgetting is the default, not the exception. Our minds are "a leaky bucket; however, most of the holes are near the top, so the water that remains at the bottom leaks out more slowly." Retention therefore splits into two distinct problems — retaining what you learn *during* a project so you don't relearn week one in week ten, and retaining the skill *after* the project ends — and there are exactly four mechanisms that answer them: **spacing, proceduralization, overlearning, and mnemonics**. Young's exemplar is Nigel Richards, who won the French World Scrabble Championship without speaking French, on nine weeks of preparation.

## Frameworks Introduced

**Three theories of forgetting.**

1. **Decay — forgetting with time.** Memories erode as time passes; last week's news is clearer than last month's. Matches common sense, but incomplete: many people vividly recall early childhood while forgetting last Tuesday's breakfast, and "vivid, meaningful things are more easily recalled than banal or arbitrary information." Decay is probably a component, "it seems exceedingly unlikely that this is the only factor."
2. **Interference — overwriting old memories with new.** Memories, unlike computer files, overlap in storage, so similar-but-distinct memories compete. Young's programming example: you learn what a `for` loop is, then learn while loops, recursion, repeat-until loops, and go-to statements — all about doing something repeatedly in different ways, all interfering with each other.
   - *Proactive interference* — previously learned information makes new knowledge harder to acquire; "the 'space' where that information wants to be stored is already occupied." His example: learning **negative reinforcement** in psychology, where "negative" means *absent*, not *bad* — negative reinforcement encourages a behavior by removing something, such as a painful stimulus. Because "negative = bad" already exists, it becomes easy to incorrectly equate negative reinforcement with punishment.
   - *Retroactive interference* — new learning erases or suppresses an old memory. "Anyone who has learned Spanish and later tried to learn French knows how tricky retroactive interference can be, as French words pop out when you want to speak Spanish again."
3. **Forgotten cues — a locked box with no key.** Many memories aren't lost, just inaccessible: retrieval requires a cue, one link in the chain has been severed (perhaps by decay or interference), and the whole memory becomes unreachable. Supported intuitively by tip-of-the-tongue experiences, and by the fact that relearning is much faster than first learning — "relearning is closer to repair work, while original learning is a completely new construction." Caveat: many memory researchers hold that remembering is an active, creative reconstruction, so memories are modified in the act of recall. "It may be, then, that 'lost' memories that are retrieved through new cues are actually fabrications. This seems especially likely in the case of 'recovered' witness testimony from traumatic events, as experiments have shown that even highly vivid memories that feel completely authentic to the subject can be untrue."

**The four retention mechanisms.**

**1. Spacing — repeat to remember.** "One of the pieces of studying advice that is best supported by research is that if you care about long-term retention, don't cram." Spreading sessions over more intervals and longer periods produces somewhat lower short-run performance (you forget between intervals) but much better long-run performance. **The rule of thumb: "If you have ten hours to learn something, therefore, it makes more sense to spend ten days studying one hour each than to spend ten hours studying in one burst."** But the trade-off inverts if intervals get too long — "If you learn something with a decade separating study intervals, it's quite possible that you'll completely forget whatever you had learned."

- *Young's own application.* After his first few MIT classes he switched from one class at a time to a few in parallel, "to minimize the impact that the crammed study time would have on my memory."
- *Spaced-repetition systems (SRS).* Finding the exact trade-off point "has been a minor obsession for some ultralearners." SRS was a major force behind Roger Craig's *Jeopardy!* memorization, and Young used it extensively for Chinese and Korean. The principle underlies many language products — Pimsleur, Memrise, Duolingo — which hide the algorithm; the open-source **Anki** is "the preferred tool of more extreme ultralearners who want to squeeze out a little more performance." SRS is ideally suited to facts, trivia, vocabulary, and definitions — knowledge presentable as a question with a single answer — and is "more difficult to apply to more complicated domains of knowledge, which rely on complex information associations that are built up only through real-world practice." The authors of a popular study guide for medical students centre their approach on SRS precisely because the volume is so large that forgetting-and-relearning is prohibitively expensive.
- *Spacing without software.* Richards's method — printing lists of words, reading them over, then rehearsing them mentally without them in front of him — "is an incredibly powerful technique." Semiregular practice of a skill also works: after the Year Without English, Young scheduled **thirty minutes of conversation practice once a week** over Skype using **italki**, maintained for one year, then dropped to **once per month for another two years**. "I don't know whether this practice schedule was ideal ... but I believe it was much better than doing nothing and letting the skills atrophy. **When it comes to retention, don't let perfect become the enemy of good enough.**"
- *Refresher projects.* For elaborate skills that resist daily habits. Young preferred this for the MIT Challenge material, since the skill he most wanted to keep was writing code, "which is tricky to do on only half an hour per week." It deviates from optimal spacing, "however, if you're prepared to do a little bit of relearning to compensate, it can still be a better approach than completely giving up practice." Scheduling maintenance in advance also "remind[s] you that learning isn't something done once and then ignored but a process that continues for your entire life."

**2. Proceduralization — automatic will endure.** "Why do people say it's 'like riding a bicycle' and not 'like remembering trigonometry?'" There is evidence that procedural skills are stored differently from declarative knowledge (the Pythagorean Theorem, the Sine Rule), and that this *knowing how* versus *knowing that* distinction has different long-term memory implications: procedural skills "are much less susceptible to being forgotten."

- *The typing example.* Starting out, you memorize letter positions, and each word means recalling each letter's position and moving your finger — a process that can fail, sending you to look down at the keyboard. With practice you stop looking down, then stop thinking about positions, then stop thinking about letters at all as whole words come out. Ask a good typist where a letter is and "you might need to actually put your hands in the keyboard position (or imagine you're doing so) and pretend to type" it to answer — "This is exactly what happened to me as I was typing out this paragraph." The explicit memory that was originally the primary access point has faded, and the durable procedural encoding in motor movement now has to be consulted. Same phenomenon with often-used passwords and PINs remembered "by feel."
- *The heuristic.* "Instead of learning a large volume of knowledge or skills evenly, you may emphasize a core set of information much more frequently, so that it becomes procedural and is stored far longer." This was an unintentional side effect of the Year Without English: being forced to speak constantly meant a core set of phrases and patterns repeated so often that "neither of us will ever forget them" — while classic language courses that "move on" from beginner patterns leave them insufficiently sticky.
- *Young's self-criticism.* "Failing to fully proceduralize core skills was a major flaw of my first major self-education effort, the MIT Challenge, which I was able to improve upon in my subsequent language-learning and portrait-drawing projects." The MIT Challenge did repeat core mathematical and programming skills, "what ended up being proceduralized was more haphazard rather than reflecting a conscious decision to automate the most essential skills."
- *Partial proceduralization is normal.* You may move variables across an equation without thinking but have to think when exponents or trigonometry appear. Some skills may never fully automate. Young offers two speculative strategies — labelled as such: ensure a certain amount of knowledge is completely proceduralized before practice concludes, or deliberately proceduralize skills that act as *cues or access points* for other knowledge (e.g. fully automating the process of starting a new programming project so you can get over that hump).

**3. Overlearning — practice beyond perfect.** "Additional practice, beyond what is required to perform adequately, can increase the length of time that memories are stored." The typical experiment: give subjects a task (assembling a rifle, running an emergency checklist), time them to their first correct performance — that's the learning phase — then vary how much *overlearning* follows. Performance doesn't improve past that point, "However, the overlearning can extend the durability of the skill."

- *Scale.* In the standard setting the effect is short-lived: "practicing a little longer in one session produces an additional week or two of recall," which suits first aid or emergency protocols kept fresh between trainings. Young suspects — again explicitly speculative — that combined with spacing and proceduralization across long projects it has longer-term implications. His evidence: the facial-feature mapping thought process from Vitruvian Studio, repeated so many times in one month that it's hard to forget; and certain programming and mathematics reflexes still easily recalled from the MIT Challenge without interim practice, "because they happened to be patterns that were repeated far more than was necessary to perform them adequately at the time (because they were components of more elaborate problems)."
- *Why it pairs with directness.* "Because direct use of a skill frequently involves overpracticing certain core abilities, that kernel is usually quite resistant to forgetting, even years later. In contrast, academically learned subjects tend to distribute practice more evenly to cover the entire curriculum to a minimum level of competency in each area, regardless of the centrality of subtopics to practical applications." Hence people who learned a language through years of schooling may have better vocabulary and grammatical nuance than Young "may trip over fairly basic phrases, because they learned every fact and skill evenly, rather than overlearning the smaller subset of very common patterns."
- *Two methods.* (a) **Core practice** — continually practicing and refining the core elements, often paired with immersion or extensive (as opposed to intensive) projects after the ultralearning phase. Young notes this shift from learning to doing "may actually involve a deeper, subtler form of learning, which shouldn't be discounted." (b) **Advanced practice** — go one level above, so the lower-level skills get overlearned in service of a harder domain. **The algebra study:** most students retested years after an algebra class had forgotten huge amounts, and the *rate* of forgetting was the same for better and poorer students (better students retained more, but decayed just as fast). One group did not show the steep decline: **those who had gone on to take calculus.**

**4. Mnemonics — a picture retains a thousand words.** Many strategies exist; what they share is that they are **hyperspecific** (designed for very particular patterns of information) and that they "usually involve translating abstract or arbitrary information into vivid pictures or spatial maps." Rajveer Meena, the Guinness World Record holder for digits of pi, knows it to **70,000 decimal places**. Competitive mnemonicists memorize a shuffled deck in under sixty seconds and repeat a poem verbatim after a minute or two of study. "These feats are quite impressive, and even better, they can be learned by anyone patient enough to apply them."

- *The keyword method.* Take a foreign word and convert it into something it sounds like in your native language, then build a vivid, bizarre mental image combining the sounds-like version with the meaning. Young's worked case: French **chavirer** (to capsize) → "shave an ear" → imagine "a giant ear shaving a long beard while sitting in a boat that capsizes." To recall "capsize" in French: capsizing → the picture → "shaving an ear" → *chavirer*. "This process sounds needlessly complicated and elaborate at first, but it benefits from converting a difficult association (between arbitrary sounds and a new meaning) into a few links that are much easier to associate and remember. With practice, each conversion of this type may take only fifteen to twenty seconds." Other mnemonics exist for lists, numbers, maps, and procedural sequences; Young recommends Joshua Foer's *Moonwalking with Einstein: The Art and Science of Remembering Everything* as an introduction.
- *Two disadvantages, which are why mnemonics come last.* (1) **Up-front cost versus usefulness.** The most impressive systems require considerable investment, and then "you can memorize digits easily, but this isn't actually a very useful task. Most of our society adapts around the fact that people generally cannot memorize digits, so we have paper and computers do it for us." (2) **Recall is not automatic.** "Knowing a mnemonic for a foreign-language word is better than failing to remember it entirely, but it's still too slow to allow you to fluently form sentences out of mnemonically remembered words. Thus mnemonics can act as a bridge for difficult-to-remember information, but it's usually not the final step."
- *Verdict.* "An incredibly powerful if somewhat brittle tool." Use for very dense information in a very specific format, especially over a few weeks or months, or as an intermediate strategy while initial acquisition is hardest. Young found them useful for language learning and terminology, and paired with SRS "they can form an effective bridge from feeling as though there's no way you can possibly remember everything to remembering it so deeply that you can't possibly forget." But in a world with paper and computers, "I feel that mnemonics tend to serve more as cool tricks than as a foundation you should base your learning efforts on. Still, there is a devoted subset of ultralearners who are fiercely committed to applying these techniques, so my word shouldn't be the final verdict."

## Key Concepts

- **Nigel Richards.** An engineer born and raised in Christchurch, New Zealand, with a long beard and retro aviator sunglasses — "a cross between Gandalf and Napoleon Dynamite." He started Scrabble late, in his late twenties, encouraged by his mother: "Nigel, since you're no good at words, you won't be good at this game, but it will keep you occupied." He had already won three World Scrabble Championships when, in Louvain-la-Neuve, Belgium, he won the **French** championship.
- **Why the French win is harder.** Most English dictionary versions have roughly **200,000** valid word entries; French, with gendered nouns and adjectives and copious conjugations, has "nearly double that with around **386,000** valid word forms." Richards **doesn't speak French**. He beat a Francophone player, Schelick Ilagou Rekawe from Gabon, in the final, received a standing ovation, and needed a translator to thank the audience. He had spent only **nine weeks** preparing, and entered mainly as an excuse to do a cycling trip through Europe.
- **Why it's possible at all.** Precedent exists: Scrabble is popular in Thailand and two former world champions, Panupol Sujjayakorn and Pakorn Nemitrmansuk, are not fluent in English. "Remembering words in one's native language and remembering words in Scrabble are different mnemonic feats. In spoken language, the meaning of a word, its pronunciation, and its feel are important. In Scrabble, those things don't matter; words are just combinations of letters." A native speaker has an advantage from familiar spellings, but there remain many arcane words to memorize, and the rearranging-and-scoring skill is identical in every language.
- **What tournament Scrabble demands.** Memory of all the short words (a decent casual player learns the two-letter words, including "AA," a type of lava, and "OE," a windstorm in the Faroe Islands) plus longer seven- and eight-letter words, because using all seven tiles in a turn earns a fifty-point "bingo." Plus speed under a timing system, finding spaces and calculating maximum points. Richards's illustrative play: given **CDHLRN and one blank**, he ignored the obvious **CHILDREN** and instead linked up multiple crosswords to make the higher-scoring **CHLORODYNE**.
- **The clues to his method.** He refuses interviews and is famously laconic. Asked whether he had special memorization methods: "No." What is known: he **reads lists** of words, starting with two-letter words and moving up; he reads the dictionary "focusing exclusively on combinations of letters, ignoring definitions, tenses, and plurals"; and then, cycling for hours, he draws them from memory and repeats them. "The cycling helps. I can go through lists in my mind." He focuses on memory, not anagramming; works forward and backward, from small words to big ones and back; and recalls words **visually** — he cannot remember words when they're spoken.
- **The cycling as personality evidence.** Before an early tournament in Dunedin, he finished work, got on his bicycle, and pedaled over **two hundred miles** from Christchurch without sleeping, starting the tournament first thing in the morning. After winning, he declined offers of a ride home and cycled the entire way back for another sleepless night before Monday work. Young reads this as "an obsessive intensity that exceeds what is considered a normal investment of effort" — the same trait seen in other ultralearners — and notes it is also the mechanism that makes hours of mental list rehearsal possible. Richards's own view leans the same way: "It's hard work, you have to have dedication to learn," and "I'm not sure there is a secret, it's just a matter of learning the words."
- **What Young can't rule out.** He is honest that the questions remain open: how many readings before he can rehearse mentally? Are the lists organized or alphabetical? Is he a savant, an all-round genius, or someone of average intelligence with extreme dedication? "We might never know the answers.... I certainly can't rule out the theory that Richards's mind is simply hardwired differently or better for memory than my own. After all, nothing I've encountered so far about his method is so boldly original that serious Scrabble players would be unaware of it."
- **Why memory matters outside Scrabble.** "Programmers must remember the syntax for the commands in their code. Accountants need to memorize ratios, rules, and regulations. Lawyers must remember precedents and statutes. Doctors need to know tens of thousands of factoids, from anatomical descriptions to drug interactions.... Being able to understand how something works or how to perform a particular technique is useless if you cannot recall it."
- **The doctors study.** One study reported that doctors give worse medical care the longer they've worked, as medical school knowledge fades despite full-time practice. Quoted abstract: "Physicians with more experience are generally believed to have accumulated knowledge and skills during years in practice and therefore to deliver high-quality care. However, evidence suggests that there is an inverse relationship between the number of years that a physician has been in practice and the quality of care that the physician provides."
- **Ebbinghaus.** In one of the first psychological experiments in history, Hermann Ebbinghaus spent years memorizing nonsense syllables — "much in the same way Richards memorizes Scrabble words" — and tracking his later recall. The result, later verified by more robust studies, is the **forgetting curve**: an exponential decay steepest immediately after learning, tapering off over time.

## Mental Models

- **Two retention problems, not one.** *During the project*: how do you keep week one's learning through week ten? Critical for memory-intensive work like Lewis's languages or Craig's trivia, "the volume of information to be learned is often so large that the forgetting becomes a practical obstacle almost immediately." *After the project*: how do you avoid losing a language completely two years later?
- **System fit beats system optimality.** Craig preferred elaborate electronic systems that optimize with algorithms at the cost of complexity; Richards prefers basic systems that succeed on simplicity. "You need to pick a mnemonic system, which will both accomplish your goals and be simple enough to stick to." Young's own mix: SRS during intense vocabulary phases; plain conversation for maintaining speaking; and for some subjects, accepting some forgetting "as long as I practice the skills I need to use continuously and have the ability to relearn." "My approaches may not reach a theoretical ideal, but they may end up working better because they have fewer possibilities for error and can be sustained more easily."

## Anti-patterns

- Cramming when you care about long-term retention.
- Spreading practice perfectly evenly across a curriculum, so nothing becomes automatic.
- "Moving on" from beginner patterns before they proceduralize.
- Building a retention system so elaborate you abandon it.
- Basing a learning strategy on mnemonics, or investing in mnemonic feats (pi digits) that society already solves with paper and computers.
- Letting perfect spacing prevent any maintenance practice at all.
- Assuming that using a skill professionally maintains the knowledge behind it (the doctors study).

## Worked Example

**Reverse-engineering Nigel Richards, mechanism by mechanism.**

Young cannot get Richards to explain himself, so the chapter does the next best thing: takes the fragments that are public and maps them onto the four mechanisms.

| Known fragment | Mechanism | How it works |
|---|---|---|
| Reads printed lists of words, two-letter first, moving up | Spacing (manual) | Repeated passes over the same lists across days |
| Rehearses the lists **from memory** while cycling for hours | Spacing + **retrieval** | Not review — active recall, the Chapter VIII effect, at extreme volume |
| Reads the dictionary ignoring definitions, tenses, plurals | Scope control | Reduces the target to letter combinations only, which is all Scrabble scores |
| Works forward and backward, small words to big and back | Interference management | Repeated re-entry from different directions rebuilds cues |
| Recalls words visually; cannot remember words spoken | Encoding channel | His retrieval cue is visual pattern, matching the tile-and-board task |
| Focuses on memory, not anagramming | Rate-determining step (Ch. VII) | He drills the bottleneck rather than the manipulation |
| 200+ mile overnight cycle rides; nine weeks for a French championship | Obsessive intensity | The volume that makes all of the above sufficient |

What's absent is as informative as what's present: **no mnemonics** (his memory is direct and visual, not built on keyword images), and **no software** — Richards is the counterexample to Craig's algorithmic approach, and Young uses the pair to make the "pick the system you'll actually sustain" point.

The concluding assessment: "What we do know about him fits the picture of other ultralearners who have dominated memory-intensive subjects: active recall, spaced rehearsal, and an obsessive commitment to intense practice. Whether you or I have the will to go as far as Richards does is an open question, but with hard work and a good strategy, it seems likely to me that the battle against forgetting need not be a losing one."

## Key Takeaways

- Forgetting is exponential and steepest right after learning; plan around it rather than being surprised by it.
- Three plausible causes — decay, interference, forgotten cues — each suggesting different countermeasures.
- Ten hours of learning is better as one hour a day for ten days than as a single ten-hour block.
- Deliberately overweight a *core* set of skills until they proceduralize; automatic knowledge barely decays.
- Overlearning buys durability; moving up a level (calculus preserving algebra) is the strongest version.
- Mnemonics are powerful, narrow, and slow to recall — a bridge, not a foundation.
- Pick the retention system you will actually sustain. Don't let perfect become the enemy of good enough.
