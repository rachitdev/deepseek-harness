# Chapter III — How to Become an Ultralearner

## Core Idea

Every case so far was found *after* the fact, which leaves open the possibility that Young was simply sifting pebbles for gold. This chapter runs the prospective test: he recruited about a dozen volunteers (mostly blog readers) and coached them into ultralearning projects, then watched. Tristan de Montebello — a musician and web consultant with almost no speaking experience — went from his first Toastmasters meeting to the top ten of the World Championship of Public Speaking in under seven months, and then changed careers. The chapter's conclusion is that you can *decide* to become an ultralearner, and it closes by naming the nine principles that structure the rest of the book.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Principles over procedures.** Ultralearning "isn't a cookie-cutter method"; every project is unique, and "if ultralearning could be bottled or standardized, it would simply be an intense form of structured education." Young therefore teaches principles: "Principles allow you to solve problems, even those you may have never encountered before, in a way that a recipe or mechanical procedure cannot." Each principle chapter gives evidence from ultralearners *and* from cognitive science, plus sample tactics — explicitly "only a small sample," a starting point for your own thinking.
- **How the nine were derived.** Young started with the ultralearners. "If one person did something in a certain way, that might be an interesting example, but it might also be an idiosyncrasy of that person. If several people or, better yet, every ultralearner I encountered, did a certain thing in a certain way, it was much stronger evidence that I had stumbled upon a general principle." He then checked each against the cognitive science literature, preferring controlled comparisons of one approach against another.
- **The ultralearning ethos.** Beyond principles and tactics: "taking responsibility for your own learning: deciding what you want to learn, how you want to learn it, and crafting your own plan." Treat the principles as flexible guidelines, not rigid rules.

## Key Concepts

- **The nine principles, in Young's own words.**
  1. **Metalearning: First Draw a Map.** Start by learning how to learn the subject. Do good research; draw on past competencies.
  2. **Focus: Sharpen Your Knife.** Cultivate concentration. Carve out chunks of time, and make it easy to just do it.
  3. **Directness: Go Straight Ahead.** Learn by doing the thing you want to be good at. Don't trade it for more convenient or comfortable tasks.
  4. **Drill: Attack Your Weakest Point.** Be ruthless about weak points. Break complex skills into parts, master the parts, rebuild.
  5. **Retrieval: Test to Learn.** Testing creates knowledge, not just measures it. Test before you feel confident; actively recall rather than passively review.
  6. **Feedback: Don't Dodge the Punches.** Use it without letting your ego in the way. Extract signal from noise.
  7. **Retention: Don't Fill a Leaky Bucket.** Understand what you forget and why. Remember not just for now but forever.
  8. **Intuition: Dig Deep Before Building Up.** Build intuition through play and exploration; don't use memorization tricks to avoid deep knowing.
  9. **Experimentation: Explore Outside Your Comfort Zone.** The others are starting points; mastery comes from exploring possibilities others haven't imagined.
- **Choosing the skill.** De Montebello first proposed piano — a safe choice given he had played guitar, fronted a band, and even sold an online guitar course. Young deliberately pushed him further outside his comfort zone, because "a musician picking up another instrument didn't seem like the ideal case to study." A week or two later he chose public speaking, having given only a handful of speeches, mostly in college.
- **The private motivation.** A talk to a dozen people at a Paris web design firm: "I cringe every time I think back to that. I could just tell I wasn't connecting. There were many pieces where I was boring them. There were jokes where I would laugh, because I thought it was funny, but nobody else would." Despite the stage experience, he was surprised "how little of it translated."
- **Public speaking as a metaskill.** De Montebello's argument for the choice: "It's the kind of skill that assists with other skills: confidence, storytelling, writing, creativity, interviewing skills, selling skills. It touches on so many different things."
- **Two doses of luck.** At his very first Toastmasters meeting, Michael Gendler — a longtime Toastmaster — was present and agreed to coach him. And he had arrived ten days before the eligibility deadline for the World Championship of Public Speaking, which gave the project a competitive structure.
- **What actually differentiated him.** Not a belief that he could reach the finals in six months — "Rather, it was his obsessive work ethic. His goal wasn't to reach some predetermined extreme but to see how far he could go."
- **The failure mode.** Of the dozen or so Young coached, some dropped out — "Life got in the way (or perhaps revealed that they weren't actually as committed as they had initially appeared to be)." Others made significant improvements in medicine, statistics, comic book drawing, military history, and yoga without de Montebello's drama. "Even the failure mode of ultralearning is usually that you will learn a skill fairly well."

## Mental Models

- **Pick the scariest option.** When de Montebello asked whether to polish an existing speech or write a new one from scratch, Gendler told him to do whichever was scariest.
- **Deliberate audience diversity.** Speaking several times per week and hopping between clubs gathers different perspectives, not just more repetitions.
- **"Make me care."** Gendler's core note after a speech: "I understand why this is important to you, but the audience doesn't care about you. You have to make me care."
- **Deform the performance to find its baseline.** A Hollywood director friend had him deliver the same speech in different styles — angry, monotone, screaming, even as a rap — then compare against his normal voice. This broke the "uncanny valley" where his ordinary delivery felt slightly unnatural.
- **Words as choreography.** A friend with a theater background walked him through the speech showing how each word and sentence implied movement he could translate into stage position, so he could use his body rather than stand constricted under the spotlight.
- **Read the room before you're on stage.** After bombing at a middle school, he learned to talk to the audience beforehand — learn their language and emotions — so he could change the speech on the fly.

## Anti-patterns

- Choosing the comfortable adjacent skill (piano for a guitarist) when the point is to test whether the method generalizes.
- Preparing in isolation and delivering rarely — the default Toastmasters cadence Young contrasts with de Montebello's several-times-a-week schedule.
- Waiting to be ready. He cranked out six mandatory qualifying speeches inside a week, finishing the last "in the nick of time."
- Treating the principles as a checklist or protocol to copy step by step.
- Attributing outcomes to genius. Young's explicit point is that had de Montebello chosen piano, his speaking would still be that one awkward Paris talk.

## Worked Example

**De Montebello's project, principle by principle** — the chapter's implicit demonstration that the nine principles are descriptive of a real project, not imposed afterward:

- **Metalearning** — he did not invent a curriculum; he benchmarked against Toastmasters, an existing institution for learning public speaking, and adopted its competition ladder as project structure.
- **Focus** — obsessive practice, "sometimes speaking twice in one day."
- **Directness** — the practice *was* giving speeches to real audiences, from the first week.
- **Drill** — the director's exercise isolates vocal delivery from content; the theater friend's exercise isolates movement from words.
- **Retrieval** — improv training removes the script, forcing him to produce rather than recite.
- **Feedback** — video review, feedback requested after every speech, multiple clubs for varied perspectives, and a coach for corrective feedback. Middle schoolers because they "would give the most ruthless feedback of all."
- **Retention** — the competition ladder forced repeated delivery of evolving material over months.
- **Intuition** — reading an audience before going on stage, then adapting live, is the skill of understanding *why* something lands rather than memorizing a script.
- **Experimentation** — running the same speech in wildly different styles is a deliberate exploration of the style space (Chapter XII's third level of experimentation).

Outcome beyond the ranking: "I was learning for this very narrow world of public speaking. It was only after that I realized the depth of all these skills I had worked so much on: storytelling, confidence, communicating." Friends asked for help; authors commanding five-figure speaking fees approached; he and Gendler went full-time as UltraSpeaking.

## Key Takeaways

- The prospective test worked once, dramatically, and worked partially many other times — which is the honest result.
- You can decide to become an ultralearner; dramatic success is not the inevitable consequence of a particular genius.
- Obsessive work ethic and "see how far I can go" framing beat a fixed target.
- The nine principles were derived by looking for what *recurred* across ultralearners, then checked against cognitive science.
- Treat the principles as flexible guidelines and test solutions yourself.
