# Chapter VII — Principle 4: Drill — Attack Your Weakest Point

> "Take care of the bars and the piece will take care of itself." — Philip Johnston, composer

## Core Idea

Bulk direct practice is not enough. Complex skills have a **rate-determining step** — one component that governs overall performance — and they also spread your cognitive resources so thin that no single component can improve. Drills fix both problems by isolating one component so all your attention lands on it. This directly contradicts Principle 3, and Young resolves the contradiction not by choosing a side but by cycling: **Direct → Drill → Direct**. His exemplar is Benjamin Franklin, who left an unusually detailed record of how he dissected the skill of writing.

## Frameworks Introduced

**The rate-determining step.** Borrowed from chemistry: in a multi-step reaction where the products of one step become the reagents of the next, the slowest step is the bottleneck that defines the time for the whole reaction. "Learning, I'd like to argue, often works similarly, with certain aspects of the learning problem forming a bottleneck that controls the speed at which you can become more proficient overall."

- *Mathematics.* You need the concepts, the algorithms, and the judgment about when each applies — but underlying all of it is arithmetic and algebra. "If your arithmetic is weak or your algebra sloppy, you'll get the wrong answers even if you've mastered the other concepts."
- *Foreign language.* Vocabulary. "The number of sentences you can successfully utter depends on how many words you know.... If you were able to suddenly inject hundreds of new words into your mental database, you might drastically expand your fluency even if your pronunciation, grammar, or other linguistic knowledge remains unchanged."

**Drills and cognitive load.** Even with no single isolatable bottleneck, drills help. Practicing a complex skill spreads attention, memory, and effort across many aspects at once. "This can create a learning trap. In order to improve your performance in one aspect, you may need to devote so much attention to that one aspect that the other parts of your performance start to go down." If you judge yourself only on overall performance, improvement appears to stall — you get worse at the whole while getting better at the part. Drills resolve this "by simplifying a skill enough that you can focus your cognitive resources on a single aspect."

**The Direct-Then-Drill Approach.** Three steps, cycled:

1. **Practice the skill directly.** Figure out where and how the skill will be used and match that situation as closely as feasible. Speak the language. Write software. Pen essays. "This initial connection and subsequent feedback loop ensure that the transfer problem won't occur."
2. **Analyze and isolate.** Find components that are either rate-determining steps or subskills you can't improve because too much else is going on. Develop drills for those and practice them separately.
3. **Return to direct practice and integrate.** Two purposes. First, even well-designed drills leave transfer hiccups when an isolated skill moves back into a complex context — "Think of this as being like building the connective tissue to join the muscles you strengthened separately." Second, it's a check on whether the drill was well designed: "Many attempts to isolate a drill may end in failure because the drill doesn't really cut at the heart of what was difficult in real practice. That's okay; this feedback is important."

**Cycle speed by stage.** "The earlier you are in the learning process, the faster this cycle should be." Beginners should cycle between direct practice and drills even within a single session. Later, as noticeable improvement requires more effort, longer detours into drills become acceptable. "As you approach mastery, your time may end up focused mostly on drills as your knowledge of how the complex skill breaks down into individual components becomes more refined and accurate and improving any individual component gets harder and harder."

**The five drill designs.**

1. **Time Slicing.** Isolate a slice in time of a longer sequence. Musicians identify the hardest passages and perfect them before reintegrating into the whole piece. Athletes drill layups or penalty shots — fractions of total playing time. Young, early in a new language, "obsessively repeat[s] a few key phrases, so they quickly get embedded into my long-term memory." Look for moments of heightened difficulty or importance.
2. **Cognitive Components.** Isolate a cognitive aspect rather than a temporal slice. In speaking a language, grammar, pronunciation, and vocabulary all occur at every moment but form distinct components that must be managed simultaneously. Young's Mandarin example: **tone drills** pronouncing pairs of words with different tones and recording himself, "without the distraction of needing to remember what the words meant or how to form grammatically correct sentences."
3. **The Copycat.** For creative skills where you can't practice one aspect without doing all the others. Copy the parts you *don't* want to drill — from someone else or from your own past work — so you can focus exclusively on the component you do. Saves time (you repeat only the drilled part) and reduces cognitive burden. Young started drawing not just from photos but from other people's drawings, which let him focus on accurate rendering while the framing and detail-selection decisions were already made. For flexible creative work, editing your own past output has the same effect.
4. **The Magnifying Glass Method.** When you must create something new and can't separate the part out: spend disproportionately more time on one component than you otherwise would. It may reduce overall performance or increase input time, but a much higher proportion of your time and cognitive resources goes to the target subskill. Young applied it to research for article writing "by spending about ten times as long on research as I had previously" — still doing all the other parts of the article, but long enough on research to build new habits and skills.
5. **Prerequisite Chaining.** Start with a skill you don't have all the prerequisites for; when you inevitably do poorly, go back a step, learn a foundational topic, and repeat the exercise. "This practice of starting too hard and learning prerequisites as they are needed can be frustrating, but it saves a lot of time learning subskills that don't actually drive performance much." Eric Barone made pixel art first, hit trouble with colors, went back and learned color theory, then redid the work. Benny Lewis starts speaking from a phrase book and only later learns the grammar that explains how the phrases function.

## Key Concepts

- **Franklin as a writer first.** Entrepreneur, inventor, scientist, diplomat, founding father — but "first and foremost a writer." After fleeing Boston to escape the final years of his indentured apprenticeship to his brother's printing company, he arrived penniless and unknown in Philadelphia, worked for a printing company, then set up as a competitor. *Poor Richard's Almanack* became an international bestseller and let him retire at forty-two.
- **What writing bought him.** As a scientist he was bad at math and more interested in practical consequences than grand theory — but Sir Humphrey Davy noted his prose was "written equally well for the uninitiated as well as the philosopher," adding "he has rendered his details as amusing as well as perspicuous." In politics, before the Revolution he wrote "An Edict by the King of Prussia," supposedly by Frederick II, satirizing British-American relations by proposing that since early settlers of the British Isles were of German origin, "revenue [should] be raised from said colonies in Britain" by the Prussian king. Later he edited Jefferson's words into "We hold these truths to be self-evident."
- **Why we know his method.** "Unlike so many other great writers, whose efforts at honing their skills remain mysterious, we have Franklin's own words for how he did it." His *Autobiography* details the exercises. The trigger: a childhood debate with a friend about educating women (Franklin for, his friend against), after which his father noticed his writing lacked persuasive ability. Franklin "determined to endeavor at improvement."
- **The Silence Dogood episode.** At sixteen, fearing his elder brother would reject his work out of hand, he disguised his penmanship and submitted essays under the pseudonym Silence Dogood, a widowed woman living in the countryside. His brother, not knowing the author, approved and published. Begun as a ruse, the practice in adopting characters proved invaluable: *Poor Richard's Almanack* was written from the perspective of a simple husband and wife, Richard and Bridget Saunders, and political essays like "An Edict by the King of Prussia" used the same flexibility.
- **What distinguished Franklin** "wasn't merely the amount he wrote or his raw talent but *how* he practiced" — the decision to break writing apart and practice its elements in isolation.
- **When drilling stops being drudgery.** "We've all spent time doing homework designed to drill into us facts and procedures that turned out to be a total waste of time. That was often because we didn't know the reasons behind what we were practicing or how it fit into a broader context. Drilling problems without context is mind-numbing. However, once you've identified that it's the bottleneck preventing you from going further, they become instilled with new purpose." In ultralearning drills are student-directed, and "far from being meaningless drudgery, carefully designed drills elicit creativity and imagination."

## Mental Models

- **The three difficulties of drilling.** (1) *Knowing what to drill.* Ask: "Which aspect of the skill, if you improved it, would cause the greatest improvement to your abilities overall for the least amount of effort?" Look for skills you must juggle simultaneously — writing an article means juggling research, storytelling, and vocabulary at once, making it hard to get much better at just one. The key is to experiment: "Make a hypothesis about what is holding you back, attack it with some drills, using the Direct-Then-Drill Approach, and you can quickly get feedback about whether you're right." (2) *Designing the drill.* Hard because it's easy to remove exactly what made the component difficult in real application. Franklin's drills were uncommon because most people, even recognizing a deficit, "would not have had the ingenuity to find ways to drill subskills such as ordering arguments persuasively and emulating a successful writing style." (3) *Doing them.* "Teasing out the worst thing about your performance and practicing that in isolation takes guts. It's much more pleasant to spend time focusing on things you're already good at."
- **Diagnostic examples of bottlenecks.** Accounting skills limited by superficial Excel knowledge. Language ability held back by inaccurate pronunciation despite knowing the right words.
- **Strenuous beats easy.** "The difficulty and usefulness of drills repeat a pattern that will recur throughout the ultralearning principles: that something mentally strenuous provides a greater benefit to learning than something easy."

## Anti-patterns

- Drilling what you're already good at because it's comfortable.
- Drilling without knowing why — the schoolwork failure mode.
- Designing a drill that removes the actual difficulty along with the surrounding context.
- Staying in drills without returning to direct practice, so the components never reconnect.
- Assuming volume of direct practice will fix a bottleneck it can't reach.
- Judging yourself only on holistic performance while working on a component, and concluding you've stalled.

## Worked Example

**Franklin's drill suite, reconstructed from the *Autobiography* as the chapter reports it.**

Franklin's direct practice was writing essays. His analysis broke the whole skill into components and he built a specific exercise for each.

| Component he isolated | The drill | Drill type |
|---|---|---|
| Reconstructing an argument (content and reasoning) | Take notes on articles from *The Spectator*, his favorite magazine; leave the notes several days; come back and try to reconstruct the original argument from memory. Then "compared my *Spectator* with the original, discovered some of my faults, and corrected them." | Copycat + retrieval |
| Vocabulary range | Turn the prose into verse. Meter and rhyme force him to replace words with synonyms he wouldn't otherwise reach for. | Cognitive Components |
| Rhetorical flow / ordering of ideas | Same imitation exercise, but with the hints **jumbled**, so he had to determine the correct sequence of ideas as he wrote. | Cognitive Components |
| Persuasive stance | Having encountered the Socratic method in an English grammar book, he carefully avoided "abrupt contradiction and positive argumentation," instead being the "humble inquirer and doubter." | Deliberate style constraint |
| Adopting a perspective | Writing as Silence Dogood — begun as a ruse, retained as a skill. | Copycat of voice |

Note what makes the second and third drills clever: he could not practice "ordering arguments logically" without writing a whole essay — the exact problem the Copycat drill exists to solve. By supplying the content from *The Spectator*, he removed the burden of invention and could spend his whole attention on sequence.

And the cost: "My time for these exercises and for reading was at night, after work or before it began in the morning." Franklin was still working long hours in his brother's print shop. Barone's parallel is in the same paragraph — he "repeated his pixel art dozens of times, going back to master prerequisite concepts and theory until he got it perfect."

## Key Takeaways

- Find the rate-determining step; improving it improves everything downstream.
- Drills also exist to free cognitive resources, even when no single bottleneck dominates.
- Direct-Then-Drill-Then-Direct. The return leg both integrates the component and validates the drill.
- Cycle quickly when you're new; drill longer as you approach mastery.
- Five designs cover most cases: time slice, cognitive component, copycat, magnifying glass, prerequisite chaining.
- Hypothesize the bottleneck, drill it, and let the return to direct practice tell you if you were right.
- Drills are only drudgery when you don't know why you're doing them.
