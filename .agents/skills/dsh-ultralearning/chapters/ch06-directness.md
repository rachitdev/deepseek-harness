# Chapter VI — Principle 3: Directness — Go Straight Ahead

> "He who can go to the fountain does not go to the water jar." — Leonardo da Vinci

## Core Idea

**Directness is "the idea of learning being tied closely to the situation or context you want to use it in."** The reason it matters so much is the failure of *transfer*: a century of research shows that knowledge learned in one context stubbornly refuses to move to another, so the safest strategy is to learn where you intend to use it. Directness is uncomfortable — harder, more frustrating, more intense than reading a book or playing with an app — and that discomfort is exactly why it remains "a potent source of competitive advantage for any would-be ultralearner."

## Frameworks Introduced

**The four tactics for learning directly.**

1. **Project-Based Learning.** Organize learning around producing something. "If you organize your learning around producing something, you're guaranteed to at least learn how to produce that thing. If you take classes, you may spend a lot of time taking notes and reading but not achieve your goal." Learning to program by building your own computer game is the canonical case; engineering, design, art, musical composition, carpentry, and writing all lend themselves naturally. Intellectual topics work too: one ultralearner Young interviewed wanted to learn military history and made his project the production of a thesis paper, because his end goal was to converse knowledgeably — more direct than reading many books and creating nothing.
2. **Immersive Learning.** Surround yourself with the target environment. Two benefits: much larger practice volume than normal (you have no choice), and exposure to a fuller range of situations where the skill applies. Language learning is canonical, but not the only case — joining communities actively engaged in the skill has a similar effect, e.g. novice programmers joining open-source projects to face new coding challenges.
3. **The Flight Simulator Method.** For skills you can't legally or safely practice directly (piloting, surgery), simulate — and note that "what matters for transfer is not every possible feature of the learning environment, such as what room you're in or what clothes you're wearing." It's the **cognitive** features: "situations where you need to make decisions about what to do and cue knowledge you've stored in your head." A flight simulator can be as good as a real plane for learning "if it sufficiently calls on the discriminations and decisions a pilot needs to make." Better graphics and sounds don't matter unless they change the decisions being made or the cues for when to apply a skill. Practical corollary: Skype tutoring transfers better than flipping flash cards for a trip to France.
4. **The Overkill Approach.** Increase the challenge so the skill level you need is *wholly contained inside* the goal you set. De Montebello spoke at middle schools because Toastmasters feedback might be "too soft or congratulatory" — "Middle school students, in contrast, would be merciless. If a joke he said wasn't funny or his delivery was boring or cheesy, he would be able to tell immediately from their faces." Benny Lewis attempts language exams because they're concrete challenges; for German he aimed at the highest level specifically because "his awareness of that goal would push him to study more than he might if he were satisfied with comfortable conversations alone." A friend of Young's decided to exhibit her photography as a way to push her skills. "Deciding in advance that your work will be viewable publicly alters your approach to learning."
   - The fear is temporary: "The first week in each new country in my language learning project was always a shock, but soon it became completely normal to live entirely within the new language."

**Directness is a dial, not a switch.** "In such situations, directness isn't an all-or-nothing feature but something you can gradually increase to improve your performance." Craig couldn't be on *Jeopardy!* hundreds of times, but practicing on actual past clues was far more direct than learning trivia from random topics. Jaiswal couldn't work at an architecture firm, but he could use their software and produce their kind of drawings.

## Key Concepts

- **Vatsal Jaiswal.** Grew up in India, moved to Canada, graduated in architecture into the worst job market since the Great Depression a few years after the 2007 crash. Hundreds of résumés with no reply; escalated to walking into firms unannounced and asking to speak to whoever was in charge; weeks of door-knocking and dozens of unsolicited visits produced not a single callback. Almost nobody in his graduating class had found an architecture job.
- **His diagnosis.** From scraps of feedback he sensed firms didn't see him as useful. His program had focused on **design and theory**, "isolated from the reality of building codes, construction costs, and tricky software." His school portfolio didn't resemble the detailed technical documents architects actually work with, so hiring him implied a lengthy training period few firms could afford.
- **His three moves.** (1) Took a low-paying job at a large-format print shop — the kind that prints architectural blueprints — which paid the bills *and* gave him daily exposure to the blueprints firms were actually using, letting him absorb countless details about how drawings are put together. (2) Taught himself **Revit**, the complex design software he'd noticed those firms using, through online tutorials at night. (3) Built a new portfolio around **a single building of his own design** — a three-tower residential structure with raised courtyards and a modern aesthetic — which pushed his software skills past the tutorials.
- **The result.** He submitted the new portfolio to just two firms. "To his surprise, they both immediately offered him a job."
- **The contrast case.** Another architect Young spoke with tried to improve his employability by deepening his knowledge of **design theories** — interesting, but disconnected from the skills of entry-level work.
- **The everyday failure mode.** "We want to speak a language but try to learn mostly by playing on fun apps, rather than conversing with actual people. We want to work on collaborative, professional programs but mostly code scripts in isolation. We want to become great speakers, so we buy a book on communication, rather than practice presenting."
- **The Duolingo critique.** Young grants it's colorful, fun, and gives a potent sense of progress — "But I suspect that much of the sense of progress is an illusion, at least if your goal is to eventually be able to speak the language." It gives English words and sentences and asks you to pick words from a word bank. "Real speaking situations don't present themselves as a multiple choice. Instead, you have to dredge up the actual words from memory or find alternative words if you haven't learned one of the ones you want to use." Cognitively a different and much harder task. (Footnote: there are ways to get more direct practice from the app, but they tend to require repeatedly practicing the same lessons on mobile.) Young also footnotes that directness is closely related to the psychological concept of **transfer-appropriate processing**.
- **Problem sets over lectures.** During the MIT Challenge, Young recognized that the critical resource wasn't recorded lectures but **problem sets**. Yet students asking him for help usually complain about missing lecture videos and rarely about insufficient problem sets — which suggests they see lectures as the learning and problems as a superficial check. "The principle of directness asserts that it's actually while doing the thing you want to get good at when much of learning takes place."

**Transfer — "education's dirty secret."**

- Transfer has been called the "Holy Grail of education": learning something in one context and using it in another. "Anything less than this is hard to describe as learning at all."
- Robert Haskell, surveying the literature: "Despite the importance of transfer of learning, research findings over the past nine decades clearly show that as individuals, and as educational institutions, we have failed to achieve transfer of learning on any significant level." And: "Without exaggeration, it's an education scandal."
- Haskell's specifics: students who took high school psychology "do no better" in college introductory psychology than students who didn't — "Some students who have taken a psychology course in high school do even worse." In another study, college graduates were asked about economic issues and no difference was found between those who had taken an economics class and those who hadn't.
- Michelene Chi on worked examples: "in almost all the empirical work to date, on the role of example solutions, a student who has studied examples often cannot solve problems that deviate slightly from the example solution."
- Howard Gardner, *The Unschooled Mind*: even "students who receive honors grades in college-level physics courses are frequently unable to solve basic problems and questions encountered in a form slightly different from that on which they have been formally instructed and tested."
- Corporate training fares no better — former Times Mirror Training Group chairman John H. Zenger: "Researchers who rigorously evaluate training have said that demonstrable changes following training are hard to find."
- **Thorndike and Woodworth, 1901**, "The Influence of Improvement in One Mental Function upon the Efficiency of Other Functions," attacked **formal discipline theory** — the idea that the brain is a muscle with general capacities of memory, attention, and reasoning that can be trained irrespective of content. That theory justified universal instruction in Latin and geometry. Thorndike showed transfer is far narrower than assumed. Young notes the theory keeps returning in new clothes: proposals that everyone learn programming or critical thinking to improve general intelligence, and brain-training games. "It's been more than one hundred years since the verdict came in, yet the allure of a general transfer procedure still has many searching for the Holy Grail."
- **But transfer exists.** Wilbert McKeachie: "Transfer is paradoxical. When we want it, we do not get it. Yet it occurs all the time." Every analogy is a transfer; ice skating to Rollerblading is a transfer; "if transfer were really impossible, we would be unable to function." Haskell's explanation is that transfer is harder when knowledge is limited, and becomes flexible with depth. Young adds his own hypothesis: "most formal learning is woefully indirect."

**How directness solves transfer — two ways.**

1. Obviously: learning close to the application "significantly reduces" the need for far transfer.
2. Less obviously: real situations share subtle details with *other* real situations that they never share with a classroom or textbook. "By learning in a real context, one also learns many of the hidden details and skills that are far more likely to transfer to a new real-life situation." Young's example is trivial but telling: one of the most important skills in the no-English project was quickly using a dictionary or translation app mid-conversation to fill a gap — "exactly this kind of practical skill that is rarely covered in a language-learning curriculum."

## Mental Models

- **Cognitive fidelity, not cosmetic fidelity.** Craig's practice would not have improved by matching the show's signature blue background, "because the background color didn't provide any information that would have changed his responses." But taking questions from Trivial Pursuit would have differed in how questions are asked, topics drawn from, and difficulty — and reading random Wikipedia articles would have skipped the fundamental skill of "recalling answers based on cryptic *Jeopardy!*-style clues" entirely.
- **Understanding is also a use context.** Some ultralearners want understanding, not a practical artifact. That doesn't exempt them: Vishal Maini wanted to think and talk intelligently about machine learning well enough to land a non-technical role in a company using it, so communicating clearly to both practitioners and laypeople was the real target — which is why building a mini-course explaining machine learning basics fit so well. "His learning was directly connected with where he wanted to apply the skill: communicating it to others."
- **Depth buys flexibility later.** Haskell's glimmer of hope: brittle knowledge welded to its learning context becomes flexible with more work and time. Dan Everett is Young's example — his linguistic depth makes new languages relatively easy, compared with someone who has learned only a second language or only learned languages academically.
- **The actuaries at the casino.** Young asked friends whether their studies kept them from enjoying gambling; they looked at him blankly. They were actuaries. "Their years spent studying statistics in the classroom should have convinced them that you can't expect to beat the house, yet that connection didn't seem to dawn on them."
- **Build outward from a real kernel.** "Building knowledge outward from the kernel of a real situation is much better than the traditional strategy of learning something and hoping that we'll be able to shift it into a real context at some undetermined future time."

## Anti-patterns

- Deepening theory when the job requires production skills (the second architect).
- Mistaking a gamified sense of progress for the skill itself.
- Treating problem sets as a check on learning rather than as the learning.
- Learning "mastering formulas before you understand the problem they're trying to solve; memorizing the vocabulary of a language because it's written on a list, not because you want to use it; solving highly idealized problems that you'll never see again after graduation."
- Believing programming, critical thinking, or brain training will generally improve your mind — the revived formal discipline theory.
- Choosing indirect methods because direct practice feels uncomfortable, boring, or frustrating.

## Worked Example

**Jaiswal's portfolio project, evaluated on cognitive fidelity.**

The question directness asks is: *which features of the target situation does the practice reproduce?* Jaiswal's target was the daily work of an entry-level architect at the firms he was applying to.

| Feature of the real job | Present in his university portfolio? | Present in his new project? | How he got it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Working in the firms' actual software | No | Yes | Self-taught Revit through online tutorials at night |
| Producing detailed technical documents, not design concepts | No | Yes | Daily exposure to real blueprints at the print shop |
| Codes for representing different materials | No | Yes | Absorbed from the blueprints passing through the shop |
| Knowing what drawings show and omit | No | Yes | Same source |
| Sustained work on one building through to full documentation | No — assorted class projects | Yes — one three-tower residential structure | Designed and drew it himself |
| Being paid by a firm while doing it | — | No | The one feature he could not get |

The one missing feature — actual employment — was the one that was unavailable, and it was the one the project was designed to *obtain*. Everything else he engineered into the practice. Two submissions, two immediate offers.

Note the choice of *forcing function*: he did not choose the print shop as a career step. He chose it as an immersion environment, in the same way de Montebello chose seventh graders and Lewis chose the C2 exam. Low-paying and low-skilled, but "the print store gave him daily exposure to the blueprints firms were using."

## Key Takeaways

- Directness means practicing with a connection to the context where the skill will be used.
- Transfer is the reason: nine decades of research show it largely fails in formal education, so minimize the distance you're asking knowledge to travel.
- Match the **cognitive** features of the real situation — the decisions and cues — not the cosmetic ones.
- Four tactics: projects, immersion, simulation, and overkill.
- Directness is a dial. When perfect directness is impossible, increase it as far as it will go.
- Depth eventually buys transferability, but that's a long-run consolation, not a short-run plan.
- Direct practice alone isn't sufficient — which is why Chapter VII immediately pulls in the opposite direction.
