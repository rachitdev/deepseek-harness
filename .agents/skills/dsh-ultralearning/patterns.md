# Patterns — Ultralearning

Young's reusable moves, grouped by the nine principles plus the project lifecycle that
surrounds them. Each is stated as **When** (the situation that calls for it), **How** (the
move itself, in the book's own terms) and **Trade-offs** (what it costs, and when it
misfires). Young insists these are "directions, not destinations" — principles over
procedures — so treat every "How" as a default to be adapted, not a protocol.

---

## Principle 1 — Metalearning: First Draw a Map

See [chapters/ch04-metalearning.md](chapters/ch04-metalearning.md).

### The Why / What / How research pass

- **When** — before any project of consequence, and again whenever the project stalls.
  Obstacles usually aren't visible from outside, so this is not a one-time gate.
- **How** — answer three questions in order. *Why* are you learning this: instrumental
  (a non-learning result) or intrinsic (the subject is the end)? *What* must you learn:
  three columns on one sheet — **Concepts** (must be understood), **Facts** (must be
  memorized), **Procedures** (must be practiced) — then underline the hardest entries in
  each column; those are your predicted bottlenecks. *How* will you learn it: **Benchmark**
  an existing curriculum (a university course sequence, what successful learners did), then
  **Emphasize/Exclude** — add weight where your goal demands it, cut what a curriculum
  designer cared about and you don't.
- **Trade-offs** — the first list is rough and will be wrong in places; Young says it
  "needn't be complete or accurate." Benchmarking imports someone else's priorities along
  with their structure, which is exactly why Emphasize/Exclude is a mandatory second step.
  Research can also become a comfortable substitute for the frustrating part.

### The 10 Percent Rule

- **When** — deciding how much research is enough.
- **How** — budget roughly 10% of expected total learning time for research. 100 hours of
  learning ⇒ ~10 hours of research (about two weeks at a normal pace). Scale toward 5% on
  500–1,000-hour projects. Then apply the marginal test: stop when an extra hour of
  research buys less than an extra hour of learning.
- **Trade-offs** — the number is a heuristic, not a measurement. Under-researching means
  learning from whatever resource happened to be nearest; over-researching is
  procrastination wearing a productive costume.

### The Expert Interview Method

- **When** — the project is instrumental, and you're about to spend serious time or money
  on the assumption that learning X produces outcome Y.
- **How** — find people who have already achieved the outcome and ask them what mattered.
  Their answers tell you whether the skill is actually the mechanism, and which parts of it
  carry the weight.
- **Trade-offs** — Young's caution: don't ask for ongoing mentorship in the first email.
  Ask a narrow, answerable question. Skipping this step is how someone spends two years and
  tens of thousands in debt on a credential that buys little.

## Principle 2 — Focus: Sharpen Your Knife

See [chapters/ch05-focus.md](chapters/ch05-focus.md).

### The three-problem escalation ladder

- **When** — you can't concentrate, and you need to know which problem you actually have.
- **How** — diagnose in order and fix the earliest one first. **Starting** (procrastination):
  recognize it, then use the smallest crutch that works — the **five-minute rule** (commit
  to five minutes only), then the **Pomodoro Technique** (25 on / 5 off), then **calendar
  blocks**. **Sustaining** (distraction): remove environmental sources, note and release
  intrusive emotions rather than suppressing them, and pick session lengths you can hold.
  **Shaping** (arousal mismatch): match environment to task type.
- **Trade-offs** — "Don't ever feel bad about backing up a stage." Jumping to a demanding
  system while you still can't start at all guarantees the system gets abandoned. Crutches
  are unnecessary for intrinsically interesting tasks — don't add ceremony you don't need.

### Arousal matching

- **When** — choosing where and how to study a particular kind of task.
- **How** — high arousal narrows and sharpens focus: good for simple, single-target work
  (a coffee shop is fine). Lower arousal broadens it: better for complex work with many
  inputs (a quiet room). For a stuck creative problem, briefly remove focus altogether —
  take a break and let ideas conjoin.
- **Trade-offs** — this is a dial, not a rule, and background media is the common
  self-deception: test it rather than rationalizing it.

### Focus is trainable, not innate

- **When** — you've concluded you're "just not a focused person."
- **How** — Mary Somerville is the counter-case: her accomplishment was focus *under
  interruption*, rebuilt repeatedly, not focus in ideal conditions. Focus is not a general
  trait, but the procedure for producing it generalizes.
- **Trade-offs** — don't chase flow. Hard learning frequently doesn't produce it, and
  treating its absence as failure is a reliable way to quit.

## Principle 3 — Directness: Go Straight Ahead

See [chapters/ch06-directness.md](chapters/ch06-directness.md).

### The four directness tactics

- **When** — the skill you're building has a real context of use, and your current practice
  doesn't resemble it.
- **How** — pick by availability, in roughly this order:
  1. **Project-Based Learning** — when the skill produces artifacts. Build a game to learn
     programming; write a thesis paper for military history.
  2. **Immersive Learning** — when an environment exists where the skill is used constantly.
     Live in the language; join an open-source project.
  3. **The Flight Simulator Method** — when real practice is illegal, unsafe, or
     unavailable. Preserve the *cognitive* features (the decisions and cues), not the
     cosmetic ones.
  4. **The Overkill Approach** — when ordinary feedback is too soft, or you need forcing
     motivation. Aim at a standard far above what you need: the C2 German exam, a public
     exhibition, a hostile audience.
- **Trade-offs** — directness is a dial, not a switch, and it trades against comfort and
  often against convenience. Also against drill: direct practice alone cannot reach some
  bottlenecks (see Direct-Then-Drill below). Depth still buys flexibility later — Young
  does not argue against understanding, only against understanding pursued *instead of* use.

### Build outward from a real kernel

- **When** — the full real task is out of reach today.
- **How** — start from the smallest genuine version of the real thing and expand, rather
  than starting from an abstraction and hoping it will transfer.
- **Trade-offs** — the kernel may be embarrassing or trivially small. That is preferable to
  the transfer failure Robert Haskell documented across nine decades of research —
  knowledge stays "welded to a place or subject matter."

## Principle 4 — Drill: Attack Your Weakest Point

See [chapters/ch07-drill.md](chapters/ch07-drill.md).

### The Direct-Then-Drill Approach

- **When** — always, as the governing cycle; drills without it drift into schoolwork.
- **How** — (1) practice the whole skill directly; (2) analyze it, isolate the
  **rate-determining step**, and drill that alone; (3) return to direct practice to
  integrate — which also tests whether the drill was well designed.
- **Trade-offs** — the three standing difficulties of drilling are knowing *what* to drill,
  being *willing* to drill (it is strenuous by design), and *designing* the drill so it
  removes context without removing the actual difficulty. Cycle fast early; longer drill
  detours are affordable near mastery.

### The five drill designs

- **When** — you have identified a bottleneck and need a shape for the drill.
- **How** — choose by what you need to isolate:
  | Design | Isolates |
  |---|---|
  | **Time Slicing** | A moment in a sequence (hard bars of music, layups, key phrases) |
  | **Cognitive Components** | One simultaneous cognitive aspect (Mandarin tones alone) |
  | **The Copycat** | Everything *except* the target component (drawing from drawings) |
  | **Magnifying Glass** | Nothing — but overweights one component (10× research time while still writing whole articles) |
  | **Prerequisite Chaining** | Discovered gaps, in order of need (color theory after starting to paint) |
- **Trade-offs** — Magnifying Glass keeps context at the cost of a weaker isolation;
  Cognitive Components isolates hardest but can strip the cues you'll need back in context.
  Prerequisite Chaining deliberately inverts the "learn fundamentals first" ordering, so it
  is uncomfortable and occasionally inefficient — Young's argument is that it discovers the
  *real* prerequisites rather than the assumed ones.

## Principle 5 — Retrieval: Test to Learn

See [chapters/ch08-retrieval.md](chapters/ch08-retrieval.md).

### Close the book

- **When** — any time you are about to review.
- **How** — **Closed-Book Learning** is the general-purpose move: take any existing
  activity and cut off the source. Retrieval *creates* knowledge; it does not merely check
  it (Karpicke and Blunt's testing effect; the forward-testing effect means testing also
  improves subsequent learning). Test *before* you feel ready — even taking the final exam
  before the class begins has value.
- **Trade-offs** — it is unpleasant and it feels less productive than rereading, because
  fluency of processing is a liar: passive review wins minutes after studying and loses
  badly days later. Judgments of learning are systematically wrong here.

### The five retrieval tactics

- **When** — you need a specific format for retrieval practice.
- **How** —
  | Tactic | Best for | Weakness |
  |---|---|---|
  | **Flash cards / SRS** | Vocabulary, maps, diagrams, definitions, equations, syntax | Fails when the retrieval cue is highly variable |
  | **Free recall** (blank page after reading) | Books, lectures, journal articles | Hard; you will miss a lot — which is the point |
  | **The Question-Book Method** | Note-taking that generates future practice | Drifts to trivia unless limited to one big-idea question per section |
  | **Self-Generated Challenges** | Skills rather than facts | You must build and revisit the list |
  | **Closed-Book Learning** | Upgrading anything | None named; it is the default move |
- **Trade-offs** — the flash-card boundary is the important one: cards suit
  question-with-one-answer knowledge. Knowledge that must be applied in variable situations
  needs a different tactic. And search engines do not substitute for knowledge in your head.

## Principle 6 — Feedback: Don't Dodge the Punches

See [chapters/ch09-feedback.md](chapters/ch09-feedback.md).

### Grade the feedback you're getting

- **When** — you're improving slowly and don't know why.
- **How** — identify which of three types you have. **Outcome** (how you did overall) →
  **informational** (what you're doing wrong) → **corrective** (how to fix it). Upgrade one
  step where you can.
- **Trade-offs** — **do not fake an upgrade.** If the assessment is genuinely holistic,
  forcing per-element specificity produces guesses. And a non-expert's suggested fix is not
  corrective feedback, however confidently offered.

### The four feedback tactics

- **When** — the feedback exists but isn't working.
- **How** —
  1. **Noise Cancellation** — every stream mixes signal with randomness. Build **proxy
     signals** that are less noisy than the headline metric (percentage of readers who
     reach the end of an article, rather than raw traffic).
  2. **Hitting the Difficulty Sweet Spot** — information is unpredictability. Adjust the
     environment so you cannot predict success or failure. Failing too often ⇒ simplify
     until you can notice what works. Succeeding too often ⇒ harden the task or tighten
     standards. "Avoid situations that always make you feel good (or bad)."
  3. **Metafeedback** — feedback on your *strategy*, measured as **learning rate** (Elo
     growth, mock-exam improvement, vocabulary acquired). Use it to decide when to persist
     and when to experiment, and to compare two study methods head to head.
  4. **High-Intensity, Rapid Feedback** — simply far more, far sooner. Chris Rock testing
     material in small clubs; de Montebello's stage volume; Lewis speaking on day one.
- **Trade-offs** — Kluger and DeNisi found feedback was *negative* in over 38% of cases,
  usually when aimed at the ego rather than the task, so more is not automatically better.
  On timing, Young recommends generally faster feedback with one serious caveat: "Feedback
  too soon may turn your retrieval practice effectively into passive review." His fix is a
  timer forcing a real attempt before you look.

## Principle 7 — Retention: Don't Fill a Leaky Bucket

See [chapters/ch10-retention.md](chapters/ch10-retention.md).

### Pick the mechanism that fits the material

- **When** — you are forgetting faster than you are learning.
- **How** — there are two retention problems (decay over time, and fragility of declarative
  knowledge) and four mechanisms:
  1. **Spacing** — never cram; distribute. SRS for dense factual loads.
  2. **Proceduralization** — automated skills barely decay. Choose a *core* set and repeat
     it far past evenness until it runs without attention.
  3. **Overlearning** — practice past adequacy, or move up a level (calculus students
     retained algebra better than algebra students did).
  4. **Mnemonics** — for dense, arbitrary, specific-format information; pair with SRS.
- **Trade-offs** — Young's rule is **system fit beats system optimality**: a retention
  system elaborate enough to be abandoned retains nothing. Spacing intervals that are too
  long lose the material entirely. Proceduralization only partially applies to most skills,
  and Young flags his own heuristics here as partly speculative. Overlearning's evidence
  base shows only a week or two of extra recall in standard studies. Mnemonics have a big
  up-front cost and recall too slowly to be fluent — "a bridge, not a foundation."
  And do not assume professional use maintains the underlying knowledge: the doctors study
  says it does not.

## Principle 8 — Intuition: Dig Deep Before Building Up

See [chapters/ch11-intuition.md](chapters/ch11-intuition.md).

### The Feynman Technique

- **When** — you can't tell whether you understand something, or you've hit the illusion of
  explanatory depth (Rebecca Lawson's bicycle-drawing result).
- **How** — write the concept at the top of a page and explain it as if to someone who has
  never heard of it; go back to the source wherever you get stuck. Three applications:
  (1) for things you don't understand at all — book in hand, going back and forth;
  (2) for problems you can't solve — walk the solution *step by step*, explaining why each
  step makes sense; (3) for expanding intuition on something you can already use — force
  examples, analogies, and visualizations.
- **Trade-offs** — application 2 fails if you summarize instead of walking, because
  summarizing skips exactly the difficulty you needed to hit. The technique is slow, and it
  is deliberately humbling.

### The four intuition rules

- **When** — building depth rather than coverage.
- **How** — (1) **Don't give up on hard problems easily** — the *struggle timer*: when you
  want to quit, set ten more minutes. Even failing after real struggle primes memory for the
  solution when you see it. (2) **Prove things to understand them** — rebuild the result
  yourself. (3) **Always start with a concrete example** — Feynman spotting errors in live
  talks by running a concrete case against each new condition. (4) **Don't fool yourself** —
  ask the "dumb" question rather than nodding along, as Feynman's Brazilian students would
  not.
- **Trade-offs** — this is the slowest principle. Expert chess players' advantage rests on
  something like 50,000 stored chunks; experts sort physics problems by deep principle while
  novices sort by surface features, and that library is built by time. The alternative —
  memorizing procedures — is faster and produces *brittle* learning that fails outside the
  textbook.

## Principle 9 — Experimentation: Explore Outside Your Comfort Zone

See [chapters/ch12-experimentation.md](chapters/ch12-experimentation.md).

### Experiment at the level your problem lives on

- **When** — your learning rate is flattening, or you've advanced far enough that
  established methods stop distinguishing you.
- **How** — three levels. **Resources** ("how can I learn this?"), **Technique** ("what
  should I learn next?"), **Style** ("who should I be?"). Van Gogh worked all three: the
  Bargue and Cassagne courses; reed pen, charcoal, chalk, watercolor, Conté crayon; then
  Millet, Japanese woodblock prints, Gauguin, and the shift from grey tones to complementary
  colors.
- **Trade-offs** — experimentation prunes as well as generates, so it costs time you could
  have spent producing. Random activity is not experimentation: "experimenting requires
  understanding what learning problems you're facing and coming up with possible ways to
  resolve them."

### The five experimentation tactics

- **When** — you need a concrete way to vary something.
- **How** —
  | Tactic | Core move |
  |---|---|
  | **Copy, Then Create** | Emulate to get a foothold, then deconstruct why it works |
  | **Compare Methods Side-By-Side** | Vary one condition and measure (Young's French mnemonics split test) |
  | **Introduce New Constraints** | Make the old method impossible |
  | **Find Your Hybrid of Unrelated Skills** | Combine non-overlapping skills (Scott Adams: engineer + MBA + cartoonist) |
  | **Explore the Extremes** | Push a dimension far out, then pull back |
- **Trade-offs** — Explore the Extremes is justified by the high-dimensional geometry
  argument (in ten dimensions, nearly three-quarters of a hypersphere's mass lies in the
  outer tenth of the radius: playing safe on every dimension leaves almost all the space
  unvisited), but extremes are also where you produce unusable work. Copy, Then Create
  stops paying once the exemplar's skill profile no longer resembles yours.

## The project lifecycle

See [chapters/ch13-your-first-ultralearning-project.md](chapters/ch13-your-first-ultralearning-project.md).

### Run the five-step pipeline

- **When** — starting a project you intend to finish.
- **How** — **1. Research** (scope, primary resources, benchmark, direct practice
  activities, backup drills) → **2. Schedule** (how much time, when, how long per chunk,
  entered in the calendar; a **pilot week** if the project runs six months or more) →
  **3. Execute** (audit continuously against all nine principles) → **4. Review** (what went
  right, what went wrong, what to change — for successes as well as failures) →
  **5. Decide** (maintenance, relearning, or mastery).
- **Trade-offs** — the calendar entry is deliberately a commitment test: a schedule you
  ignore is information, not a failure of willpower. "Conception failures masquerade as
  willpower failures." Warm-up time should determine chunk size, so short chunks are wasteful
  for slow-to-start work.

### Choose ultralearning only when it's the right tool

- **When** — before committing, and honestly.
- **How** — three-way choice.
  | Situation | Strategy |
  |---|---|
  | Learning is accumulation, spontaneous, low-frustration, self-rewarding | **Low-intensity habit** |
  | Improvement requires *unlearning* (pronunciation, stale programming habits) | **Ultralearning** |
  | High frustration barriers block any casual practice | **Ultralearning** |
  | Credentials legally required or strongly recommended | **Formal education** |
  | Apprenticeship studio, team projects, or research-community immersion needed | **Formal education** |
- **Trade-offs** — self-direction still applies in all three cases; formal education is a
  resource you direct, not an abdication. Ultralearning is intense by definition, so its cost
  is real and choosing it where a habit would do is a genuine mistake.

### Decide what happens after the project

- **When** — at the end, before the skill starts decaying.
- **How** — **Maintenance** (a recurring, decaying habit — Young ran 30 min/week per
  language for a year, then 30 min/month), **Relearning** (accept the loss, plan a refresher
  when the need is foreseeable), or **Mastery** (another project, or sustained lighter
  practice).
- **Trade-offs** — relearning is cheapest but risks losing the *recognition* of when the
  skill applies, which is the part refreshers don't restore. Maintenance is a permanent tax.

### Foster it in someone else

- **When** — teaching, parenting, or building a team's learning culture. See
  [chapters/ch14-an-unconventional-education.md](chapters/ch14-an-unconventional-education.md).
- **How** — Laszlo Polgar's five steps (early start, specialize, practice as play, positive
  reinforcement, never coerce — and teach self-education because you will be outgrown), and
  Young's three suggestions: an inspiring goal, careful competition (compete where the
  learner has aptitude; make it unique where they're behind), and making learning a genuine
  priority.
- **Trade-offs** — Young lists the Polgar experiment's weaknesses himself: no control group,
  no randomization, no blinding, and the three girls were not identical. Confidence is
  upstream of practice intensity, which is why reference-group management matters more than
  it sounds — but it is also why the method is hard to transplant.
