# Cheatsheet — Ultralearning

Dense reference. Numbers and names are Young's. Chapters in roman numerals as the book uses.

## 1. The nine principles at a glance

| # | Principle | One-line rule | Chapter |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Metalearning** — First Draw a Map | Learn how the subject is learned, before learning it | [IV](chapters/ch04-metalearning.md) |
| 2 | **Focus** — Sharpen Your Knife | Solve starting, sustaining, shaping — in that order | [V](chapters/ch05-focus.md) |
| 3 | **Directness** — Go Straight Ahead | Practice in the context where the skill will be used | [VI](chapters/ch06-directness.md) |
| 4 | **Drill** — Attack Your Weakest Point | Isolate and hammer the rate-determining step | [VII](chapters/ch07-drill.md) |
| 5 | **Retrieval** — Test to Learn | Close the book; recall creates knowledge | [VIII](chapters/ch08-retrieval.md) |
| 6 | **Feedback** — Don't Dodge the Punches | Take it early and hard; filter signal from noise | [IX](chapters/ch09-feedback.md) |
| 7 | **Retention** — Don't Fill a Leaky Bucket | Space, proceduralize, overlearn, or mnemonic — by fit | [X](chapters/ch10-retention.md) |
| 8 | **Intuition** — Dig Deep Before Building Up | Build the library; don't memorize shortcuts | [XI](chapters/ch11-intuition.md) |
| 9 | **Experimentation** — Explore Outside Your Comfort Zone | Vary deliberately; the principle that ties the rest together | [XII](chapters/ch12-experimentation.md) |

**Definition:** "A strategy for acquiring skills and knowledge that is both self-directed
and intense." *Strategy* (not a commandment) · *self-directed* (who drives, not where) ·
*intense* (optimized for effectiveness, not comfort).

## 3. Selection matrices

### 3a. Should I ultralearn at all? (Ch XIII)

| Situation | Strategy |
|---|---|
| Accumulation, spontaneous, low-frustration, self-rewarding | Low-intensity habit |
| Improvement requires **unlearning** (pronunciation, stale coding habits) | **Ultralearning** |
| Frustration barriers make casual practice impossible | **Ultralearning** |
| Credential legally required or strongly recommended | Formal education |
| Apprenticeship studio, team project, or research-community immersion needed | Formal education |
| Any of the above | Self-direction still applies — treat the institution as a resource |

### 3b. Which column does this material belong in? (Ch IV "What?" worksheet)

| Column | Test | Examples | Countermeasure |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Concepts** | Must be *understood* flexibly | Math, physics; legal principles; derivatives | Explain them to other people (Feynman Technique) |
| **Facts** | Recall suffices | Vocabulary, pronunciation rules, trig identities | Spaced-repetition software |
| **Procedures** | Must be *practiced*, possibly unconsciously | Riding a bike; pronunciation | Repetition and drill |

Underline the hardest item in each column — that is your predicted bottleneck.

### 3c. Which directness tactic? (Ch VI)

| Use when | Tactic | Example |
|---|---|---|
| The skill produces artifacts | **Project-Based Learning** | Build a game; write a thesis |
| An environment exists where the skill is constant | **Immersive Learning** | Live in the language; open source |
| Real practice illegal / unsafe / unavailable | **Flight Simulator Method** | Simulators; Skype tutoring over flash cards |
| Feedback too soft, or you need forcing motivation | **Overkill Approach** | C2 German exam; public exhibit; middle-school audience |

**Indirect → direct swaps:** word-bank app → conversation from day one · design theory
reading → the firm's software and a firm-style portfolio · random Wikipedia → archived
*Jeopardy!* clues · watching lectures → doing problem sets · communication book → speaking
to real audiences, repeatedly.

### 3d. Which drill design? (Ch VII)

| Isolate… | Design | Example |
|---|---|---|
| A moment in a sequence | **Time Slicing** | Hard bars of music; layups; key phrases |
| One simultaneous cognitive aspect | **Cognitive Components** | Mandarin tone drills with a recording |
| Everything except the target | **The Copycat** | Drawing from other people's drawings |
| Nothing, but overweight one part | **Magnifying Glass** | 10× research time, still writing whole articles |
| Discovered gaps, in order of need | **Prerequisite Chaining** | Color theory after starting; grammar after phrases |

**Likely rate-determining steps:** mathematics → arithmetic/algebra fluency · foreign
language → vocabulary size (or pronunciation if words are known) · accounting → Excel depth
· writing → whichever of research, ordering, vocabulary, or style is weakest.

### 3e. Which retrieval tactic? (Ch VIII)

| Tactic | Best for | Fails when |
|---|---|---|
| **Flash cards / SRS** | Vocabulary, maps, diagrams, definitions, equations, syntax | The retrieval cue is highly variable |
| **Free recall** (blank page) | Books, lectures, journal articles | — (it's meant to be hard) |
| **Question-Book Method** | Notes that generate future practice | It drifts to trivia; cap at one big-idea question per section |
| **Self-Generated Challenges** | Skills, not facts | You never revisit the list |
| **Closed-Book Learning** | Upgrading any existing activity | — (general-purpose default) |

### 3f. Which retention mechanism? (Ch X)

| Mechanism | Fixes | Best fit | Limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Spacing** | Decay over intervals | Everything; dense factual loads via SRS | Gaps too long lose the material |
| **Proceduralization** | Declarative fragility | A chosen *core* set, repeated past evenness | Most skills only partially proceduralize |
| **Overlearning** | Durability after adequacy | Rare-but-critical skills; moving up a level | Studies show only ~1–2 weeks extra recall |
| **Mnemonics** | Dense, arbitrary, format-specific info | Vocabulary, terminology, weeks-to-months | Slow recall; big setup cost; a bridge, not a foundation |

**Rule:** system *fit* beats system *optimality*. A system you abandon retains nothing.

### 3g. Which Feynman Technique application? (Ch XI)

| Situation | Application | Young's MIT Challenge case |
|---|---|---|
| You don't understand it at all | Book in hand, back and forth | Photogrammetry (machine vision) |
| You can't solve the problem type | Walk the solution step by step, explaining *why* | Grid acceleration (computer graphics) |
| You can use it but have no feel for it | Examples, analogies, visualizations | Voltage (electromagnetism) |

### 3h. Which level of experimentation? (Ch XII)

| Level | Question | Van Gogh |
|---|---|---|
| **Resources** | How can I learn this? | Bargue and Cassagne courses; sketching from life |
| **Technique** | What should I learn next? | Reed pen and ink, charcoal, chalk, watercolor, Conté |
| **Style** | Who should I be? | Millet, Japanese woodblocks, Gauguin; grey → complementary colors |

## 4. Tells and smells — symptom → diagnosis → fix

### Focus (Ch V)

| Symptom | Diagnosis | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Never sit down at all | Starting problem | Five-minute rule; back up a stage without guilt |
| Sit down, break constantly | Sustaining problem | Pomodoro; remove environmental distraction sources |
| Eyes move, mind elsewhere ("reading in hypnosis") | Sustaining, cognitive | Switch to a task requiring output; shorten the block |
| Intrusive worry mid-session | Emotional distraction | Note and release it — suppression makes it worse |
| Complex work in a noisy café going badly | Arousal too high for the task | Quiet room for many-input work |
| Simple drilling feels flat and slow | Arousal too low | Noisier environment is fine, even helpful |
| Guilt that learning doesn't feel like flow | Chasing flow | Stop chasing it; hard learning often lacks it |

### Learning not sticking / not transferring

| Symptom | Diagnosis | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Reviewing feels smooth, tests go badly | Fluency illusion; judgments of learning lie | Closed-book retrieval; test before you feel ready (Ch VIII) |
| Recognize the material but can't produce it | Recognition ≠ recall | Free recall on a blank page (Ch VIII) |
| Can do textbook problems, fails outside them | Brittle learning; sorted by surface features | Feynman Technique app. 2/3; concrete examples (Ch XI) |
| Course finished, skill unusable at work | Transfer failure — knowledge welded to the classroom | Direct practice; match *cognitive* fidelity (Ch VI) |
| App streak long, can't hold a conversation | Gamified progress ≠ the skill | Immersion or conversation from day one (Ch VI) |
| Knew it last month, gone now | Decay | Spacing / SRS; a maintenance habit (Ch X) |
| Used it professionally for years, underlying knowledge gone | The doctors-study effect | Deliberate refreshers; don't assume use maintains it (Ch X) |
| Nodding along while reading | Illusion of explanatory depth | Write it at the top of a page and explain it cold (Ch XI) |

### Progress stalled

| Symptom | Diagnosis | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Plateau despite continued use | No feedback at all — stagnation | Add any outcome feedback: a blog, an audience, a score |
| Know you're wrong, not why | Only outcome feedback | Find per-element signal, or an expert |
| Know what's wrong, can't fix it | Only informational feedback | Coach, mentor, tutor, or solution keys |
| Wildly inconsistent reactions | Not yet good enough for signal to emerge | Keep iterating; consistency is the tell |
| Always succeeding | Difficulty too low — no information | Raise difficulty or tighten standards |
| Always failing | Difficulty too high | Simplify until you can notice what works |
| Wide swings from one data point | Noise | Build a proxy signal |
| Learning rate collapsing | Method exhausted | Metafeedback → experiment (Ch IX → XII) |
| More hours of practice, no movement | Bottleneck direct practice can't reach | Direct-Then-Drill; isolate the rate-determining step (Ch VII) |
| Drills going well, whole skill unchanged | Never returned to direct practice | Close the cycle; re-check the drill's design (Ch VII) |
| Advanced, methods no longer distinguishing you | Need experimentation, not more of the same | Resources → Technique → Style (Ch XII) |

### Project-level

| Symptom | Diagnosis | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Still researching weeks in | Research as procrastination | Marginal test: another hour of research vs. another hour of learning (Ch IV) |
| Schedule made and ignored | Conception failure masquerading as willpower | Treat the ignored calendar as data; re-scope (Ch XIII) |
| Two years and a credential that bought nothing | Instrumental goal never verified | Expert Interview Method *before* committing (Ch IV) |
| Curriculum covers things you don't need | Someone else's priorities | Benchmark, then Emphasize/Exclude (Ch IV) |
| Sessions too short to get anywhere | Warm-up time exceeds the chunk | Longer chunks — warm-up determines chunk size (Ch XIII) |
| Skill decaying after the project | No post-project decision made | Choose maintenance / relearning / mastery (Ch XIII) |

## 5. The project pipeline template (Ch XIII)

| Step | Produce |
|---|---|
| **1. Research** | Scope · primary resources · benchmark curriculum · direct practice activities · backup materials and drills |
| **2. Schedule** | Total hours · which days · block length · entered in the calendar · pilot week if ≥6 months |
| **3. Execute** | Run it, auditing continuously against all nine principles; adjust mid-course |
| **4. Review** | What went right · what went wrong · what to change — for successes as well as failures |
| **5. Decide** | Maintenance · Relearning · Mastery |

**Post-project decision:**

| Option | Use when | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| **Maintenance** | You want the skill continuously available | A recurring, decaying habit (30 min/wk → 30 min/mo) |
| **Relearning** | Used infrequently; the need is foreseeable | Refresher time later; risk losing recognition of when it applies |
| **Mastery** | The project exposed avenues worth pursuing | Another project, or sustained lighter practice |

## 6. Nine-principle project audit

Run down this list whenever a project is going badly.

1. **Metalearning** — Do I have a map, or am I using whatever resource was nearest?
2. **Focus** — Which of the three focus problems do I actually have right now?
3. **Directness** — Does what I'm doing resemble the situation I want to perform in?
4. **Drill** — Do I know my rate-determining step, and am I attacking it or avoiding it?
5. **Retrieval** — Is the book closed?
6. **Feedback** — What grade of feedback am I getting, and is difficulty in the sweet spot?
7. **Retention** — Which mechanism fits this material, and am I actually running it?
8. **Intuition** — Can I explain this cold, from a blank page?
9. **Experimentation** — Has my learning rate flattened, and have I varied anything?

## 8. Anti-pattern quick list

Comfort-seeking is the common thread: nearly every anti-pattern below is choosing the
version of the activity that feels better.

- Researching indefinitely because starting is uncomfortable (IV)
- Assuming a credential is the mechanism, without asking anyone who has the outcome (IV)
- Adopting a demanding focus system while still failing to start at all (V)
- Chasing flow, and feeling guilty when hard learning doesn't produce it (V)
- Choosing an indirect method because direct practice is boring or frightening (VI)
- Believing programming or brain training generally improves the mind (VI)
- Drilling what you're already good at (VII)
- Staying in drills and never reintegrating (VII)
- Rereading notes and calling it studying (VIII)
- Waiting until you feel ready to test yourself (VIII)
- Peeking at the answer before a real attempt — turning retrieval into review (VIII, IX)
- Praise-seeking; building an environment that reliably feels good (IX)
- Forcing element-level critique out of a genuinely holistic assessment (IX)
- Changing your whole approach on one data point (IX)
- Cramming; or building a retention system so elaborate you abandon it (X)
- Basing a strategy on mnemonics (X)
- Nodding along while reading; summarizing instead of walking a problem (XI)
- Avoiding "dumb" questions to look knowledgeable (XI)
- Random activity mistaken for experimentation (XII)
- Assuming your learning style is fixed (XII)

## 9. Caveats Young states himself

- The nine are **"directions, not destinations"** — principles, not procedures.
- Case studies are exemplars, "not a guarantee that you can achieve an identical result
  with identical effort." Natural ability exists and matters (the "Terence Tao problem").
- Proceduralization heuristics, long-term overlearning effects, and the ultralearning
  workplace are labelled speculation in the source.
- The Polgar experiment has no control group, no randomization, and no blinding.
