# Glossary — Ultralearning (Scott H. Young, 2019)

**10 Percent Rule** — Invest roughly 10 percent of expected total learning time in research before starting; scale down toward 5 percent for 500–1,000-hour projects (Ch IV).

**Advanced practice** — Overlearning by moving one level above a skill so its components are overpracticed in service of a harder domain; algebra students who took calculus forgot far less (Ch X).

**Anki** — Open-source spaced-repetition software, "the preferred tool of more extreme ultralearners" (Ch X).

**Arousal** — General alertness from sympathetic nervous system activation; high arousal narrows focus and suits simple tasks, lower arousal broadens it and suits complex ones (Ch V).

**Autor, David** — MIT economist whose work on skill polarization underpins the "average is over" argument (Ch II).

**Average Is Over** — Tyler Cowen's book and thesis that top performers increasingly outperform the rest (Ch II).

**B2 / C2** — CEFR levels. B2 is upper intermediate, Benny Lewis's usual three-month target; C2 is complete mastery, the level his German challenge aimed at (Ch I).

**Barone, Eric** — Solo developer of *Stardew Valley* (February 2016), who learned programming, pixel art, music composition, sound design, and story writing over ~5 years; 3M+ copies in year one (Ch I).

**Bargue, Charles** — Author of *Exercices au fusain* and *Cours de dessin*, home-study drawing courses van Gogh worked through obsessively (Ch XII).

**Benchmarking** — Metalearning tactic: find how people normally learn the skill (curricula, syllabi, forums) to design a default starting strategy (Ch IV).

**Bjork, R. A.** — Psychologist whose concept of desirable difficulty explains why harder retrieval produces better learning (Ch VIII).

**Blunt, Janell** — Co-author with Jeffrey Karpicke of the studies showing free recall beats review and concept mapping (Ch VIII).

**Brookhart, Susan M.** — Co-author, with Maria Araceli Ruiz-Primo, of the definition of optimal feedback (Ch IX).

**Carr, George Shoobridge** — Author of *A Synopsis of Elementary Results in Pure and Applied Mathematics*, the theorem list without proofs that Ramanujan mined (Ch VIII).

**Cassagne, Armand** — Author of *Guide de l'alphabet du dessin*, another home-study course used by van Gogh (Ch XII).

**Chi, Michelene** — Cognitive science researcher who found students who study example solutions "often cannot solve problems that deviate slightly" from them (Ch VI).

**Chunks (chess)** — Recognizable multi-piece patterns stored in long-term memory; researchers estimate ~50,000 are needed for expert status (Ch XI).

**Cirillo, Francesco** — Italian management consultant who created the Pomodoro Technique (Ch V).

**Closed-Book Learning** — Retrieval tactic: cut off the ability to look things up in any learning activity, converting it into retrieval practice (Ch VIII).

**Cognitive components (drill)** — Drill type that isolates one simultaneously-applied cognitive aspect, e.g. Mandarin tone drills without meaning or grammar (Ch VII).

**Cognitive features** — The decisions and cues in a task, which are what must be matched for transfer; cosmetic features need not be (Ch VI).

**Concepts** — Metalearning column for things that must be *understood* flexibly, not merely memorized (Ch IV).

**Copy, Then Create** — Experimentation tactic: emulate an example to get a foothold and to deconstruct why it works, then venture out (Ch XII).

**Copycat drill** — Drill type in which you copy the parts of a skill you don't want to practice so you can focus exclusively on the one you do (Ch VII).

**Core practice** — Overlearning method: continually practicing and refining the core elements of a skill, often paired with immersion (Ch X).

**Corrective feedback** — The best grade of feedback: shows both what is wrong and how to fix it; usually requires a coach, mentor, teacher, or solution keys (Ch IX).

**Cowen, Tyler** — Economist whose "average is over" argument frames the economic case for ultralearning (Ch II).

**Craig, Roger** — Computer scientist who mined the entire *Jeopardy!* clue archive, mapped his weaknesses with text mining and data visualization, used SRS, and set the single-day ($77,000) and five-game (~$200,000) records (Ch I).

**Csikszentmihalyi, Mihaly** — Psychologist who pioneered the concept of flow (Ch V).

**de Montebello, Tristan** — Musician and web consultant who went from his first Toastmasters meeting to the top ten of the World Championship of Public Speaking in under seven months, then co-founded UltraSpeaking (Ch III).

**Decay** — Theory of forgetting: memories simply erode with time; incomplete on its own (Ch X).

**Declarative knowledge** — "Knowing that" — explicit facts and propositions, more susceptible to forgetting than procedural knowledge (Ch X).

**DeNisi, Angelo** — Co-author with Avraham Kluger of the feedback meta-analysis showing negative effects in over 38 percent of cases (Ch IX).

**Desirable difficulty** — Bjork's concept: more difficult retrieval produces better learning, provided the retrieval succeeds (Ch VIII).

**Direct-Then-Drill Approach** — The cycle that reconciles Principles 3 and 4: practice directly, isolate and drill the bottleneck, then return to direct practice to integrate and validate (Ch VII).

**Directness** — Principle 3: learning tied closely to the situation or context in which the skill will be used (Ch VI).

**Drill** — Principle 4: isolate and attack the rate-determining component of a complex skill (Ch VII).

**Dunning-Kruger effect** — Inadequate understanding paired with inflated confidence, because lacking knowledge also removes the ability to assess it (Ch XI).

**Duolingo** — Language app Young critiques for word-bank translation, which is cognitively unlike speaking (Ch VI); also cited as hiding a spacing algorithm (Ch X).

**Dweck, Carol** — Stanford psychologist behind the fixed/growth mindset distinction, which Young extends into the experimental mindset (Ch XII).

**Ebbinghaus, Hermann** — Ran one of the first psychological experiments, memorizing nonsense syllables for years, and discovered the forgetting curve (Ch X).

**Emphasize/Exclude Method** — Metalearning tactic: modify a benchmarked curriculum by emphasizing what serves your goal and omitting or delaying what doesn't (Ch IV).

**Ericsson, K. Anders** — Psychologist behind deliberate practice; argues immediate feedback is essential to expertise, that flow is inconsistent with deliberate practice, and prefers Long-Term Working Memory to the chunking model (Ch II, V, IX, XI).

**Erdős, Paul** — Mathematician cited as an extreme case of pursuing focus (via amphetamines) (Ch V).

**Everett, Dan** — Linguist whose monolingual fieldwork demonstration decodes an unknown language in half an hour; fluent in Pirahã (Ch IV, VI).

**Expert Interview Method** — Metalearning tactic: email people who have already achieved your goal, ask for fifteen minutes, and use them to vet instrumental projects (Ch IV).

**Experimentation** — Principle 9: explore outside your comfort zone across resources, technique, and style; the principle that ties the others together (Ch XII).

**Facts** — Metalearning column for things that need only be memorized and recalled (Ch IV).

**Fehsenfeld, Diana** — New Zealand librarian who ran ultralearning projects in statistics with R and data visualization (Ch II, IV).

**Feedback** — Principle 6: seek information about your performance early and intensely, and filter signal from noise (Ch IX).

**Feynman, Richard** — Physicist whose "magical" intuition Young dissects into memorized patterns, self-derivation, and concrete examples (Ch XI).

**Feynman Technique** — Write a concept at the top of a page, explain it as if teaching a novice (for problems, explain why the procedure makes sense), and consult the source wherever you get stuck (Ch XI).

**Five-minute rule** — Procrastination crutch: commit to five minutes on the task before allowing yourself to stop (Ch V).

**Flight Simulator Method** — Directness tactic: simulate the target environment, matching its cognitive features, when real practice is unavailable (Ch VI).

**Flow** — Csikszentmihalyi's absorbed state between boredom and frustration; Young's advice is not to worry about it (Ch V).

**Forgetting curve** — Ebbinghaus's finding that knowledge decays exponentially, steepest immediately after learning, then tapering (Ch X).

**Forgotten cues** — Theory of forgetting: memories are inaccessible rather than lost, because a retrieval link has been severed (Ch X).

**Formal discipline theory** — The refuted idea that the brain is a muscle whose general capacities can be trained by any content; attacked by Thorndike and Woodworth in 1901 and revived repeatedly (Ch VI).

**Forward-testing effect** — Retrieval of previously studied information makes new information easier to learn, even when there is nothing yet to retrieve (Ch VIII).

**Foer, Joshua** — Author of *Moonwalking with Einstein*, Young's recommended introduction to mnemonics (Ch X).

**Franklin, Benjamin** — Writer whose documented drills — reconstructing *Spectator* essays, versifying prose for vocabulary, jumbling hints for ordering — exemplify Principle 4 (Ch VII).

**Free recall** — Retrieval tactic: write down everything you can remember on a blank page, without prompting; the highest-difficulty and best-retaining format (Ch VIII).

**Fusion project** — A workplace project that accomplishes a real objective while being designed to teach something new (Ch XIV).

**Gardner, Howard** — Developmental psychologist; *The Unschooled Mind* documents honors physics students failing slightly rephrased problems (Ch VI).

**Gauguin, Paul** — Artist who pushed van Gogh to paint from memory and mute his colors; the experiments failed (Ch XII).

**Gendler, Michael** — Longtime Toastmaster who coached de Montebello and co-founded UltraSpeaking; source of "Make me care" (Ch III).

**Goethe-Institut** — Administers the German exams; recommends at least 750 hours of instruction for C2 (Ch I).

**Growth mindset** — Dweck's belief that one's capacity for learning can be actively improved; the experimental mindset extends it into an active plan (Ch XII).

**Hardy, G. H.** — Mathematician who received Ramanujan's 1913 letter: the formulas "must be true because, if they were not true, no one would have had the imagination to invent them" (Ch VIII).

**Haskell, Robert** — Psychologist whose review of transfer research produced "it's an education scandal" and the observation that depth makes knowledge more flexible (Ch VI).

**High-Intensity, Rapid Feedback** — Feedback tactic: get far more feedback far more often; the emotional desensitization often outweighs the information (Ch IX).

**Hitting the Difficulty Sweet Spot** — Feedback tactic: adjust difficulty so you cannot predict success or failure, since predictable outcomes carry no information (Ch IX).

**Illusion of explanatory depth** — Rebecca Lawson's finding that people believe they understand familiar things (bicycles, can openers) they cannot actually explain or draw (Ch XI).

**Immersive Learning** — Directness tactic: surround yourself with the environment where the skill is practiced, forcing volume and variety (Ch VI).

**Informational feedback** — Middle grade of feedback: tells you what you're doing wrong but not how to fix it (Ch IX).

**Instrumental project** — Learning pursued to achieve a different, non-learning result; requires extra vetting that the skill will actually produce the outcome (Ch IV).

**Interference** — Theory of forgetting in which similar memories compete; *proactive* (old blocks new) and *retroactive* (new suppresses old) (Ch X).

**Interleaving** — Alternating between aspects of a skill within a study block, rather than blocking one topic (Ch V).

**Intrinsic project** — Learning pursued for its own sake; "intrinsic doesn't mean useless" (Ch IV).

**Intuition** — Principle 8: build deep understanding through play, self-derivation, and concrete examples rather than memorization tricks (Ch XI).

**italki** — Online tutoring and language-exchange service Young used for weekly maintenance conversations (Ch X, XIII).

**Jaiswal, Vatsal** — Architecture graduate who taught himself Revit, absorbed real blueprints at a print shop, and built a single-building portfolio; two submissions, two offers. Also Young's partner on the Year Without English (Ch VI, appendix).

**Jaunzeikare, Diana** — Ultralearner replicating a computational linguistics PhD, benchmarked against Carnegie Mellon, while working at Google (Ch I).

**Judgments of learning (JOLs)** — Self-assessments of how well you've learned, based partly on processing fluency, and systematically misleading on delay (Ch VIII).

**Kac, Mark** — Mathematician who distinguished ordinary geniuses from "magicians," and put Feynman in the second category (Ch XI).

**Karpicke, Jeffrey** — Psychologist behind the testing-effect studies and the JOL explanation for why students misjudge their strategies (Ch VIII).

**Kasparov, Garry** — Former world chess champion; the Linares touched-piece incident against Judit Polgar and his 2002 resignation to her (Ch XIV).

**Keyword method** — Mnemonic technique: convert a foreign word into a similar-sounding native phrase, then build a vivid image linking it to the meaning (*chavirer* → "shave an ear") (Ch X).

**Kluger, Avraham** — Co-author of the feedback meta-analysis; feedback works when informative, backfires when aimed at the ego (Ch IX).

**Kulik, James A. and Chen-Lin C.** — Reviewers of feedback-timing literature; applied studies usually favour immediate feedback (Ch IX).

**Laplace, Pierre-Simon** — Author of *Traité de mécanique céleste*, which Somerville translated and expanded (Ch V).

**Law of Diminishing Returns** — Applied to metalearning: keep researching only while marginal research beats marginal learning (Ch IV).

**Lawson, Rebecca** — Researcher behind the illusion of explanatory depth and the bicycle-drawing study (Ch XI).

**Levels-of-processing effect** — Retention depends on *how* you think about information, not how motivated you are; deep processors remembered almost twice as much (Ch XI).

**Lewis, Benny** — Irish polyglot whose method is to speak from day one; usually targets B2 in three months; his German C2 attempt passed four of five criteria (Ch I).

**Long-Term Working Memory** — Ericsson's alternative to the chunking model of expertise (Ch XI).

**Low-intensity habits** — Alternative strategy to ultralearning, suited to spontaneous, low-frustration, accumulation-style learning (Ch XIII).

**Magnifying Glass Method** — Drill type: spend disproportionately more time on one component than usual, e.g. 10× on research while still writing the whole article (Ch VII).

**Mair, Victor** — Linguist and Sinologist cited for the recommendation to learn to speak Mandarin before learning characters (Ch IV).

**Maini, Vishal** — Marketer who ran a six-month project into machine learning, using a mini-course as his direct practice (Ch II, VI).

**Maintenance** — Post-project option: enough practice to sustain a skill, tapering as forgetting decays (Ch XIII).

**Mauve, Anton** — Artist who persuaded van Gogh to try charcoal and chalk, watercolor, and Conté crayon (Ch XII).

**McKeachie, Wilbert** — "Transfer is paradoxical. When we want it, we do not get it. Yet it occurs all the time." (Ch VI).

**Meena, Rajveer** — Guinness World Record holder for digits of pi, at 70,000 decimal places (Ch X).

**Metafeedback** — Feedback tactic: evaluate the learning *strategy*, chiefly via learning rate, and use it to decide when to experiment (Ch IX).

**Metalearning** — Principle 1: learning about how knowledge in a subject is structured and acquired (Ch IV).

**Metalinguistic awareness** — Knowledge about how a language's information is structured; treated as interchangeable with metalearning (Ch IV).

**Millet, Jean-François** — Painter whose *The Sower* van Gogh copied repeatedly (Ch XII).

**MIT Challenge** — Young's project (October 2011 – September 2012) to cover MIT's undergraduate computer science curriculum by passing final exams and completing programming projects (Ch I, appendix).

**Mnemonics** — Retention mechanism: hyperspecific techniques translating arbitrary information into vivid images or spatial maps; a bridge, not a foundation (Ch X).

**Monolingual fieldwork demonstration** — Kenneth Pike's method for decoding an unknown language without a shared language, using objects and actions (Ch IV).

**Noise Cancellation** — Feedback tactic: filter randomness out of feedback streams, e.g. by using proxy signals such as read-through rate instead of raw traffic (Ch IX).

**Outcome feedback** — Least granular grade: tells you how you did overall but not what or how to fix (Ch IX).

**Overkill Approach** — Directness tactic: raise the challenge so the required skill level is wholly contained in the goal (middle-school audiences; the C2 exam; a public exhibit) (Ch VI).

**Overlearning** — Retention mechanism: practice beyond the point of adequate performance to extend durability (Ch X).

**Pavlina, Steve** — Ultralearner who took a triple course load and completed computer science and mathematics degrees in three semesters (Ch I).

**Pike, Kenneth** — Everett's teacher, who developed the monolingual fieldwork demonstration (Ch IV).

**Pilot week** — For projects of six months or more, test the schedule for one week before committing (Ch XIII).

**Pirahã** — Amazonian language, one of the most unusual and difficult on the planet, in which Everett became fluent (Ch IV).

**Polgar, Judit** — Youngest-ever grand master at fifteen; peaked eighth in the world; beat Kasparov in 2002 rapid play (Ch XIV).

**Polgar, Laszlo** — Author of *Raise a Genius!* (*Nevelj zsenit!*), who deliberately raised three world-class chess players (Ch XIV).

**Polgar, Klara** — Ukrainian foreign-language teacher who agreed to Laszlo's pedagogical project before they met (Ch XIV).

**Pomodoro Technique** — Twenty-five minutes of focus followed by a five-minute break; from Francesco Cirillo (Ch V).

**Prerequisite Chaining** — Drill type: start too hard, and learn foundational topics only as failure reveals the need (Ch VII).

**Proceduralization** — Retention mechanism: practice until knowledge becomes automatic motor/procedural memory, which barely decays (Ch X).

**Procedures** — Metalearning column for things that must be practiced, often without conscious thought (Ch IV).

**Project-Based Learning** — Directness tactic: organize learning around producing something, guaranteeing you at least learn to produce that thing (Ch VI).

**Proxy signal** — A measure that doesn't equal success but strips noise from feedback (Ch IX).

**Question-Book Method** — Retrieval tactic: take notes as questions rather than answers; limit yourself to one big-idea question per section (Ch VIII).

**Ramanujan, Srinivasa** — Self-taught mathematician who derived results from Carr's proofless theorem lists, an extreme case of retrieval practice (Ch VIII).

**Rappard, Anthon van** — Artist who convinced van Gogh to try reed pen and ink and short, fast strokes (Ch XII).

**Rate-determining step** — From chemistry: the slowest step that governs the whole reaction; in learning, the component that caps overall performance (Ch VII).

**Relearning** — Post-project option: let a skill decay and refresh it later, since relearning is far faster than first learning (Ch XIII).

**Retention** — Principle 7: counteract forgetting via spacing, proceduralization, overlearning, and mnemonics (Ch X).

**Retrieval** — Principle 5: recall from memory creates knowledge rather than merely measuring it (Ch VIII).

**Revit** — Architectural design software Jaiswal taught himself in order to be immediately useful to hiring firms (Ch VI).

**Richards, Nigel** — New Zealand Scrabble champion who won the French World Championship without speaking French, after nine weeks of preparation (Ch X).

**Rock, Chris** — Comedian who tests new material unannounced at the Comedy Cellar to escape the automatic positive feedback his fame generates (Ch IX).

**Ruiz-Primo, Maria Araceli** — Co-author of the definition of optimal feedback (Ch IX).

**Self-Generated Challenges** — Retrieval tactic: while going through passive material, write challenges to be solved later in practice (Ch VIII).

**Skill polarization** — Autor's finding that inequality rises at the top and compresses at the bottom as medium-skilled jobs disappear (Ch II).

**Smalley, Susan** — UCLA mindfulness researcher; with Diana Winston, argues for noting and releasing distracting thoughts rather than suppressing them (Ch V).

**Somerville, Mary** — Scottish mathematician and scientist whose translation and expansion of Laplace made her famous; Young's model of focus under constant interruption (Ch V).

**Spaced-repetition system (SRS)** — Software scheduling reviews at optimal intervals; descended from Piotr Woźniak's 1980s algorithm (Ch I, X).

**Spacing** — Retention mechanism: distribute study across intervals rather than cramming; ten hours is better as one hour a day for ten days (Ch X).

**STEMpunk Project** — Trent Fowler's yearlong, module-based project across computation, robotics, AI, and engineering (Ch I).

**Struggle timer** — Set an extra ten minutes when you feel like giving up on a hard problem (Ch XI).

**Tal, Mikhail** — Former world chess champion who predicted Judit Polgar might contend for the title (Ch XIV).

**Tamu** — Anonymous forum poster who studied Chinese 70–80+ hours a week for four months toward HSK 5 (Ch I).

**Tao, Terence** — Fields Medalist whose precocity and lack of special method pose the "Terence Tao problem" for the universality of ultralearning (Ch II).

**Tersteeg, H. G.** — Art seller and family friend who told van Gogh "You started too late.... you are no artist" (Ch XII).

**Testing effect** — Retrieval practice outperforms review and concept mapping; free recall students remembered ~50 percent more (Ch VIII).

**Thorndike, Edward** — With Robert Woodworth, refuted formal discipline theory in 1901 by showing transfer is narrow (Ch VI).

**Time Slicing** — Drill type: isolate a slice in time of a longer sequence, as musicians and athletes do (Ch VII).

**Toastmasters International** — The organization whose competition ladder gave de Montebello's project its structure (Ch III).

**Transfer** — Using knowledge learned in one context in another; "the Holy Grail of education," and largely absent from formal schooling (Ch VI).

**Transfer-appropriate processing** — The psychological concept directness is closely related to (Ch VI).

**Ultralearning** — "A strategy for acquiring skills and knowledge that is both self-directed and intense" (Ch II). Term first used by Cal Newport in the headline of an article Young wrote for his site (Ch I).

**UltraSpeaking** — The coaching consultancy Gendler and de Montebello founded, named as a nod to ultralearning (Ch III).

**Van Gogh, Vincent** — Painter who started at twenty-six with no obvious talent and reached originality through relentless cycles of experimentation (Ch XII).

**Vitruvian Studio** — Source of the systematic portrait-drawing technique Young adopted mid-project (Ch IV, X, appendix).

**Willingham, Daniel** — Cognitive psychologist quoted as "Memory is the residue of thought" (Ch X).

**Winston, Diana** — Meditation teacher at UCLA's Mindful Awareness Research Center (Ch V).

**Woodworth, Robert** — Co-author with Thorndike of the 1901 paper refuting general transfer (Ch VI).

**Woźniak, Piotr** — Polish researcher who developed the spaced-repetition algorithm in the 1980s (Ch I).

**Year Without English** — Young and Vatsal Jaiswal's project (September 2013 – August 2014): Spain, Brazil, China, South Korea, three months each, no English from day one (Ch I, appendix).

**Zenger, John H.** — Former Times Mirror Training Group chairman quoted on the difficulty of finding demonstrable change after corporate training (Ch VI).
