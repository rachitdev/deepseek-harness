# Patterns — Music Theory for Computer Musicians (Michael Hewitt)

The book's reusable moves, grouped by its own natural parts. Each is stated as **When / How /
Trade-offs**.

---

## Part 1 — Learning and Internalizing Material

### Learn a scale physically before conceptually
**When** — Any time a new scale or key enters your vocabulary (Ch 3, 9, 11, 15).
**How** — Play it up and down slowly on a MIDI keyboard, aiming for an even tone on each note;
thumb under for the fourth note ascending, reverse the fingering on the descent. Continue until
you can "hear" the scale simply by imagining it. Without a keyboard, sequence it and listen carefully.
**Trade-offs** — Slow to acquire, and Hewitt warns "do not be tempted to go too fast." But it is
what develops the inner ear and aural memory; knowing note names without hearing them is
explicitly insufficient.

### The four-step new-scale procedure
**When** — Adopting any unfamiliar scale (harmonic minor, melodic minor, a new key, a mode).
**How** — (1) Play the scale until fluent. (2) Learn its chords, practicing them **as arpeggios**
(root with the thumb, third with finger three, fifth with the little finger). (3) Build three-chord
progressions from the primary chords. (4) Add secondary chords and substitute variants (e.g. V for
v) to hear the difference (Ch 11).
**Trade-offs** — Four passes over the same material takes time, but steps 3–4 are where the scale
becomes compositionally usable rather than just playable.

### Learn one key deeply, transpose the rest
**When** — Expanding beyond C major and A minor (Ch 9, 15).
**How** — Learn the scale, define its triads, then take progressions you already wrote and
transpose them into the new key.
**Trade-offs** — "Knowledge you acquire about one key… effectively equates to knowledge of all
keys" — but only if you actually do the transposition drill; passive recognition doesn't transfer
to playing.

### Study by analytical listening
**When** — Building any vocabulary — motives, modes, exotic scales, chord progressions (Ch 7, 10, 22).
**How** — Listen to music you enjoy with one question in mind, work out what you heard in note
values or intervals, then use it. For motives: "Why has the writer used this motive? What do the
intervals suggest?" For exotic scales: identify intervals from the tonic, available triads, and the
signature two- or three-note melodic groups.
**Trade-offs** — Slower than copying a preset, but it is what builds a personal catalog rather than
a borrowed one.

## Part 2 — Rhythm and Meter

### Rhythm before pitch
**When** — Writing a motive, a bass line, or a rhythmic arpeggio (Ch 10, 24).
**How** — "Set up the rhythm that you want first on a single note. When you've done so, then
introduce the changes of pitch. This is much easier than trying to do both at the same time."
**Trade-offs** — Feels mechanical, but it separates two problems that are hard to solve at once,
and it puts the identity-bearing element (rhythm) first.

### Syncopate against an established cycle
**When** — A four-square pattern needs energy (Ch 7).
**How** — Establish the metric cycle so the listener internalizes the stress pattern, then place
stress on a normally weak point. Hewitt's dance-music case: kick beats 2, 3, and 4 arriving a
quaver later than expected.
**Trade-offs** — Depends entirely on the expectation being set first; syncopation with no
established cycle is just an irregular rhythm.

### Compose meters additively
**When** — 4/4 has become a default rather than a decision (Ch 14).
**How** — Chain cells of two and three: 2+3 = quintuple; 3+3+2 = 8; 3+3+2+3+2 = 13/8;
3+3+3+2+2+2 = 15/8. Set up a steady count out loud before attempting hand coordination.
**Trade-offs** — Odd meters can alienate a dance floor; the compromise is additive grouping
*inside* 4/4 — 3+3+2 over eight steps, or 3+3+3+3+2+2 over sixteen, as Cuban and Latin music do.

### Layer divisions against each other (cross-rhythm)
**When** — A drum track needs density and edge (Ch 7).
**How** — Play triplets against pairs — tambourine on sixteenth triplets over closed hi-hats on
straight sixteenths. Classically the three-against-two case is hemiola.
**Trade-offs** — Dense and exciting, but requires the parts to be rhythmically clean; Hewitt notes
the technique is neglected in electronic music and worth experimenting with.

### Use odd step counts in arpeggios
**When** — An arpeggio pattern feels static across bars (Ch 24).
**How** — Use 5, 7, or 11 steps against an even bar instead of 4/8/16/32. Because the count is odd,
different notes land on the strong points each cycle — "wheels within wheels" — generating inner
melodies for free.
**Trade-offs** — The pattern no longer locks to the bar, which is the point, but it can obscure
the downbeat if nothing else marks it.

### Quantize selectively
**When** — Recorded parts sit off the grid (Ch 4).
**How** — Set the quantize value in note values and apply. Resolution controls what the Pencil
draws; snap-to-grid controls where drawn notes land; quantization moves recorded notes.
**Trade-offs** — "This is not always desirable though, because it can lead to a rigid, mechanical
sound." Velocity variation is the companion move that restores life (Ch 1).

## Part 3 — Melody

### Build melody from motives, not note streams
**When** — A lead or bass line lacks identity (Ch 10).
**How** — Write a two- or three-note cell with a strong, immediately recognizable rhythm. Pair two
statements in a question/answer relationship to form a phrase. Repeat and vary.
**Trade-offs** — Constrains the melody to a small cell, which is exactly what makes it memorable.

### Choose intervals by emotional intent
**When** — The rhythm is set and pitches must be chosen (Ch 10).
**How** — Ask: what does the motive represent? Is the energy rising or falling? Major or minor?
Erratic leaps or smooth stepwise motion? Then pick the interval that answers. Build a personal
catalog of interval-to-feeling associations.
**Trade-offs** — Subjective — and Hewitt says that is fine: "If an interval causes you to feel
something, it is liable to cause the same feeling in your listeners."

### Treat a motive to develop material
**When** — A motive needs to sustain a longer section (Ch 10, Appendix B Track 29).
**How** — Augmentation (longer note values), diminution (shorter values), inversion (upside down),
retrograde (backwards), retrograde inversion. Or repeat it at different pitches on different tracks
to build an ambient texture.
**Trade-offs** — Systematic transformation can sound academic; Hewitt's film-music framing —
treating the motive to signal emotional context, as Williams does in *Jaws* — keeps it purposeful.

## Part 4 — Harmony and Voicing

### Anchor a track with the three primary triads
**When** — Starting out in any key (Ch 8, 9).
**How** — Learn I, IV, V (or i, iv, v). Between them they define all seven notes of the scale, so
they can harmonize any tune in it. Work through their six orderings, end on I with IV or V before it.
**Trade-offs** — "The progressions they produce might sound very familiar" — three-chord rock,
twelve-bar blues. That is the price of the foundation; secondary triads are the next step.

### Choose a cadence for the ending you want
**When** — Closing a section (Ch 8).
**How** — **V–I (perfect)** for complete, unambiguous closure. **IV–I (plagal/Amen)** for a mellow
settling. In minor keys, **substitute major V for minor v** (harmonic minor) when you want the
decisive version.
**Trade-offs** — The harmonic-minor V is more incisive but pulls the passage toward classical
diction; the natural-minor v keeps the mellow, quiescent character.

### Pick root movement for smoothness or contrast
**When** — Deciding what chord comes next (Ch 18).
**How** — By **fourths** (1 shared note) for the strongest movement, especially in sequence.
By **thirds** (2 shared notes) for the smoothest connection. By **seconds** (0 shared notes) for
maximum contrast.
**Trade-offs** — A direct spectrum: smoothness and contrast trade against each other, and fourths
sit at the balance point. Mixing types within one progression is normal.

### Voice-lead by exploiting shared notes
**When** — A progression sounds blocky and disjointed (Ch 13).
**How** — Choose inversions and doubling so that notes common to consecutive chords are sustained;
score for at least four parts, doubling root and fifth; ensure each chord remains a complete triad.
Test by imagining the parts sung.
**Trade-offs** — Requires deciding chord positions rather than defaulting to root position, but it
is the difference between "awkward, blocky, and very unmusical" and "smooth, musical, and coherent."

### Free the bass with slash chords
**When** — The bass line is jumping between roots (Ch 13).
**How** — Use first inversion (third in bass, C/E) or second inversion (fifth in bass, C/G) to give
the bass a smoother, more melodic path.
**Trade-offs** — Inversions are less stable than root position; keep root position for endings,
where most songs land.

### Vary one chord instead of adding another
**When** — A static or single-chord section needs interest (Ch 13, 23).
**How** — Take one chord and re-voice it: change inversion, change spacing (closed to open),
double different notes, move notes between registers. Sequence and listen to each version.
**Trade-offs** — Costs nothing harmonically and works in styles built on static harmonic fields;
does not, however, create *progression* — that requires root movement.

### Harmonize by parallelism for atmosphere
**When** — You want an "old world," Oriental, or rock-power-chord character (Ch 8).
**How** — Shadow the tune at fourths, fifths, and octaves. Production shortcut: tune the second
oscillator a perfect fifth (seven semitones) or a fourth above the first.
**Trade-offs** — "A strong but empty plaintive kind of sound"; the effect is a cliché in film music
for Oriental atmosphere, so use knowingly. The same technique with ninth chords ("parallel ninths")
gives a modern equivalent — sample one ninth chord and play it on different keys.

## Part 5 — Extended and Complex Harmony

### Introduce sevenths as passing chords
**When** — Triadic harmony needs spicing up but full seventh-chord writing is too big a step (Ch 21).
**How** — Insert a seventh between two triads so the seventh resolves in the next chord —
**Dm – Dm7 – G**, where the seventh (C) acts as a passing note between D and B.
**Trade-offs** — A safe, incremental route; the payoff is limited until you commit to progressions
built entirely from sevenths.

### Handle dissonance by resolution or by saturation
**When** — Any extended chord (Ch 21).
**How** — Either resolve the dissonance stepwise to the nearest concord (classical), possibly
chaining sevenths so each resolves into the next; or place it in a texture so full of sevenths that
the ear accepts them as stable (jazz).
**Trade-offs** — "Both methods work equally well, provided that they occur **in context**." Mixing
the two arbitrarily leaves dissonances sounding like mistakes.

### Trim extended chords to their defining notes
**When** — A five-, six-, or seven-note chord is muddy (Ch 21, 23).
**How** — Seventh: keep the **third and seventh**, drop the fifth first, double the root below.
Ninth: keep the **seventh and ninth**. Eleventh: keep **root, third, seventh, eleventh**.
Thirteenth: keep **root, seventh, thirteenth**; avoid the third.
**Trade-offs** — Thinner texture, but the chord's identity survives; keeping everything destroys it.

### Chromatically alter the dominant
**When** — A dominant chord needs jazz color (Ch 23).
**How** — Keep the third and seventh (they maintain the dominant pull) and alter the fifth, seventh,
ninth, or eleventh. Sharpen a natural eleventh clashing with the third — this gives the Lydian
eleventh, C E G B D F#.
**Trade-offs** — Produces "a great number of complex sonorities," but only works over clear root
movement; the third and seventh are non-negotiable.

### Anchor complexity with strong root movement
**When** — Writing dense or borrowed harmony (Ch 18, 23).
**How** — Fix a clear root scheme first, then treat everything above it as color — including notes
and whole chords borrowed from other keys and modes.
**Trade-offs** — "Provided that the root movement scheme is clear, the harmony's sense of direction
will be retained." Without it, the ear has nothing to interpret against.

## Part 6 — Scales, Modes, and Colour

### Rotate the tonic to change the mood
**When** — Major/minor feels exhausted (Ch 16, 20).
**How** — Keep the note set, move the tonic. Seven diatonic modes; five pentatonic modes; × 12
keys gives 84 and 60 possibilities respectively.
**Trade-offs** — Each mode has one signature deviation that carries its character — and one
liability. Locrian has no proper tonic triad; the Ionian is the only mode where a perfect cadence
is desirable.

### Fix the mode with a drone
**When** — Writing modal or exotic-scale material (Ch 20, 22).
**How** — Sustain the tonic (or tonic and fifth, sitar-style) as a drone or pedal point; in
loop-based music, make it the note the bass revolves around. Each scale note is then heard as an
interval against it.
**Trade-offs** — Fixes the mode unmistakably and makes exotic intervals legible, at the cost of
harmonic movement.

### Avoid the perfect cadence in modal writing
**When** — Any non-Ionian modal piece (Ch 21).
**How** — Steer clear of anything resembling V–I. "When it does occur, the ear will automatically
focus upon the implied tonic toward which the cadence occurs."
**Trade-offs** — Removes the strongest closure device you have; drones and characteristic
progressions (Dm–G for Dorian, Em–F for Phrygian) take its place.

### Flatten the second to make anything Neapolitan
**When** — You want an Eastern or exotic inflection from a familiar scale (Ch 22).
**How** — Take any mode or scale and flatten the second degree. The signature note group in a
Neapolitan major is C Db E.
**Trade-offs** — Cheap and portable; the resulting scales "don't always offer suitable harmonies,"
so treat them melodically or use the specific chord mixes Hewitt names (Bbm/Eb/F in Neapolitan
Dorian; Bbm/C in Neapolitan Mixolydian).

### Enrich triads with pentatonic added notes
**When** — Common triads sound plain (Ch 16).
**How** — Add the sixth, the ninth, or both to a major triad. Or swap the third for a neighbor note
to make a suspended chord. Or abandon triads for quartal/quintal stacks — a five-note quintal
chord uses the whole pentatonic scale.
**Trade-offs** — "Can give your tracks an edge over music that just uses common triads." The
pentatonic scale itself contains only two complete triads, so this is the main way to get harmony
out of it — the alternative being to borrow Dm, Em, F, and G from the diatonic scale.

### Analyze any new scale with three questions
**When** — Adopting an unfamiliar exotic scale (Ch 22).
**How** — (1) What intervals does each note form with the tonic? (2) What conspicuous triads are
available for harmony? (3) What two- or three-note groups give the scale its character?
**Trade-offs** — Front-loads work before you write a note, but it turns an unusable note list into
a compositional resource.

## Part 7 — Sound and Tuning

### Vary velocity to defeat the machine
**When** — MIDI parts sound flat (Ch 1, Appendix B Track 3).
**How** — Vary velocity across the 0–127 scale; add subtle panning changes on percussion.
**Trade-offs** — None; "uniform levels of velocity lead to dull and uninspired parts."

### Shape timbre by harmonic content
**When** — Choosing or sculpting a sound (Ch 1).
**How** — Pick the waveform by harmonic content — square (odd harmonics) for hollow dance basses,
triangle for flute-like clarity, sawtooth (harmonically rich) for strings, brass, and trance leads.
Then use EQ to attenuate or suppress upper harmonics for brightness or dullness, and shape the
ADSR envelope.
**Trade-offs** — "The skill of successful equalization lies in getting the balance just right";
harmonic content without envelope emulation gives unconvincing imitations.

### Retune for authenticity in modal music
**When** — Working with Balinese, Arabian, Hindustani, or overtone material (Ch 25).
**How** — Use cent-level fine-tuning (1200 cents per octave, 100 per semitone). Cubase and the Korg
Triton's user scales both support this.
**Trade-offs** — "It is not possible to construct a pure scale for more than one diatonic key" —
all keys pure would need ~60 microtones. Modal music, staying in one key, is where it is practical.
