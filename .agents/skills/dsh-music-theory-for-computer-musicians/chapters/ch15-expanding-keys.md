# Chapter 15 — Expanding Your Knowledge of Keys

## Core Idea
"To write music with any proficiency, you need knowledge both of individual keys and of the
system to which they belong… Otherwise, it is like working in the dark." The system is simple
because the chromatic scale has only twelve notes, so **there are only twelve possible keynotes**.
Apply TTSTTTS from any of them and the black keys you are forced to use — as sharps or as flats —
*are* that key's **key signature**.

## Frameworks Introduced

### Twelve Keynotes, One Formula
"Because the chromatic scale only has 12 notes, this means that, practically speaking, there are
only 12 possible keynotes. You can take any of these 12 notes to be the prospective keynote (or
tonic) of a major or minor scale. To construct a major scale upon any keynote, you must apply the
formula **Tone-Tone-Semitone-Tone-Tone-Tone-Semitone**."

### Deriving Accidentals from the Formula
The chapter's method is to *derive*, not memorize:
- **G major** needs **F#** — "because you need a whole tone between the sixth and seventh degrees
  of the scale. If you used the note F, there would only be a semitone between them."
- **D major** needs **two** black keys: **F#** "to give the whole tone required between the second
  and third degrees," and **C#** "to give the whole tone required between the sixth and seventh
  degrees."
- **Some scales use the black keys, but as flats.** F major and Bb major are the chapter's two examples.

### Key Signature
"Because music written in a particular key will use the sharps or flats that belong to it, you can
identify each key by a **key signature**, which indicates the notes to be sharpened or flattened.
This is placed on staff systems at the beginning of the piece of music."
- In **F major**, "every time a note B occurs, it is to be played flat."
- In **D major**, "all F's and C's will be played sharp."

**Why a Reason/Live user still needs them:** "If your musical output is confined to software
programs, such as Reason or Ableton Live, the use of key signatures is not necessary. You still
need to learn them, though, in order to know which notes to select on the piano roll view or
matrix pattern sequencer for the particular key used in your current song." And staff notation
"is a universal format" — essential when downloading MIDI files for a remix or arrangement,
because "such information necessarily includes the key of the song."

### Relative Minor Rule (generalized)
"To work out a relative minor key, **count down three notes (or up six notes)** from the tonic of
the equivalent major key." Because major and relative minor use the same notes, **their key
signatures are identical** — "This applies to all types of minor scale."

### The Learn-a-New-Key Procedure
1. **Learn to play the scale through.** (Fingerings are in Appendix A, "Scales.")
2. **Define the triads of the scale.**
3. **Take the chord progressions produced earlier and play or sequence them in the new key.**
   "Called **transposition**, this is the transference of music from one key to another."

### Harmonic Mixing
"When mixing, a DJ needs a certain degree of key awareness — an awareness that is honed to its
maximum within the process known as **harmonic mixing**."

## Key Concepts
- **Keynote / tonic** — the note a scale is built on and the note that identifies the key.
- **Transposition** — moving music from one key to another; the exercises drill it on both major
  and minor three-chord progressions.
- **Why C major and A minor first** — "Starting with the white keys of C major and A minor is
  easier in this respect because they use the white keys of the keyboard. So you avoid the
  complexities of sharps and flats."

## Mental Models
- **Derive, don't memorize.** Every accidental in a key signature exists to preserve a specific
  tone or semitone required by TTSTTTS. Knowing *which degree* forced each sharp or flat makes the
  key signature reconstructible rather than arbitrary.
- **The key system is small.** Twelve keynotes × two modes, with major and relative minor sharing
  a signature — the whole system collapses into a manageable table.
- **A key is scale + chords + progressions, not just a scale.** The three-step procedure treats a
  new key as fully learned only when your existing progressions have been transposed into it.

## Anti-patterns
- Working without knowing the key: "it is like working in the dark."
- Skipping key signatures because your DAW has no staff view — you still need them to pick the
  right piano-roll notes, and to read MIDI files you did not write.
- Memorizing key signatures as arbitrary lists instead of deriving them from the formula.

## Worked Example
**Exercise 9:** "Transpose the following major key three-chord progression (I, IV, V) into the
required keys." The book supplies C major complete as the model.

| Key | I | IV | V |
|---|---|---|---|
| **C major** (given) | C E G | F A C | G B D |
| **Bb major** | **Bb D F** | **Eb G Bb** | **F A C** |
| **D major** | **D F# A** | **G B D** | **A C# E** |
| **F major** | **F A C** | **Bb D F** | **C E G** |

**Exercise 11** runs the same drill in the minor, with the book supplying A natural minor:

| Key | i | iv | V (or v) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A natural minor** (given) | A C E | D F A | E G B |
| **G harmonic minor** | **G Bb D** | **C Eb G** | **D F# A** (V major, sharp 7) |
| **D melodic minor** | **D F A** | **G B D** | **A C# E** |
| **E natural minor** | **E G B** | **A C E** | **B D F#** |
| **B harmonic minor** | **B D F#** | **E G B** | **F# A# C#** (V major, sharp 7) |

Note the pattern the exercise is teaching: in the **harmonic** minor keys the seventh degree is
sharpened, which turns chord v into a **major** chord V — exactly Chapter 11's cadence point,
now transposed.

**Exercise 5**, answered from the chapter's own rule: count down **three** notes (or up **six**);
the relative minor of G is therefore **E minor**.

**Exercise 3** applies TTSTTTS from each keynote:
- C major: C **D E F G A B C**
- D major: D **E F# G A B C# D**
- F major: F **G A Bb C D E F**
- Bb major: Bb **C D Eb F G A Bb**
- G major: G **A B C D E F# G**

## Key Takeaways
1. Twelve chromatic notes = twelve possible keynotes; one formula (TTSTTTS) generates every major scale.
2. Every accidental in a key signature exists to preserve a required tone or semitone — derive it.
3. G major = F#. D major = F#, C#. F major = Bb. Bb major = Bb, Eb.
4. A key signature at the start of the staff applies to every occurrence of those notes.
5. Relative minor = down three (or up six); major and relative minor share a key signature.
6. Learn a new key in three steps: play the scale, define its triads, transpose your existing
   progressions into it.
7. DAW-only producers still need key signatures — for piano-roll note choice and for reading
   downloaded MIDI files.
