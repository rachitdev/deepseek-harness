# Chapter 3 — The Major Scale

## Core Idea
A scale is "a rising or falling series of notes, usually within the range of an octave — and
repeated in all other octaves," and it is the shared source from which both melody (notes used
successively) and harmony (notes used simultaneously) draw. The major scale is defined not by its
letters but by a **pattern of tones and semitones — TTSTTTS / 2212221** — and because it is a
pattern, it can be transposed onto any of the twelve keynotes.

## Frameworks Introduced

### Scale versus Key
- **Key** concerns *the note upon which a scale is built*. The MIDI keyboard offers twelve notes
  per octave (5 black + 7 white), so there are twelve possible keynotes.
- **Scale** is the pattern of notes built from that keynote. "A scale that has note C as the
  starting point is said to be in the key of C."

### The Major Scale Formula
The interval between any two adjacent keys (or guitar frets) is a **semitone**; there are
**12 semitones in the octave**, each 1/12 of an octave. That series of twelve is the
**chromatic scale** — "not generally used as a musical scale in its own right," but the pool from
which the notes of any scale are selected. A gap of two semitones is a **tone**.

Two memorizable formats of the major-scale pattern:
- **Numerical: 2 2 1 2 2 2 1** (semitones between successive degrees)
- **Letter: T T S T T T S** (T = tone, S = semitone)

On a guitar this translates as **2212221** successive frets.

Critical observation: "this difference between tones and semitones in the major scale cannot be
seen or recognized when the notes are placed on the staff. These differences only become apparent
when the notes of the major scale are plotted on the keyboard."

### Scale Degrees
Each of the seven notes gets a number showing its order, the first degree (C in C major) being 1.

### Building a Major Scale on Any Note
Once the pattern is known, "it becomes possible to build a major scale on any note simply by
counting up from that note: tone, tone, semitone, tone, tone, tone, semitone." Figure 3.9 shows
this applied to **F#** in a piano roll view.

### The Learn-by-Playing Protocol
1. Play the scale up and down slowly on a keyboard (or guitar/bass).
2. Aim for an **even tone on each note**; Track 6 indicates the target speed.
3. Fingering for C major right hand: thumb = finger 1 on C, second finger on D, third on E; for
   **F the thumb passes under the hand**, then the next available finger for each note upward. At
   top C the fingering pattern reverses for the descent.
4. Continue until you can "hear" the scale simply by imagining it — this develops inner ear and
   aural memory.
5. Without a MIDI keyboard, sequence the scale up and down and listen carefully.

## Key Concepts
- **Interval** — "the gap between two notes"; the octave and the major third C–E are examples.
- **Chromatic scale** — the 12 semitones of the octave.
- **Tone / semitone** — 2 semitones / 1 semitone. In C major the semitone gaps are **E–F** and
  **B–C** (the pairs with no black key between them); tones occur C–D, D–E, F–G, G–A, A–B.
- **Tonic** — the most important note of the scale, the first degree, the tonal center.
- **Harmonic coherence** — "Recognizing and using a key and scale system is one of the first steps
  toward creating beautiful music because it means that all of your musical layers are in a
  particular scale and key and thus work together musically."

## Mental Models
- **Pattern, not letters.** The major scale is a shape you slide up and down the chromatic ladder.
  TTSTTTS "is a useful formula because it enables you to build the same scale system on other
  keynotes, thus giving you access to an entire range of keys."
- **Brightness axis.** The major scale "is a generally bright-sounding scale — unlike the more
  moody and introspective minor scale" — yet still "capable of expressing strong emotions."
  Hewitt's examples of songs in C major: John Lennon's "Imagine" and "Somewhere Over the Rainbow."
- **One scale is enough for a whole track.** "As a complete musical scale system in its own right,
  the scale of C major offers all the notes needed for an entire composition."

## Anti-patterns
- Practicing scales fast: "do not be tempted to go too fast."
- Learning names without sound: "It is one thing to know the names of the notes, but it is
  another to hear them clearly in your mind."
- Trying to read tone/semitone structure off the staff instead of the keyboard.
- Relying only on decks/MIDI keyboard with no keyboard facility — Hewitt notes that every
  computer musician he tutors, "without exception," wants to improve keyboard skills, because the
  MIDI keyboard is "the point of entry into the computer for vital musical information."

## Worked Example
**Exercise 7:** "Define the gaps between the following pairs of notes in terms of the number of
semitones: A. C–D; B. B–C; C. A–B; D. E–F."

Worked from the chapter's own rule — a black key between two white keys means two semitones (a
tone); no black key between them means one semitone:
- **C–D = 2 semitones** (a tone; C# lies between)
- **B–C = 1 semitone** (a semitone; no black key)
- **A–B = 2 semitones** (a tone; A# lies between)
- **E–F = 1 semitone** (a semitone; no black key)

**Exercise 4:** "Which degree of the scale are these notes in C major? D; B; G; F." From
Figure 3.2's numbering C=1: **D = 2, B = 7, G = 5, F = 4**.

**Exercise 6** is the formula itself: **T T S T T T S** (2 2 1 2 2 2 1).

Applying the formula to a new keynote, as Figure 3.9 does for **F#**: start F#, up a tone → G#,
up a tone → A#, up a semitone → B, tone → C#, tone → D#, tone → E#(F), semitone → F#.

## Key Takeaways
1. Scale = the note pool; key = the note the pool is built on. Twelve keynotes are available.
2. The major scale is TTSTTTS (2212221) — memorize both formats.
3. Semitone = adjacent keys/frets; twelve of them make the chromatic scale; a tone = two semitones.
4. In C major the two semitone steps are E–F and B–C.
5. The formula transposes: apply it from any keynote to build that major scale.
6. Learn scales physically and slowly; the goal is being able to hear the scale in your head.
