# Chapter 19 — The Cycle of Fifths

## Core Idea
Two principles generate the entire Western key system, and both follow from TTSTTTS:
**each key whose tonic is a fifth higher requires a further sharp; each key whose tonic is a fifth
lower requires a further flat.** Follow either direction and you eventually meet the other side at
F#/Gb, closing a circle of twelve keys. "The scheme of 12 major and 12 minor keys linked by a
common cycle of fifths represents the practical summary of the key system of Western music."

## Frameworks Introduced

### The Two Important Principles
> **Important Principle: Each key whose tonic is a fifth higher requires a further sharp in the key
> signature.**
>
> **Important Principle: Each key whose tonic is a fifth lower requires a further flat in the key
> signature.**

The mechanism, from Chapter 15: a fifth up from C is G, which needs F# "to give the requisite
whole-tone gap between the sixth and seventh degrees of the scale (TTSTTTS)"; a fifth above G is
D, which needs two sharps; and so on.

### Bright Side / Dark Side
- **Sharp keys are "keys on the bright side."** "Keys with sharps in them are often thought by
  musicians to be emotionally bright keys. **The more sharps a key has, the brighter it is felt to
  be.**"
- **Flat keys are "keys on the dark side."** "Keys with flats are generally felt to be emotionally
  darker or more somber than the sharp keys. **The more flats a key has, the darker it is felt to
  be.**"
- **C is the pivot.** "The key of C — a key with no sharps or flats — represents a pivot or point
  of balance between the bright sharp keys and the more somber flat keys."
- Hewitt notes some musicians develop **color associations** with keys instead, and that either way
  it "can affect your choice of key for a particular project."

### Closing the Cycle — the Enharmonic Meeting Point
"After **B major** in the cycle, which has **five sharps**, comes **F# major**, which has **six**.
Similarly, after **Db major** in the cycle, which has **five flats**, comes **Gb major**, which has
**six**. Notice that the keys with six sharps/flats have the same keynotes — F# and Gb."

"The anomaly is that those keys on the extreme sharp side of the cycle meet those keys on the
extreme flat side of the cycle. This meeting point (**F#/Gb**) is the point of closure of a cycle
of 12 keys whose keynotes embrace all 12 notes of the chromatic scale."

> **Advanced note (Hewitt's own):** "With pure tuning… the note F# is slightly higher in pitch than
> note Gb by a tiny microtonal interval called the **Pythagorean comma** (about **1/5 of a
> semitone**). Because we use an equally tempered system of tuning, the two are struck by the same
> key/fret. The keys of F# and Gb major are therefore **enharmonically equivalent keys** — that is,
> the same key but spelled differently."

### Key Relationship = Notes in Common
"Keys to the left and right of a particular key are the major keys most closely related to it. This
closeness lies in the fact that keys to the right and left of a given key in the circular scheme
have the most notes in common."
- **Most closely related to D: G and A.** "This is useful to know when you are attempting to
  modulate (in other words, change key). Key changes to closely related keys are the most common
  and occur in many types of music."
- **Most distantly related to C major: Gb major**, "simply because the two keys share the fewest
  notes in common. This distance of relationship is seen in their placement on **diametrically
  opposite points of the circle**."

### The Cycle and Minor Keys
"With minor keys the cycle of fifths works according to exactly the same principle, the difference
being that **the cycle begins with A minor because this is the minor key that has no sharps or
flats**." The closest minor keys to D minor are **A minor** (a fifth above) and **G minor** (a
fifth below). "Should a modulation… be needed from a major to a minor key or vice versa, **the
relative minor key is the closest association**."

So the closest-relations set around C/Am comprises C major with F and G major, and Am with Dm and
Em (Figure 19.6).

### The Four Competencies
Hewitt's explicit checklist for mastery of the key system:
- "To ensure that you become capable of identifying all of the major and minor keys"
- "To know your key signatures"
- "To be able to effect the correct alterations for the relevant harmonic and melodic minor modes"
- "To be able to locate all of the relevant chords in each of those keys."

"If you can achieve this, you will then have mastery over the key system of Western music."

## Key Concepts
- **Pythagorean comma** — about 1/5 of a semitone; the gap between F# and Gb under pure tuning,
  erased by equal temperament (Chapter 25).
- **Enharmonically equivalent keys** — F#/Gb; the same key spelled differently.
- **Software assumption:** "Musical software manufacturers generally assume such knowledge.
  Cakewalk SONAR, for example, offers recognition to a full **15 major and minor keys**."
- **Where next:** the pentatonic modes give "each of its five modes… in all 12 keys, thus giving
  you **60 possible options**," and the old church modes follow in Chapter 20.

## Mental Models
- **One step round the circle = one accidental.** The cycle turns key signatures from a list to be
  memorized into a position to be counted.
- **Adjacency = relatedness = modulation ease.** Neighbours on the circle share the most notes, so
  they are the natural destinations for a key change; the opposite point is the most remote.
- **Brightness is a spatial axis.** Sharpward = brighter, flatward = darker, C at the balance point.

## Anti-patterns
- Memorizing key signatures without the generative rule.
- Modulating to a diametrically opposite key by default when a neighbouring key would be smoother.
- Forgetting that minor keys need their own harmonic/melodic alterations applied on top of the key
  signature.

## Reference Tables

### The cycle of fifths — sharp side (rising fifths from C)
| Key | Accidentals | Sharps added |
|---|---|---|
| C major | 0 | — |
| G major | 1 sharp | F# |
| D major | 2 sharps | F#, C# |
| A major | 3 sharps | F#, C#, G# |
| E major | 4 sharps | F#, C#, G#, D# |
| B major | 5 sharps | F#, C#, G#, D#, A# |
| **F# major** | **6 sharps** | + E# |

### The cycle of fifths — flat side (falling fifths from C)
| Key | Accidentals | Flats added |
|---|---|---|
| C major | 0 | — |
| F major | 1 flat | Bb |
| Bb major | 2 flats | Bb, Eb |
| Eb major | 3 flats | Bb, Eb, Ab |
| Ab major | 4 flats | Bb, Eb, Ab, Db |
| Db major | 5 flats | Bb, Eb, Ab, Db, Gb |
| **Gb major** | **6 flats** | + Cb |

**F# major (6 sharps) and Gb major (6 flats) are the same key, enharmonically spelled** — the
point of closure.

### The minor cycle (starting from A minor, no accidentals)
| Direction | Sequence |
|---|---|
| Rising fifths (sharps) | A – E – B – F# – C# – G# – D# minor |
| Falling fifths (flats) | A – D – G – C – F – Bb – Eb minor |

### Closest-relation lookup
| Key | Closest major keys | Closest minor association |
|---|---|---|
| C major | F major, G major | A minor (relative) |
| D major | G major, A major | B minor (relative) |
| D minor | A minor (5th above), G minor (5th below) | F major (relative) |
| C major (most distant) | **Gb major** | — |

## Worked Example
**The chapter's own worked closure of the cycle:**

Start at C and go up in fifths, adding one sharp each step:
C (0) → G (1) → D (2) → A (3) → E (4) → **B (5)** → **F# (6)**.

Start at C and go down in fifths, adding one flat each step:
C (0) → F (1) → Bb (2) → Eb (3) → Ab (4) → **Db (5)** → **Gb (6)**.

The two arms meet: **F# major and Gb major have the same keynote**. Under pure tuning they differ
by the Pythagorean comma (~1/5 semitone), but equal temperament strikes them with the same
key/fret — so they are enharmonically equivalent, and the twelve keys form a closed circle
covering all twelve chromatic notes. "Beginning with C, you can go either way around the circle
until you arrive back at C again."

**Exercise 1**, answered from the chapter's own text: "A major scale whose **tonic** (keynote) is a
perfect fifth above the keynote of C requires the addition of a **sharp** in the key signature (the
key of G major). This produces the requisite semitone gap between the **seventh** and **eighth
(tonic)** degrees of the scale [the whole-tone gap between the sixth and seventh]. Similarly, a
major scale whose tonic is a **fifth** above G (D major) requires **two** sharps in the key signature."

**Exercise 2:** "A major scale whose tonic lies a **fifth** below the keynote of C requires the
addition of a **flat** in the key signature (F major). A major scale whose tonic is a further fifth
down (Bb) then requires **two** flats in the key signature."

**Exercise 5b:** "Circle the key that is enharmonically equivalent to the key of F# major" —
**Gb major**.

## Key Takeaways
1. Up a fifth = one more sharp; down a fifth = one more flat. Two rules generate all key signatures.
2. Sharp keys read as bright, flat keys as dark, C as the balance point.
3. The circle closes at F#/Gb (6 sharps = 6 flats), enharmonically equivalent under equal temperament.
4. Under pure tuning F# and Gb differ by the Pythagorean comma, ~1/5 of a semitone.
5. Circle neighbours share the most notes and are the easiest modulation targets; diametric
   opposites are the most remote.
6. The minor cycle works identically, starting from A minor; relative major/minor is the closest
   cross-mode relationship.
7. Mastery = identify all keys, know all signatures, apply harmonic/melodic minor alterations, and
   locate every chord in every key.
