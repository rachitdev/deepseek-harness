# Chapter 9 — The Natural Minor Scale

## Core Idea
"Knowledge you acquire about one key… effectively equates to knowledge of all keys" — only the
starting pitch changes. Having done C major, the *next* key to learn is its **relative minor,
A minor**, because it uses the identical white-key set. What changes is not the notes but the
**values assigned to them**: A becomes the tonic, so a different chord becomes home, and the
three primary triads become minor rather than major. That single shift is the whole difference
between the bright major tonality and the mellow minor one.

## Frameworks Introduced

### Relative Major / Relative Minor
- **A minor is the relative minor of C major** because it uses the same notes; C major is
  A minor's **relative major**.
- **Rule of thumb: to find the relative minor of a major key, count three notes down.**
- A minor's scale is simply the musical alphabet starting at A: **A B C D E F G**.

### Key versus Mode
- **Key** concerns the *note* upon which a scale is built.
- **Mode** concerns the *type* of scale — major or minor.
- The difference in tone/semitone pattern between major and minor is therefore a difference of
  **mode**, and both are seven-note scales.

| Mode | Pattern |
|---|---|
| **Major mode** | T T S T T T S |
| **Natural minor mode** | T S T T S T T |

Knowing the minor pattern "makes it easy to build a minor scale on any key of the keyboard or
from any fret of the guitar."

### Why the Minor Sounds Mellow
"In the major tonality, the three primary triads (I, IV, and V) are all major, while in the minor
tonality (i, iv, and v) they are all minor. And because the primary triads are the most important
chords in each key, this explains why the minor tonality leans toward more mellow moods."
(Audio Track 25.)

### Three Facts to Remember About Primary Triads
- The **tonic** chord of any key is always built on the **first degree** of either mode.
- The **subdominant** is always built on the **fourth degree** of either mode.
- The **dominant** is always built on the **fifth degree** of either mode.

### The Progression-Building Ladder
1. Play the scale up and down until fluent (same fingering approach as Chapter 3: thumb on A,
   thumb under for the fourth note, reverse on the descent).
2. Work through the **six possible orders of the three primary chords** (Table 9.3).
3. Then develop four-, five-, and six-chord progressions bringing in the **secondary triads
   C, F, and G**.

### Modulation
With the same six common chords you can **change key between C major and A minor and back** —
"if you write your verses in the minor key, for example, you can switch up to the relative major
for the choruses." This is **modulation**, "a useful facility that enables you to create
structurally contrasting sections in your songs."

## Key Concepts
- **Common chords** — major and minor chords collectively; "the staples of most popular music."
  Six learned so far: C, F, G (major) and Am, Dm, Em (minor).
- **Tonic of A minor** — the triad Am (A C E), not C.
- **Arpeggio practice** — Exercise 9 has the student play the arpeggios of the chords of A natural
  minor with given fingerings, as a way of internalizing the chord set.

## Mental Models
- **Same notes, different center of gravity.** "Because the A minor scale uses the same notes as
  the C major scale, both keys share the same chords… The difference between them is how they are
  used."
- **Learn one key deeply, get all keys.** The principles are transposable; only the tonic changes.
- **Mode is a color, key is a position.** Separating these two ideas is what makes the rest of the
  book's scale material tractable.

## Anti-patterns
- Stopping at C major: "it would be a mistake to allow your knowledge to stop with the scale of
  C major."
- Learning a scale without learning its chords: "it is not enough just to learn it and be able to
  play it through. You also need to make an effort to become familiar with the various chords
  within the scale."

## Worked Example
**Exercise 5:** "Which degree of the scale are these notes in A natural minor? D; B; G; F."

From the scale A(1) B(2) C(3) D(4) E(5) F(6) G(7):
- **D = degree 4**, **B = degree 2**, **G = degree 7**, **F = degree 6**.

(Contrast Chapter 3's Exercise 4, which asked the same four notes in C major and got 2, 7, 5, 4 —
the point of the pairing is that the *same notes* take different degrees, and therefore different
functions, in the two relative keys.)

**Exercise 6:** "Fill in the notes required to give the three primary triads of the key of A
natural minor." From Table 9.2:
- Chord i = **A C E**
- Chord iv = **D F A**
- Chord v = **E G B**

**Exercise 1 and 4** answer directly from the chapter text: the relative minor of C major is
**A minor**, found by counting **three** notes down; Major mode = **T T S T T T S**; Natural
minor mode = **T S T T S T T**.

## Key Takeaways
1. A minor is C major's relative minor — same notes, different tonic. Count three notes down from
   a major key to find its relative minor.
2. Key = the starting note; mode = the type of scale.
3. Natural minor pattern: T S T T S T T.
4. Minor primary triads i, iv, v (Am, Dm, Em) are all minor — the reason for the minor mode's
   mellow character.
5. Tonic is always degree 1, subdominant degree 4, dominant degree 5, in either mode.
6. Six common chords (C F G Am Dm Em) let you write in both keys and modulate between them —
   e.g. minor verses, relative-major choruses.
