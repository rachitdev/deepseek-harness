# Chapter 24 — Arpeggiation

## Core Idea
"An arpeggio in its simplest form occurs when **the notes of a chord are played successively**."
Its purpose is always the same: **"to create a sense of motion within the static field of a given
harmony."** Two levers control the result — **non-chord tones**, which add melodic interest without
disturbing the harmony, and **step count**, where deliberately going out of sync with the bar
generates inner melodies for free.

## Frameworks Introduced

### Why Arpeggios Exist
"They arose primarily in relation to instruments such as **pianos and harps that do not have much
sustain**. Through use of arpeggios, one could sustain a harmony through any number of bars."
The modern equivalent is the guitarist's picking technique: "By plucking different strings in
succession, you produce a sense of motion that affects whatever chord is being used at the time."

"As such, you can arpeggiate any chord, **including entire chord progressions themselves**."
(Track 51 gives an arpeggiation moving through a simple four-chord progression.)

### Non-Chord Tones
"A **non-chord tone** is any note that does not form part of the chord. The use of these in arpeggio
patterns is very effective because they are capable of **enhancing the melodic interest** of the
arpeggio."

| Type | Definition | Chapter's example |
|---|---|---|
| **Passing note** | "The note between two chordal tones that are a third apart. Occurring on a **rhythmically weak part of the bar** and safely nestled between two chordal tones, the passing note's use provides melodic interest but does not in itself affect the harmony." | An A minor arpeggio in which **both notes B and D are used as passing notes** — "the use of passing notes does not disturb or affect the implied A minor harmony" |
| **Returning tone** | "The chord tone **rises or falls to the non-chord tone and then immediately returns to it**. This tone is often a step above or below the chord tone." | **Note A used in reference to the chord tone Bb** in a C7 arpeggio |
| **Accented passing note / appoggiatura** | A non-chord tone placed on the **strong** beat instead | In a D minor arpeggio, the non-chord tone "references the D minor harmony **through its resolution to the chord tone F**" |
| **Delayed returning tone** | A returning tone whose return is postponed | "Note E" in the same D minor figure |

**The governing principle:** "these are melody notes that do not disturb the harmony. **As long as
the harmony is sufficiently referenced, you can exercise freedom in where you place these non-chord
tones.**"

For long patterns: "For arpeggios with greater range than an octave, using non-chord tones gives
them musical interest and variety. **The same pattern moving through a chord progression gives a
very musical effect.**"

### Steps in an Arpeggio
"Ordinarily, arpeggios are in **four, eight, sixteen, or thirty-two steps**, which means that the
arpeggio is **in sync with the bar**. However, you can obtain amazing effects with step numbers
**out of sync** with the bar." The generic odd types that "have found their primary application in
various styles of dance music" are **five-step, seven-step, and eleven-step patterns**.

**Why odd step counts work:** "Because the number of steps is odd, **different notes of the
arpeggio are highlighted on the strong points of the measure**. This is a sort of
**wheels-within-wheels effect that generates inner melodies in addition to the arpeggio**."
Figure 24.6 circles the notes that land on the strong points of a five-step pattern — "Notice that
these are different each time."

### The Rhythm-First Rule for Rhythmic Arpeggios
"You can omit notes in order to set up a characteristic rhythm. In terms of rhythmic arpeggios,
**set up the rhythm that you want first on a single note. When you've done so, then introduce the
changes of pitch. This is much easier than trying to do both at the same time.**"

(The same rhythm-before-pitch principle Hewitt gives for motive writing in Chapter 10.)

### The Two-Layer Build
"First, the harmonic foundations need to be laid down — that is, the harmony that is going to be
arpeggiated. When the harmonic foundations have been laid down, you can introduce other notes —
non-chord tones — that enhance the melodic interest of the arpeggio. **Don't worry too much about
what these non-chord tones are called. Basically, they are notes a step up or down from any given
chord tone. As always, let your musical taste guide you.**"

## Key Concepts
- **Arpeggiation = pattern sequencing** — the chapter treats these as the same technique.
- **Hardware/software:** "Whatever software or hardware you use for your musical productions, it is
  likely to have some kind of arpeggiating device. **Propellerhead's Reason offers two nice devices
  specifically for this purpose — the Matrix Pattern Sequencer and the RPG-8 Monophonic
  Arpeggiator.**"
- **Preset dependence is a limit:** "Many synthesizers have an abundance of arpeggio patterns
  preprogrammed and ready for use… However, **dependence upon such preprogrammed patterns can be
  limiting**, and sooner or later you will want to produce your own patterns."
- **Polyrhythm connection:** the odd-step patterns are the arpeggio equivalent of Chapter 14's
  polyrhythm — "cycles of five and seven beats… heard against a cycle of four beats."

## Mental Models
- **Motion inside stasis.** The chord doesn't move; the listener's attention does. "Although the
  chord itself may be static, there will be a strong sense of movement engendered by the arpeggio."
- **Wheels within wheels.** An odd step count against an even bar means the pattern precesses, and
  the accents fall on different chord tones each cycle — an inner melody you did not have to write.
- **Weak beats are free.** A non-chord tone on a weak part of the bar between two chord tones a
  third apart costs the harmony nothing.
- **Separate the problems.** Rhythm on one note first, pitches second.

## Anti-patterns
- Relying on factory arpeggiator presets indefinitely.
- Trying to compose rhythm and pitch contour at once in a rhythmic arpeggio.
- Placing non-chord tones so freely that the harmony is no longer "sufficiently referenced."
- Always using 4/8/16/32 steps and so never getting the precessing accent effect.

## Reference Tables

### Non-chord tone types
| Tone | Position in bar | Motion | Effect on harmony |
|---|---|---|---|
| Passing note | Weak | Steps between two chord tones a third apart | None |
| Returning tone | Either | Step away and immediately back | None |
| Accented passing note (appoggiatura) | **Strong** | Resolves onto a chord tone | References the harmony via its resolution |
| Delayed returning tone | Either | Return postponed | None, if the chord tone is reached |

### Step counts
| Step count | Relationship to the bar | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| 4, 8, 16, 32 | In sync | Stable, predictable accents |
| **5, 7, 11** | Out of sync | Accents precess; inner melodies emerge; dance-music staple |

a simple triad, there are a **limited number of ways in which it can be arpeggiated**."

## Worked Example
**The chapter's own worked figures, reproduced with their analysis:**

**1. A minor arpeggio with passing notes (Figure 24.2).**
Chord tones of Am: **A, C, E**. Between A and C lies **B**; between C and E lies **D**. Both are a
third apart, so both gaps admit a passing note:
> A – **B** – C – **D** – E

"Both notes B and D are used as passing notes… Importantly, the use of passing notes **does not
disturb or affect the implied A minor harmony**." Note that each passing note is placed on a
rhythmically weak part of the bar.

**2. Returning tone in a C7 arpeggio (Figure 24.3).**
Chord tones of C7: **C, E, G, Bb**. The pattern reaches **Bb**, steps down to the non-chord tone
**A**, and immediately returns to Bb. "The characteristic feature of the returning tone is that the
chord tone rises or falls to the non-chord tone and then **immediately returns to it**."

**3. D minor arpeggio with an accented non-chord tone (Figure 24.4).**
Chord tones of Dm: **D, F, A**. Here "the non-chord tone appears **on the strong beat** like a
classical **appoggiatura** or accented passing note. It references the D minor harmony **through
its resolution to the chord tone F**. There is also another non-chord tone — note **E** — that acts
as a **delayed returning tone**."

**4. The five-step pattern (Figure 24.6).**
A five-note pattern played against a bar divided into an even number of steps: each bar, the
pattern starts one position further along, so a different note of the arpeggio lands on each strong
point. Hewitt circles them — "Notice that these are different each time." Seven-step and eleven-step
patterns "work by the same principle."

**Exercise 3:** "List three kinds of non-harmonic tones." From the chapter: **A. passing note**,
**B. returning tone**, **C. accented passing note / appoggiatura** (with the delayed returning tone
as a fourth variant the chapter names).

The remaining exercises are the chapter's applied sequence, reproduced:
1. "Develop and sequence an **eight-step** arpeggio pattern using a resolution of sixteenth notes
   that traces the following two-bar chord progression."
2. "Compose and sequence a **sixteen-step** arpeggio pattern that traces the chord of **A minor**.
   Introduce **at least two passing notes**." (The chapter's own Figure 24.2 shows exactly which two
   are available: B and D.)
4. "Compose and sequence a **32-step** arpeggio pattern that moves through **three octaves** and
   employs two kinds of non-harmonic tones. Build the arpeggio pattern around a **Cmaj7** chord."
5. "Compose and sequence a **five-step** arpeggio pattern that uses an **exotic scale** of your choice."
6. "Compose and sequence a **seven-step** arpeggio pattern that emulates the **acid house bass lines
   popular in the late '80s and early '90s**. Build it around **note E**, making occasional
   incursions to other notes of your choice. **Tip: Once you have sequenced the pattern, experiment
   with the filter frequency and filter resonance to achieve that characteristic acid sound.**"

## Key Takeaways
1. An arpeggio plays a chord's notes successively to create motion within static harmony.
2. You can arpeggiate a chord or an entire progression.
3. Non-chord tones add melodic interest without harming the harmony, provided the harmony stays
   sufficiently referenced.
4. Passing note = between two chord tones a third apart, on a weak beat. Returning tone = step away
   and straight back. On a strong beat it becomes an appoggiatura.
5. Step counts of 4/8/16/32 sync with the bar; 5, 7, and 11 precess and generate inner melodies.
6. For rhythmic arpeggios, set the rhythm on one note first, then add pitches.
7. Build in two layers: harmonic foundation, then melodic non-chord tones.
