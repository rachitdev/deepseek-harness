# Chapter 8 — Chords

## Core Idea
This is the book's pivot chapter. An interval "represents the simplest possible unit of harmony —
in other words, a chord," and whether two notes agree is decided by **the simplicity of their
frequency ratio**: "the simpler the ratio of the two frequencies, the more pleasing the sound is
to the ear," because the two notes share more harmonics. From that acoustic fact Hewitt builds the
full apparatus — concord/discord, perfect/imperfect intervals, triads, Roman numerals, tonality,
primary triads, and cadences.

## Frameworks Introduced

### Concord and Discord by Ratio
- **Perfect concords** — the three intervals with the simplest ratios: **octave 2:1**,
  **perfect fifth 3:2**, **perfect fourth 4:3**. "Along with the octave, they count as true
  universals of the musical language of humanity." In Western theory these were the
  **three perfect consonances**.
- **Imperfect concords** — thirds and sixths; comparatively simple ratios, "a very agreeable sound."
- **Discords** — seconds and sevenths; "a much rougher sound… owing to the complexity of their
  ratios," which produce **beats** from clashing wavelengths.

Crucially, concord/discord is **relative, not absolute**: "Jazz harmony is full of sevenths and
ninths, which traditionally are regarded as being discordant. However, when discord becomes the
norm, as in jazz, the ear comes to accept such discords as being relatively stable harmonies."

### Perfect versus Imperfect Mode
- **Perfect intervals** — fourth, fifth, octave. Fixed; they have only one mode. They "provide
  the stable background structure for chords."
- **Imperfect intervals** — second, third, sixth, seventh. Variable; each can be **major or
  minor**. They "determine whether the chord is major or minor."

### Parallelism and Power Chords
Harmonizing a tune by shadowing it at fourths, fifths, and octaves is **parallelism** — the
harmony of Medieval monastic chant and of traditional Chinese music, "a cliché among writers of
film music who want to create an Oriental atmosphere" (Track 18). Production technique: **tune the
second oscillator a perfect fifth (seven semitones) above the first** for harmony by fifths, or up
a fourth for harmony by fourths — "a strong but empty plaintive kind of sound" (Track 19). The
same interval is the basis of rock **power chords**, popularized by Black Sabbath ("Paranoid") and
Deep Purple ("Black Knight").

### Diabolus in Musica
Build a fifth on every note of the C major scale and one sounds different: **B–F**, a
**diminished fifth**, "very distinctive because of its strange and even eerie sound." It was
officially banned by the monks and called **diabolus musica** — the devil in music. Its
counterpart, the fourth **F–B**, was "the devil's fourth."

### The Triad
"Triads occur when three different notes are sounded simultaneously." In the 14th century the
English monk **Walter Odington** discovered that combining thirds and fifths produces
three-note chords all of whose intervals are concordant.
- **Major triad** = perfect fifth + **major** third = a major third with a minor third stacked on top.
- **Minor triad** = perfect fifth + **minor** third = a minor third with a major third stacked on top.
- The three note names: **root** ("like the foundation of a house… the note by which the triad is
  identified and named"), **third**, **fifth**. Triad of C major = Root C + Third E + Fifth G.
- **The semitone formula:** major third = 4 semitones, minor third = 3 semitones. For a **major**
  chord count up **4 then 3**; for a **minor** chord count up **3 then 4**.
- Emotionally: "the major chord is emotionally bright in comparison to the darker, moodier minor chord."
- Modern rule for sonorities: "Any note may be combined with any other note."

### Roman Numeral Labelling
Each scale degree is the root of a triad. **Uppercase Roman numeral = major triad; lowercase =
minor triad.** Popular songbooks add guitar chord symbols.

### Tonality, Function, and Cadence
- **Tonality** raises one chord above all others: the **tonic**, built on degree 1 — "the home
  chord." Progressions "tend to arise from it and return to it at the end."
- The **dominant** is always on **degree 5**. "Tonic and dominant chords represent the passive and
  active poles of tonality." Progressions head from the tonic pole to the dominant pole and then
  return.
- The **subdominant** is on **degree 4**. "The name of the subdominant reveals its function: It
  serves as a counterbalance to the dominant. Its fifth, note C, tends to reinforce and back up
  the tonic. This serves to stabilize chord progressions and give them a sense of balance."
- **Three primary triads = I, IV, V.** "Their job is to lay the foundations for the tonality of any
  particular key," and "between them, the primary chords define all seven notes of the scale."
- **Secondary triads** = all the minor chords in the key: Dm, Em, Am (ii, iii, vi).
- **Cadence** — "the way in which a chord progression is satisfactorily brought to a close." Two
  basic types: the **perfect cadence, V to I** (Track 22), which "alone creates a complete sense of
  closure"; and the **plagal or "Amen" cadence, IV to I**, named for the hymns that end that way,
  with "a nice mellow feel to it, a feeling of settling upon the tonic triad."
- Structural rule of thumb: "The last chord of a progression would usually be I, and the
  second-to-last chord would be IV or V."

## Key Concepts
- **Sonority** — any simultaneous combination of notes; the interval is the simplest sonority,
  then the triad.
- **Beats (acoustic)** — the roughness produced by complex ratios; the physical cause of discord.
- **Three-chord rock** — songs built entirely on I, IV, V. Hewitt's examples: The Troggs' "Wild
  Thing," The Clash's "Should I Stay or Should I Go," Led Zeppelin's "Rock and Roll." I, IV, V
  also underpin the **12-bar blues** progression.
- **Amen-cadence song example:** Bob Marley's "No Woman No Cry." **Three-primary-chord song
  example:** Bob Dylan's "Blowing in the Wind."
- **Psycho stab** — minor seconds (one semitone) used as string stabs in the shower scene; "since
  then, their use has become a horror-movie standard."

## Mental Models
- **Intervals are paint.** "Intervals are for the musician what the colors of the palette are for
  the visual artist."
- **Shared harmonics = agreement.** "The ear very cleverly compares these notes: The more the
  harmonics they share in common, the better the two notes seem to blend."
- **Leaving home and coming back.** "Tonal chord progressions are analogous to, say, going out
  from home in the morning, doing your activities, and coming home again. Without the process of
  coming home, you would feel incomplete."
- **Building, not stacking randomly.** "In a chord you are piling up a stack of musical notes to
  be heard at the same time. Hopefully, you will put together that stack using tangible principles."
- **You already know this subconsciously.** "We are all unknowing experts on the subject of chord
  progressions… we can often spot a single wrong note among what may be thousands." Frustration
  comes from "a lack of clear knowledge of underlying principles."

## Key Takeaways
1. Simpler frequency ratio → more shared harmonics → more concordant.
2. Three perfect concords (8ve 2:1, 5th 3:2, 4th 4:3); thirds and sixths are imperfect concords;
   seconds and sevenths are discords. Concord/discord is style-relative.
3. Perfect intervals have one mode; imperfect intervals are major or minor and decide chord quality.
4. Major triad = 4 semitones then 3; minor triad = 3 then 4.
5. C major yields I C, ii Dm, iii Em, IV F, V G, vi Am, vii B° — uppercase major, lowercase minor.
6. Tonic (I) = home, dominant (V) = active pole, subdominant (IV) = counterbalance. Together the
   three primary triads cover all seven scale notes.
7. Two cadences: perfect V–I (full closure) and plagal IV–I (mellow settling).
