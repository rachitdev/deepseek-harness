# Chapter 21 — Chords of the Seventh

## Core Idea
"You obtain a chord of the seventh when you add to the root, third, and fifth of any of the
aforementioned triads a fourth note that is a **seventh above the root of the chord**. This
seventh is usually taken from the scale or key in which the triad occurs." The seventh is a
**dissonance**, and the whole chapter is about the two legitimate ways to handle that dissonance
— **resolve it stepwise** (the classical way) or **saturate the texture with sevenths until the
ear accepts them as stable** (the jazz way). "Both methods work equally well, provided that they
occur in context."

## Frameworks Introduced

### Building a Seventh Chord (the two-step procedure)
1. **Establish the triad** of root, third, and fifth.
2. **Add the seventh above the root, being careful to stay within the chosen scale.**

"From the root, count up seven notes within that scale. This locates the seventh, which is then
added to the triad to form a four-note sonority or **tetrad**."

### Two Treatments of the Seventh's Dissonance
| Treatment | Method | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Stepwise resolution** (classical) | "The seventh is resolved stepwise to the nearest point of concord" — usually **downward to a consonant third** | John Lennon's "Imagine" uses **Cmaj7–F** |
| **Chained resolutions** | "There is no rule that says the next chord cannot be a seventh either." The resolution is embedded in an Fmaj7, "which in turn sets up another dissonance." "Chains of seventh chords would be set up, each one serving to resolve the other" | Favored by classical composers |
| **Saturation** (jazz) | "Place it in an environment where sevenths are so abundant that the ear accepts them as being stable sonorities" | Erik Satie's "Gymnopédie No. 1" (Track 45); jazz generally, where "all sorts of what were in classical times viewed to be dissonant sonorities are strung together without a second thought" |

### Major versus Minor Seventh
| Seventh type | Semitones | Character |
|---|---|---|
| **Major seventh** | 11 | "Quite a sharp dissonance" |
| **Minor seventh** | 10 | "Although dissonant, is much mellower in its sound" |

**Naming convention:** "Because the seventh chords… are mostly minor sevenths, convention rules
that these are simply represented by a **7**. In contrast, major seventh chords require
'**maj7**' after the letter — for example, Fmaj7. The abbreviation **Cm7** would thus signify a C
minor chord with a minor seventh added, while **Cm/maj7** would signify a C minor chord with a
**major** seventh added."

### Spacing and Doubling a Four-Note Chord
"Because seventh chords have four notes, they offer more opportunities for interesting types of
chordal spacing." The chapter's four worked voicings of Cmaj7 (Figure 21.6, Track 44):
- **A — closed position:** notes crowded together as closely as possible.
- **B — five parts, third doubled:** "This wide spread gives a rich, balanced sonority."
- **C — one note omitted:** "This is generally the **fifth**… for the **seventh and the third are
  the intervals that define the chord in its essential aspect**. To make the harmony fuller, the
  root is doubled in the octave below."
- **D — seven parts, very wide-open sonority:** root doubled three times, third doubled twice.

### Four Positions (four notes → four positions)
| Position | Bass note | Symbol |
|---|---|---|
| Root position | C (root) | **Cmaj7** |
| First inversion | E (third) | **Cmaj7/E** |
| Second inversion | G (fifth) | **Cmaj7/G** |
| Third inversion | B (seventh) | **Cmaj7/B** |

### The Dominant Seventh and Why It Works
"The most popular and frequently used seventh chord is the **dominant seventh chord**, which
occurs on the **fifth degree of either the major or the minor scale** (chord G7)." "For this
reason, the chords of C and G7 are often the first two chords that guitarists learn to play."

The mechanism of V7 → I (Figure 21.9):
- (a) The **tritone** in the chord — in C major, between **B and F** — resolves: "the note B (the
  leading note) passes smoothly up to the tonic, and the note F falls down to the third of the scale."
- (b) Combined with **perfect fifth root movement** from dominant to tonic degree,
- (c) "a powerful cadence results."

"When a seventh is added to the dominant triad, the perfect cadence is even more incisive and final."

### Modulation
"Because of the strength of resolution to the tonic triad, the dominant seventh chord has always
represented the main agent for a change of key in a song — called **modulation**. It is important
to remember that **when you are trying to change key, the target chord is the dominant seventh of
the new key, rather than the tonic**. This is because the ear will only accept that a modulation
has occurred when it registers a **perfect cadence in the new key**. Without such a cadence, the
ear will register only a transitory shift toward that key."

Two consequences Hewitt draws:
- **Why the minor mode gets altered:** "It does not possess the requisite chord for a perfect
  cadence. So it needs to be chromatically altered in order to have one." (The harmonic minor.)
- **The central problem of modal harmony:** "To achieve success in modal harmony, you must **avoid
  at all costs any semblance of a perfect cadence** because when it does occur, the ear will
  automatically focus upon the implied tonic toward which the cadence occurs. And the Ionian mode
  is the only mode in which this is desirable."

### The Introduction Route for Seventh Chords
"Probably the best way to learn to use seventh chords is to **insert them between triads so that
the seventh resolves in the next triad**. A good example of this is to go **Dm – Dm7 – G**." Here
"the seventh of the chord ii (note C) appears as a **passing note** between two triad notes — the
D of the Dm triad and the B of the G triad." Then progress to progressions in which all chords
are sevenths, "using the principles of root movement to assist this process."

### Sevenths in the Minor Complex
"The sevenths for the natural minor scale are the same as those present in the relative major
scale… the difference between the two systems is one of emphasis — where the tonic chord is placed."
Across the whole minor complex (natural + harmonic + melodic) there are more triads to extend, so
a single degree can offer several options. On degree two of A minor you might use **B°7 or Bm7**.
"The answer depends entirely on the context. Ultimately, your ear must be the final arbiter."
Hewitt's advice: **learn to handle the dominant seventh of the minor key first** (it is the same
chord as on degree five of the major scale), then incorporate the others.

## Worked Example
**The chapter's own construction of Cmaj7** (Figures 21.2–21.3), then the V7–I cadence
(Figure 21.9):

Step 1 — the triad on degree 1 of C major: **C E G**.
Step 2 — count up seven notes within the scale from C: C(1) D(2) E(3) F(4) G(5) A(6) **B(7)**.
Add B: **C E G B = Cmaj7**, a four-note tetrad.

The V7 → I resolution, worked:
| | Chord | Notes | What moves |
|---|---|---|---|
| V7 | **G7** | G **B** D **F** | B and F form the **tritone** |
| ↓ | | | B (leading note) rises a semitone to **C**; F falls a semitone to **E** (the third of the tonic) |
| I | **C** | C E G | Root moves G → C, a perfect fifth |

"Combined with the perfect fifth root movement from dominant to tonic degree, a powerful cadence
results."

## Anti-patterns
- Leaving a seventh unresolved in a context where sevenths are otherwise rare — the ear hears an
  unfinished dissonance rather than a color.
- Omitting the **third or the seventh** when trimming a four-note chord; omit the fifth instead —
  the third and seventh "define the chord in its essential aspect."
- Aiming at the new **tonic** when modulating instead of the new key's **dominant seventh**.
- Allowing a perfect cadence to slip into modal writing — it collapses the mode back into a key.

## Key Takeaways
1. Seventh chord = triad + the seventh above the root, taken from the scale. Two steps, always.
2. Major seventh = 11 semitones (sharp dissonance); minor seventh = 10 (mellow). Symbols: maj7 vs
   plain 7.
3. Two valid treatments of the dissonance: stepwise resolution (classical) or saturation (jazz).
4. Four notes = four positions; slash notation names the inversion. Trim the fifth, never the
   third or seventh.
5. V7 → I is the strongest cadence because the tritone resolves outward *and* the root moves a
   perfect fifth.
6. Modulation targets the **dominant seventh of the new key**; the ear needs a perfect cadence
   there to accept the change.
7. Introduce sevenths by inserting them between triads (Dm – Dm7 – G) before writing all-seventh
   progressions.
