# Chapter 7 — Meter

## Core Idea
Meter "concerns the way beats are combined into larger units or groups that make sense to the
ear." Each unit is a **metric cycle**, **measure**, or **bar**. A metric cycle is fundamentally a
**pattern of stresses** — strong, medium, weak — that repeats and thereby gives the listener a
framework for feeling the beat. Metric cycles "are as important to the sense of rhythm in your
music as a musical scale is to your melodies and harmonies."

## Frameworks Introduced

### The Three Basic Metric Cycles
| Meter | Grouping | Stress pattern |
|---|---|---|
| **Duple time** | Twos | s w \| s w |
| **Triple time** | Threes | s w m \| s w m |
| **Quadruple time** | Fours | s w m w \| s w m w |

(s = strong, m = medium, w = weak.) Quadruple is "the most popular grouping of all — the one used
in most tracks today."

### Time Signatures
Two numbers:
- **Upper number (numerator)** — how many beats in the metric cycle.
- **Lower number (denominator)** — the value of those beats (halves, quarters, eighths…).

Placed at the very beginning of the staff, **after the clef**; not repeated in subsequent bars
unless the signature changes. Reason's default is **4/4** — "a useful default because most music
producers tend to prefer to work in 4/4. It is an easy, convenient pattern to work with, and it
displays nice properties of balance and symmetry."

### Simple versus Compound Time
Duple, triple, and quadruple are **simple** time signatures. **When each beat of a simple time
signature is subdivided into three, the equivalent compound time signature is obtained.**

| Compound meter | Time signature | Beats |
|---|---|---|
| Compound duple | 6/8 | 6 |
| Compound triple | 9/8 | 9 |
| Compound quadruple | 12/8 | 12 |

"The division of each beat into three gives the rhythm a swinging, moving quality." Famous
example: J. S. Bach's "Sheep May Safely Graze," in 9/8. Audio Track 13 contrasts the three.

### Syncopation
Meter sets up an expectation (1 2 3 4 with strong beats in bold); syncopation plays on it by
placing stress on a normally **weak** point of the bar. "You can use syncopation to generate
energy and excitement in the rhythm. Its use is prevalent in all styles of electronic music —
especially the faster, more upbeat styles, such as dance, breakbeat, drum and bass."
Figure 7.13's syncopated kick pattern is "a commonly used alternative to the regular four-square
beat of the kick drum found in dance music. It is syncopated because the second, third, and
fourth beats arrive a quaver later than expected." (Audio Track 14.)

### Rhythmic Motives
"A rhythmic motive is a short, identifiable unit of rhythm." The example in Figure 7.14 —
**two sixteenths followed by an eighth**, repeated through the bar in quadruple time — is
"extremely popular with dance-music writers" and "creates a strong, driving rhythmic energy."
Motives are often transformed into variants for variety (Figure 7.15 gives Motive A and its main
variant in compound quadruple time). Figure 7.16 is a **syncopated Cuban jazz motive**, which
uses a **tie**: "the value of the two notes is added together — in other words, the second note
of the pair does not sound."

Method for acquiring motives: listen to music with an ear for the motives used → work the motive
out in note values → use it in your own compositions. Particular genres use characteristic motives.

### Triplets, Shuffle, Cross-Rhythm
- **Triplet** — three notes played in the time of two. Three triplet quavers = one quarter note.
  On hi-hat sixteenths they "generate a sense of more pace as three sixteenths are being heard in
  the space of two"; often used in drum and bass (Track 15).
- **Shuffle rhythm** — "when the central note of each triplet is missed." Originating in jazz and
  swing, it now "pervades all types of modern music, from dance to popular" (Track 16). To get a
  shuffle on a drum machine you must select **triplet resolution**.
- **Cross-rhythm** — triplets played against pairs; "three notes in one part being heard in the
  same time as two notes of another part." In classical times this specific cross-rhythm was
  called **hemiola** (Track 17). In drum and bass: tambourine on sixteenth triplets against
  closed hi-hats on straight sixteenths. "In East African drumming, cross-rhythm has been
  developed to a fine art, and it is no rare thing to hear some really dense rhythms in which two
  is played against three which is played against five."

### Triplet Resolution by Software
| Software | How triplets are named |
|---|---|
| **Logic** | Eighth-note triplets are called **twelfths**; sixteenth-note triplets are called **twenty-fourths** |
| **Cakewalk SONAR** | Referred to directly as **triplets** |
| **Reason** | The note value followed by a **T** (Redrum offers 1/8T and 1/16T) |

Redrum also has a **Shuffle button** — press it and shuffle is applied automatically to all pairs
of sixteenth notes, with no resolution change needed; the degree of shuffle is adjustable ("the
more shuffle you apply, the greater the value of the first note of the pair and the lesser the
value of the second").

## Key Concepts
- **Bar line** — the vertical line drawn on the score after each completed metric cycle; the term
  "bar" arose from this practice.
- **Free time** — music with no sense of meter. Hewitt's rare example: **Christian plainchant**,
  whose "gentle melodies simply meander from one note to another with no sense of beat, stress,
  or fixed pulse."
- **Asymmetric meters** — odd-numbered beats to the bar such as **7/8 or 11/4**; deferred to
  Chapter 14 as a more advanced concept, arising in electronic music from Eastern and Middle
  Eastern influence.
- **Tie** — joins two notes into one duration; the second does not re-sound.

## Mental Models
- **A conga tells you what meter is.** "Some of the beats will be louder, and some will be
  softer. A metric cycle is a pattern of these stresses that makes sense to and satisfies the ear."
- **Expectation is the raw material.** Meter builds an expectation; syncopation, cross-rhythm and
  the shuffle all get their effect by violating it in a controlled way. Techniques of this kind
  "depend upon the presence of such a cycle" — which is why most music is metric.
- **Two against three.** Cross-rhythm is a layering strategy, not a single-part device: the
  interest comes from the friction between simultaneous divisions.

## Anti-patterns
- Placing stresses randomly instead of learning the metric cycles first — "Having learned these,
  you then arrive at a position where you can start to experiment with placing stresses on
  unusual or unexpected points in that cycle."
- Attempting a shuffle at straight resolution on a drum machine (unless the machine has a
  dedicated shuffle control).
- Neglecting cross-rhythm — Hewitt notes it "seems to be neglected in modern electronic music,
  although one that would be well worth experimenting with."

## Worked Example
**Exercise 8a:** "List the rhythmic motives that can be devised from two sixteenth notes and one
eighth note, with each value appearing only once. 1. ___ 2. ___ 3. ___"

Working it out — the eighth note can sit in one of three positions relative to the two sixteenths:
1. **16th – 16th – 8th** (the driving dance motive of Figure 7.14)
2. **16th – 8th – 16th**
3. **8th – 16th – 16th**

Each fills one quarter-note beat. **Exercise 8b** then asks you to "compose a four-bar rhythm in
quadruple metre that uses at least two of these motives."

**Exercise 9:** "What note values are the following triplets equivalent to?" — applying the
chapter's own rule that three triplet quavers equal one quarter note: a triplet of three eighths
= **one quarter note**; a triplet of three sixteenths = **one eighth note**; a triplet of three
quarters = **one half note**.

**Exercise 3:** "A time signature consists of ___ numbers — an upper number that is the ___ and a
lower number that is the ___. The upper number tells you ___ beats there are in a bar, while the
lower number tells you their ___." From the chapter: **two**; **numerator**; **denominator**;
**how many**; **value**.

## Key Takeaways
1. Meter = the repeating pattern of strong/medium/weak stresses that groups beats into bars.
2. Three simple meters: duple, triple, quadruple. Quadruple (4/4) dominates modern production.
3. Divide each simple beat by three to get compound time: 6/8, 9/8, 12/8 — swinging, moving quality.
4. Syncopation = stress on a normally weak point; it is the main energy generator in dance rhythm.
5. Rhythmic motives are short, reusable rhythm cells; two sixteenths + one eighth is the classic
   dance-music motive.
6. Triplets = 3 in the time of 2; drop the middle one for a shuffle; layer them against straight
   notes for cross-rhythm (hemiola).
7. Know how your DAW names triplets — Logic's "twelfths" and "twenty-fourths" are eighth and
   sixteenth triplets.
