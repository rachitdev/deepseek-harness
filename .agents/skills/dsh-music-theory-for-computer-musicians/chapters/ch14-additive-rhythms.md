# Chapter 14 — Additive Rhythms

## Core Idea
Western rhythmic theory is **divisive**: you take a beat and subdivide it into twos and threes,
which yields the simple and compound time signatures of Chapter 7 — and which means "time
signatures such as 7/8 or 11/4 just do not figure in this system. In this sense, the theory lies
behind the practice." The alternative is **additive rhythm**: take a short beat (say a sixteenth)
and **multiply** it into cells of **two** and **three**, then string cells together. "The
principle of additive rhythm, in other words, generates a virtual infinity of metric groupings."

## Frameworks Introduced

### Divisive versus Additive
| | Divisive (Western/classical) | Additive (modern/world) |
|---|---|---|
| Method | Subdivide a beat into 2s and 3s | Multiply a short value into groups of 2 and 3, then add groups |
| Yields | Simple and compound time signatures | Any grouping, including 5, 7, 10, 11, 13, 15 |
| Where 7/8 sits | Does not figure | Natural result |

### The Two Cells
Everything is built from two rhythmic cells:

| Cell | Beats | Stress |
|---|---|---|
| **A — duple** | 1 2 | s w |
| **B — triple** | 1 2 3 | s w m |

### Combination Arithmetic
- **Two cells → 4 possibilities:** 2+2, 2+3, 3+2, 3+3.
  - 2+2 = four beats (familiar).
  - **2+3 and 3+2 both give five beats — quintuple time — "a time signature that has no place or
    origin within conventional simple and compound rhythms."**
- **Three cells → 8 possibilities** (Table 14.1).
- **Four cells → 16 possibilities; five cells → 32**, and so on.

### Reading an Odd Meter as a Cell String
"Consider 13/8 — easy! It is just **3+3+2+3+2**, a combination of five rhythmic cells."

### Additive Rhythm *Inside* 4/4
"Even if you prefer writing in 4/4, additive rhythms can still add a lot to your music. Cuban and
Latin American music uses additive rhythms all the time within a 4/4 framework. This is achieved
by using combinations of two and three beats that fit into 4/4."
- Totalling eight: **3+3+2**.
- Totalling sixteen: **3+3+3+3+2+2 = 16** — "used frequently in many styles of dance music."

### Polyrhythm
"How about combining different rhythms together? This is called **polyrhythm**" — treated in
Chapter 24, where "cycles of five and seven beats are heard against a cycle of four beats. These
are called **five-step and seven-step patterns**."

### Additive Rhythm in Melody
"Additive rhythms do not just have to be a feature of your rhythm tracks. They also offer
fascinating ways to string together melodic motives. **Track 35** of the audio CD is a simple sine
wave lead that puts together motives within a framework of **15 beats to the bar**. Have a listen,
and you will discover that 13, 15, 17, or any other type of unusual combination does actually
sound effective."

## Key Concepts
- **Asymmetric metric cycles** — 7/8, 10/8, 11/4 and their kin, standard in India and the Middle
  East, where musicians "think nothing of using more exotic groupings of beats."
- **House skip** — the name Hewitt gives to one of the practical hand-drum rhythms in the exercise set.
- **Rhythmic cell** — the two- or three-unit building block.

## Mental Models
- **Why 4/4 dominates is not a musical argument.** "Why 4/4 is used so often in Western music is
  something of a mystery. Perhaps it has more to do with habit than anything else."
- **Add, don't divide.** The mental switch from "how do I cut this bar up?" to "which cells do I
  chain?" is what makes 5, 7, 11, and 13 feel natural rather than exotic.
- **Odd meters are just cell strings.** Any unusual signature decomposes into 2s and 3s; find the
  decomposition and it becomes playable.

## Anti-patterns
- Staying inside 4/4 by default: "You no longer have to remain within the stricture of 4/4 all of
  the time."
- Trying to *feel* an odd meter without a count. Hewitt's advice: "When practicing these exercises,
  set up a count — either out loud or, if you are more experienced, in your head. Once you have a
  clear and regular count set up, you can think about the coordination of your hands."

### Practical hand-drum exercise notation
"L" signifies a left-hand beat, "R" signifies a right-hand beat; the tempo is flexible. Four

have no hand drum, "use whatever is on hand — even a tabletop will do."

## Worked Example
**Exercise 3** is the chapter's central drill, and the book supplies the first case complete as
the model:

> "Complete the following. **7/8 can consist of the following groupings of two and three:
> A. 2 plus 3 plus 2; B. 2 plus 2 plus 3; C. 3 plus 2 plus 2.**
> 11/8 can consist of the following groupings of two and three. (Give four possible different
> types of groupings.) …
> 8/8 can consist of the following groupings of two and three: A ___ B ___ C ___"

The 7/8 answers are the three AAB permutations from Table 14.1. Extending the same method:

**11/8** — eleven is reached by 2+3+3+3 or 3+3+3+2 (four cells) or 2+2+2+2+3 (five cells).
Four valid groupings:
- A. **3 + 3 + 3 + 2**
- B. **2 + 3 + 3 + 3**
- C. **3 + 2 + 3 + 3**
- D. **3 + 3 + 2 + 3**

**8/8** — Table 14.1's own three-cell rows totalling eight give the answer directly:
- A. **2 + 3 + 3**
- B. **3 + 2 + 3**
- C. **3 + 3 + 2**

**The 13/8 decomposition** the chapter works in full: 13/8 = **3 + 3 + 2 + 3 + 2**, five rhythmic
cells (Figure 14.3).

**Exercise 4** applies additive meters to melody with specified keys, tying the chapter back to the
scale material: "A. 7/8 in the key of C major. B. 5/8 in the key of A natural minor. C. 13/8 in
the key of A harmonic minor."

(The book's answer sheet was a separate download from www.courseptr.com/downloads; the groupings
above are worked from Table 14.1 and the chapter's own method.)

## Key Takeaways
1. Divisive rhythm subdivides; additive rhythm chains cells of 2 and 3. The latter is how odd
   meters actually work.
2. Two cells give 4 groupings, three give 8, four give 16, five give 32 — a virtual infinity of meters.
3. 2+3 and 3+2 give quintuple time, which divisive theory cannot produce.
4. Decompose any odd signature into 2s and 3s: 13/8 = 3+3+2+3+2.
5. Additive grouping works *inside* 4/4 too — 3+3+2 over eight, or 3+3+3+3+2+2 over sixteen (a
   dance-music staple).
6. Set up a steady count before attempting the hand coordination.
7. Additive framing also organizes melodic motives, not just drums.
