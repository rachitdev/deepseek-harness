# Chapter 23 — Complex Harmony

## Core Idea
Chords are built by the **chain of thirds**: a triad is two stacked thirds, a seventh chord is
three, and you can keep going — ninth, eleventh, thirteenth. "The chain of thirds stops with a
thirteenth because a fifteenth would bring you back to the starting note." With five, six, and
seven notes the central question stops being what to add and becomes **what to leave out** — and
the one thing that keeps dense harmony intelligible is a strong scheme of root movement.
"Provided that the root movement scheme is clear, the harmony's sense of direction will be retained."

## Frameworks Introduced

### The Chain of Thirds
| Chord | Thirds stacked | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Triad | 2 | 3 |
| Seventh | 3 | 4 |
| **Ninth** | 4 | 5 |
| **Eleventh** | 5 | 6 |
| **Thirteenth** | 6 | 7 — "theoretically involve all seven notes of the scale" |

"A thirteenth, after all, uses all seven notes of the scale at the same time!"

### Essential Notes — What Defines Each Chord
| Chord | Essential members | Notes usually omitted |
|---|---|---|
| **Ninth** | "The factors that qualify it to be a ninth chord are the **seventh and the ninth**. Without the seventh, it would be construed as a simple **added ninth** chord." | "When scored for four parts, **the fifth is usually omitted** because it is the least important chord member." |
| **Eleventh** | "The most important notes are the **root, third, seventh, and eleventh**." "To qualify as an eleventh chord, it needs at least a **root, a seventh, and an eleventh**." | Can be effectively scored in four parts |
| **Thirteenth** | "The most essential elements are the **root, the seventh, and the thirteenth**." | "The **third** is generally avoided because it clashes with the eleventh. If it is included, the eleventh tends to be sharpened by way of chromatic alteration." Thicken by adding the eleventh and fifth. |

### Chromatic Alteration
"The alteration of a chord using a note not in the scale… is called **chromatic alteration**, and
it is a common practice in all types of modern music that use complex harmonies."

Worked case: in Cmaj11 (C E G B D F) "there is a clash between the third of the triad (note E) and
the eleventh (note F). Because the core foundations for the harmony have already been laid by the
triad and seventh, the offending note — the eleventh — is often **sharpened** to give an eleventh
chord that would normally be found in the **Lydian mode: C E G B D F#**. This results in a very
beautiful harmony."

**The dominant is the chord most vulnerable to chromatic alteration.** "Provided that the third
and seventh are present to maintain the chord's dominant pull, you can apply a wide variety of
chromatic colorings. Principally, these apply to the **fifth, seventh, ninth, and eleventh**." The
result is "a great number of complex sonorities, many of which are the staples of the jazz
musician's diet."

### Borrowing from Other Modes
"Another common practice with complex harmony is **borrowing from other modes**. Here root
movement rules the day, which means that there is nothing wrong with borrowing notes and even
whole chords from other keys and modes." Figure 23.16's example: "the root is clearly static, and
the sense of change comes from the alternation between the **tonic eleventh of the major and
parallel minor mode**."

### Parallel Ninths
"Ninth chords work extremely well in **parallel textures**… To achieve this type of harmony, try
**sampling a single ninth chord and then playing it on different keys of your keyboard**." (Same
principle as the parallel fifths of Chapter 8.) Figure 23.12's roots move **D → F → G → C** — "This
strength of root movement serves as an anchor for the ears' efforts to interpret the complex harmony."

### The Study Method (repeated for each chord class)
Hewitt gives essentially the same four-step method three times:
1. **Investigate a single chord.** Try it in different inversions and types of spacing.
2. **Study its intervallic makeup** and get to know its particular sound; examine its properties
   when certain notes are removed.
3. **Look at the same chord type built on each degree of the scale**, revealing the different
   varieties.
4. **Compose progressions** with them. "Doing this will put them on your palette of available
   chords. Once you have them there, your ear will guide you the rest of the way."

For thirteenths specifically: "Sequence a single chord sustained for, say, eight bars, and then
experiment by taking certain notes out, swapping notes around between different registers, and so
on. In other words, **have fun**! … the sound will take over and readily suggest how it should be
progressed. **There are no rules for such complex harmony, simply because the possibilities are so
numerous.**"

## Key Concepts
- **Ninths as passing notes** — "Chords of the ninth originally occurred in classical music, often
  resulting from the use of **passing notes** between ordinary triad tones." Figure 23.4: Cmaj9
  appears briefly between the triads of C and F, the discord resolving on the fifth of the F chord.
  "Modern use is not governed by any particular rules. You can use ninths freely to spice up triadic harmony."
- **Dominant ninth** — built on the fifth degree; "generally… used to enhance the resolution of
  dominant seventh chord to the tonic."
- **Dominant minor ninth (Cb9)** — in the harmonic minor mode the ninth is minor, "an interval that
  has a much sharper bite," notated as a flat ninth. "Not often used in a simple popular music
  context because it has a particular dramatic quality that **made it a favorite of silent-movie
  piano players!**"
- **Minor ninth chords in production** — "Minor chords with a ninth have a beautiful mellow quality
  that is often exploited in **deep and funky house, Detroit techno, nu jazz, and ambient
  textures**. The most commonly used chords are the chord of **m9 as it occurs on the second degree
  of the major scale (that is, Dm9 in C major) and the sixth degree (that is, Am9). In the relative
  minor key, these same chords are the tonic ninth and the subdominant ninth. Together, they form
  a beautiful pair of chords that work very well with one another.**"
- **The iii ninth** — "The ninth on the third degree is not used very often because it has a minor
  ninth that gives it an unpleasant sound. If used, it tends to be altered so that the ninth is major."
- **Thirteenths in ambient** — "Such harmonies are a strong feature of **ambient music**, especially
  when gentle pads, strings, and such are involved. **Usually no more than two or three chords are
  involved.**"

## Mental Models
- **Subtraction, not addition.** "The central problem with a thirteenth chord is **what to leave in
  and what to leave out**." Every extended chord has two or three notes that define it and the rest
  are optional color.
- **Root movement is the anchor.** "With complex harmony, there is a great degree of freedom in
  terms of what you can do, **provided that there is a really strong sense of root movement in the
  chord progression**."
- **Dissonance has direction.** "The dissonant seventh gives the chord punch and bite… the seventh
  chord also acquires a **leaning — a desire for resolution** that gives chord progressions that
  use it great power and momentum." Extensions intensify that leaning.
- **Approach chords.** The dominant thirteenth's "principal job" is as "an **approach to the
  dominant seventh or ninth chord**."

## Anti-patterns
- Playing extended chords with every note present — the sound becomes unreadably dense.
- Omitting the **seventh** from a "ninth" chord: without it "it would be construed as a simple
  added ninth chord."
- Leaving a natural eleventh clashing against the third — sharpen the eleventh (Lydian) or drop
  the third.
- Using the iii ninth unaltered because of its minor ninth.
- Piling on complex harmony over vague root movement — the ear has nothing to hold on to.

## Worked Example
**Exercise 1**, the chapter's own drill, with its own tip: "Build the triad first. Then, to each
triad, add a seventh and a ninth in that order."

Applied to **C natural minor (C D Eb F G Ab Bb C)**:

| Degree | Triad | + 7th | + 9th | Chord |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| I | C Eb G | Bb | D | **Cm9** |
| II | D F Ab | C | Eb | **Dm7♭5 ♭9** |
| III | Eb G Bb | D | F | **Ebmaj9** |
| IV | F Ab C | Eb | G | **Fm9** |
| V | G Bb D | F | Ab | **Gm7 ♭9** |
| VI | Ab C Eb | G | Bb | **Abmaj9** |
| VII | Bb D F | Ab | C | **Bb9** |

**Exercise 2** — the five positions of a five-note chord, obtained by successively moving the
lowest note up an octave (Chapter 13's procedure extended):

| Position | **Cmaj9** (C E G B D) | **Fm9** (F Ab C Eb G) |
|---|---|---|
| Root position | C E G B D | F Ab C Eb G |
| First inversion | E G B D C | Ab C Eb G F |
| Second inversion | G B D C E | C Eb G F Ab |
| Third inversion | B D C E G | Eb G F Ab C |
| Fourth inversion | D C E G B | G F Ab C Eb |

**Exercise 3 — dominant ninth chords** (built on degree V of each key, root + third + fifth +
seventh + ninth):
- A. **G minor** → dominant is D; with the harmonic-minor sharp seventh: **D F# A C Eb** (D7♭9)
- B. **D major** → dominant is A: **A C# E G B** (A9)
- C. **Bb minor** → dominant is F; sharp seventh: **F A C Eb Gb** (F7♭9)
- D. **E major** → dominant is B: **B D# F# A C#** (B9)

Note the pattern: in **minor** keys the dominant ninth comes out as a **flat ninth** — the
"silent-movie" chord the chapter describes.

**Exercise 5 — four chromatic alterations of the dominant eleventh G B D F A C**, following the
chapter's rule that the third (B) and seventh (F) stay put while the fifth, ninth, and eleventh
are colored:
- A. **G B D♭ F A C** (flattened fifth)
- B. **G B D♯ F A C** (sharpened/augmented fifth)
- C. **G B D F A♭ C** (flattened ninth)
- D. **G B D F A C♯** (sharpened eleventh — the Lydian alteration, which also removes the B/C clash)

**The chapter's own worked alteration**, reproduced: Cmaj11 = **C E G B D F**; E and F clash a
semitone apart; sharpen the eleventh → **C E G B D F#**, the Lydian eleventh — "a very beautiful
harmony."

## Key Takeaways
1. The chain of thirds generates triad → seventh → ninth → eleventh → thirteenth, and stops there.
2. Ninth = 5 notes, defined by the **seventh and ninth**; drop the fifth first.
3. Eleventh = 6 notes, defined by **root, third, seventh, eleventh**; scoreable in four parts.
4. Thirteenth = 7 notes (the whole scale), defined by **root, seventh, thirteenth**; avoid the third.
5. Chromatic alteration is standard practice; the dominant tolerates it best as long as the third
   and seventh survive.
6. Sharpen the eleventh (Lydian) to remove its clash with the third.
7. Minor ninth chords (Dm9, Am9) are the house/techno/nu-jazz/ambient staple pair.
8. Dense harmony works only over strong root movement.
