# Chapter 18 — Chord Progressions and Root Movement

## Core Idea
"For the ear, the most important feature of chord progressions is the sense of **root movement**…
the identifiable movement between the roots of different chords. If the root movement is strong,
clear, and logical, then so will be the chord progression upon which it is based." And there are
only **three kinds** — by fourths, by thirds, by seconds — which means "the principles of root
movement reduce the choices from any given chord to three."

## Frameworks Introduced

### Root Movement Is the Definition of Progression
Hewitt's negative test: three inversions of C major in succession are **not** a chord progression.
"Because the three chords are simply inversions of the chord of C major, no root movement takes
place, and therefore there is no sense of progression for the ear. A sense of progression or
forward movement only occurs when there is a **change of chordal roots**."

The root is what makes this work: "a chord whose root note is F is always a chord of F — no matter
what type of chord it is. It is the presence of the root, therefore, that enables the ear to
identify a particular chord."

### The Three Types of Root Movement
| Type | Notes in common between the two chords | Character |
|---|---|---|
| **By fourths** (rising or falling) | **One** | "This one note acts as a link between the two, with the other two notes offering contrast by change. **Root movement by fourths is the strongest kind of root movement**, and it is often used sequentially, such as in a **cycle of fourths**." |
| **By thirds** (rising or falling) | **Two** | "This makes them very similar, although the connection between them is very smooth. This type of root movement does not offer the same kind of contrast as root movement by fourths." |
| **By seconds** (rising or falling) | **None** | "This kind of change offers the **maximum possible contrast**, although because the two chords share no notes, the connection is not so smooth." |

"All chord progressions involve root movement of these three types, although they can often
employ a mixture of movements."

### The Chord-Vocabulary Procedure
1. Learn to play the scale of the key through without difficulty.
2. "Work out the seven triads that belong to the key… by taking each note of the scale as the root
   of a triad and adding to it the third and the fifth (**being careful to remain within the bounds
   of the chosen scale**)."
3. String them into sequences that make sense to the ear.

### The Exploration Drill
"Take a key and play the seven chords in all three orders — root movement by seconds, thirds, and
fourths. This will assist you in getting to know the key, and it will probably give you inspiration
for your chord progressions."

### Why It Matters More as Harmony Gets Denser
"This becomes especially important when you are using really complex harmony… When harmony is
dense and complex, **a strong scheme of root movement gives it anchorage and makes it easier for
the ear to interpret**."

## Key Concepts
- **Static chord / no progression** — a chord repeated in different inversions; used deliberately
  in styles built on "static harmonic fields" (Chapter 13) but not a progression.
- **Cycle of fourths** — sequential root movement by fourths; the strongest and most sequential
  scheme (and the mechanism behind Chapter 19's cycle of fifths, its inversion).
- **Trade-off structure** — the three types form a spectrum: fourths = strong and balanced,
  thirds = smooth but low contrast, seconds = high contrast but rough.

## Mental Models
- **Roots, not voicings, are what the ear tracks.** Inversions change color; only root change
  creates motion.
- **Shared notes measure smoothness.** 1 shared note = strong; 2 shared = smooth; 0 shared =
  maximum contrast. This single number predicts how the join will sound.
- **Three choices, always.** From any chord, the next chord's root is a second, a third, or a
  fourth away (up or down). That's the whole decision space.

## Anti-patterns
- Mistaking inversion changes for a progression — "there is no sense of progression for the ear."
- Straying outside the key when building the seven triads.
- Writing dense extended harmony over a weak root scheme, leaving the ear with nothing to anchor to.

| Third | 2 | Highest | Lowest | Moderate |
| Second | 0 | Lowest | **Highest** | Moderate |

## Worked Example
**Figure 18.2 — the chapter's own four-chord progression in A minor**, presented with its root
scheme:

| | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Chords** | Am | Dm7 | G | C |
| **Root movement** | A | D | G | C |

Hewitt's analysis: "the scheme of root movement follows a pattern — A D G C. **All four roots are
linked by fourths.**" (A up to D is a fourth; D up to G is a fourth; G up to C is a fourth.) This
is the "cycle of fourths" applied sequentially — the strongest kind of root movement — and it is
why the progression feels driven.

Verifying with the shared-note rule: Am (A C E) → Dm7 (D F A C) shares A and C; Dm (D F A) →
G (G B D) shares D — one note, the predicted link note for fourth movement.

**Figure 18.1's counter-example:** three positions of C major in succession. Roots: C, C, C. No
root movement, therefore no progression.

**Exercise 1**, answered from the chapter's own text: "A sequence of chords is called a
**chord progression**. The most important feature of chord progressions is the sense of
**root movement**, of which there are three types: **root movement** by seconds, thirds and fourths."

**Exercise 4** is the chapter's applied drill, one composition per movement type:
- 4a. "Compose and sequence a four-chord progression in the key of **F major** based on
  **root movement by fourths**." (Roots a fourth apart within F major, e.g. F – Bb – Eb… kept in
  key: **F – Bb – Gm – C** gives roots F Bb… the in-key cycle-of-fourths chain from C is
  **C – F – Bb – Gm**, roots C F Bb G.)
- 4b. "…in the key of **D minor** that uses **root movement by thirds**." (Roots a third apart:
  **Dm – F – Am – C**, roots D F A C.)
- 4c. "…in the key of **G minor** that uses **root movement by seconds**." (Adjacent roots:
  **Gm – F – Eb – D**, roots G F Eb D — the same shape as the chapter's own D minor second-movement
  example, transposed.)

**Exercise 2** works in the other direction — given a progression, identify (2a) the key, (2b) each
chord including its inversion using slash notation such as **Em/G**, and (2c) circle the roots.
This trains the same skill in analysis rather than composition.

## Key Takeaways
1. A chord progression exists only where the *roots* change; inversions alone are static.
2. Exactly three root movements: by fourths, by thirds, by seconds — used singly or mixed.
3. Fourths share one note: the strongest movement, ideal for sequential cycles.
4. Thirds share two notes: smoothest, least contrast.
5. Seconds share none: maximum contrast, least smooth.
6. Build a key's chord vocabulary by taking every scale degree as a root and adding a third and a
   fifth, staying in key.
7. Drill a key by playing its seven chords in all three root-movement orders.
8. The denser the harmony, the more the root scheme must carry the ear.
