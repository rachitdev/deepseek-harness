# Chapter 20 — The Seven Diatonic Modes

## Core Idea
The key system "is only really pertinent to the classical music period between, say, 1720 and
1870." Before it, composers used **modes** — and the seven diatonic modes are obtained simply by
**rotating which note of the white-key scale is the tonic**. "The word 'mode' comes from the
ancient Greek word for 'mood'," and each mode has its own characteristic atmosphere, which is
exactly why "with the advent of loop-based music — found, for example, in trance, techno, house,
drum and bass — modal scales have also gained a newfound popularity. They favor the use of
repetition and looping techniques."

## Frameworks Introduced

### Rotation of the Tonic
"There are seven diatonic modal scales in all, and to obtain these, **the position of the first
degree of the scale (that is, the tonic) is changed**." The seven modes as shown all occur on the
white keys. "But they can also be used in any other key. This means that there are **84 (12 × 7)
modal possibilities** to contend with." Naming convention: key prefix + mode name — "F# Dorian
means the Dorian mode in the key of F#. Bb Locrian means the Locrian mode in the key of Bb."

### The Tonic as Drone
"As in the major and minor modes, the most important note is the tonic… In modal music, this is
often heard throughout the tune in the form of a **drone or pedal point** that provides an anchor
for the harmony. In loop-based music, it often represents the most important note in the bass —
that is, the note around which the bass revolves."

### The Harmonic Form of the Chromatic Scale
To use *all seven modes in one key*, you need more than seven notes. "To use any of the seven
modes in a particular key, you are in effect drawing the notes that you use from a full chromatic
scale. The name for this type of scale is the **harmonic form of chromatic scale**."

"The importance of the harmonic form of chromatic scale is that it uses all 12 keys on the
keyboard, **but referenced against a single tonic**. Therefore, each note represents a particular
relationship to that tonic. From this relationship, the note itself acquires its meaning and
significance in the scale. The note Db, for example, is significant as the flat second that may be
found in the Phrygian mode… The note F# is significant as the augmented fourth used in the Lydian
mode."

"Viewed in this context, the harmonic form of the chromatic scale is simply a complete summary of
modal interval relationships to a fixed tonic note. For composers, therefore, **it is very much
like an artist's palette of color**… There is only one scale of 12 notes to think about — a scale
that operates on two levels: **as a scale of 12 keynotes from which the tonic is chosen, and as a
host scale from which the notes for the modes are selected**."

Fingering: "There is a particular technique for playing the chromatic scale involving the thumb,
first, and second fingers of the right hand," numbered 1, 2, and 3.

## Key Concepts
- **Why modal music returned** — "Toward the end of the 1800s… composers started to feel
  restrained by the key system, and they began to experiment outside of it," looking both backward
  (pre-key-system modes) and sideways (folk music, in which the old modes survived).
- **Modal repertoire cited:** Gregorian chant; the chants of **Hildegard von Bingham**
  ("recordings of which are sampled in electronic tracks on occasion"); "**Sumer Is Icumen In**,"
  well known through the film *The Wicker Man*; the Beatles' output generally, "**Norwegian
  Wood**" in particular; progressive rock, jazz, blues, film, folk, and all styles of modern dance
  music.
- **Neapolitan triad** — the chord of F in E Phrygian; the characteristic Em→F progression.
- **Spanish Phrygian** — the Phrygian with a **sharp third**, obtained "when the chord of E major
  is used as a substitute for the chord of E minor." "It is used all of the time in Spanish
  Flamenco music," and "the major chords of E, F, and G are also often used to give that particular
  Spanish feel."
- **The harmonic scale** — the Lydian mode used with a **flat seventh**; "so named because the
  first 11 members of the harmonic series naturally suggest this scale." "Very atmospheric and
  works particularly well with the chords F or F7 and Cm."

## Mental Models
- **One scale, seven moods.** The white keys don't change; only where you put the tonic does. That
  relocation is the entire mechanism.
- **Each mode has one signature deviation.** Dorian = major sixth in a minor context; Phrygian =
  flat second; Lydian = sharp fourth; Mixolydian = flat seventh; Locrian = flat fifth. Identify the
  deviation and you have the mode's character.
- **The chromatic scale as palette.** Rather than twelve separate key systems, hold one 12-note
  palette against a fixed tonic and select the notes that make the mood you want.

## Anti-patterns
- Trying to write a conventional tonal progression in **Locrian**: "the Locrian mode is problematic
  because it doesn't have a proper tonic triad — B D F" (a diminished triad).
- Assuming the modes only work on white keys — they transpose to all 12 keys.

## Reference Tables

### The Seven Diatonic Modes (white-key rotations)
| Mode | Tonic (white-key form) | Notes | Deviation from the parallel major/minor | Character & uses |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Ionian** | C | C D E F G A B | = the major scale | Medieval nickname the **Wanton mode**; Greeks linked it to Venus, so "the Church was thus rather afraid of its use in case it inspired carnal feelings amongst the brethren!" |
| **Dorian** | D | D E F G A B C | Like natural minor but the **sixth is major** | "A strange character somewhere between major and minor." Revered in ancient Greece. |
| **Phrygian** | E | E F G A B C D | Like E natural minor but the **second is flat** | "Mellow character of the minor mode, although the flat second gives it a more stately, exotic quality." |
| **Lydian** | F | F G A B C D E | Like F major but the **fourth is sharp** (augmented fourth with the tonic) | "Can produce a euphoric sense." |
| **Mixolydian** | G | G A B C D E F | Like G major but the **seventh is flat** (whole tone F–G instead of a leading note) | "A distinct folk flavor." |
| **Aeolian** | A | A B C D E F G | = the natural minor scale | "The mode in which most popular music is written. As the mainstay of rock, pop, and all kinds of dance music." |
| **Locrian** | B | B C D E F G A | Like B natural minor but the **fifth is lowered** (diminished fifth with tonic) | "Really dark feeling"; no proper tonic triad. |

## Worked Example
**The chapter's own worked case — building all seven modes on a common tonic C** (Figure 20.10).

"If you choose the tonic to be note C, then for the **Phrygian** mode, you also need the notes
**Db, Eb, F, G, Ab, and Bb**. For the **Lydian** mode in the same key, you will also need the
notes **D, E, F#, G, A, and B**."

Applying each mode's deviation to C:

| Mode on C | Notes | Black keys required |
|---|---|---|
| C Ionian | C D E F G A B | none |
| C Dorian | C D Eb F G A Bb | 2 |
| C Phrygian | C Db Eb F G Ab Bb | 4 |
| C Lydian | C D E F# G A B | 1 |
| C Mixolydian | C D E F G A Bb | 1 |
| C Aeolian | C D Eb F G Ab Bb | 3 |
| C Locrian | C Db Eb F Gb Ab Bb | 5 |

"This is because **only the Ionian mode in the key of C exclusively uses the white keys**. All of
the other modes require the use of one or more black keys. You can see, for example, that the
**Locrian mode in the key of C uses five such keys**." Collect every note used and you have the
harmonic form of the chromatic scale (Table 20.3).

**Exercise 1:** "Choose the note that is the tonic or first degree of the following modal scales"
(all given as the white-key set C D E F G A B C). From the rotation table:
- Ionian → **C** · Mixolydian → **G** · Dorian → **D** · Aeolian → **A** · Phrygian → **E** ·
  Locrian → **B** · Lydian → **F**

**Exercise 4:** "Fill in the notes required to complete each mode in the key indicated." Applying
each mode's tone-semitone formula (Figure 20.9's table) from the given tonic:

| | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **C Dorian** | C | D | Eb | F | G | A | Bb |
| **F# Locrian** | F# | G | A | B | C | D | E |
| **G Aeolian** | G | A | Bb | C | D | Eb | F |
| **Eb Lydian** | Eb | F | G | A | Bb | C | D |

**Exercise 2** works in reverse, with the book supplying the method as a tip: "First, find the
major scale indicated by the key signature. Second, find the degree of that scale upon which the
above are built. This will tell you what the mode is. In other words, **if the mode begins on
degree four of a particular major scale, then it is a Lydian mode**, and so on."

## Key Takeaways
1. Seven modes = seven rotations of the tonic within one seven-note scale; × 12 keys = 84 possibilities.
2. Ionian = major; Aeolian = natural minor. The other five each have one signature deviation.
3. Dorian: major sixth in a minor context (Dm–G). Phrygian: flat second (psy-trance's mode).
   Lydian: sharp fourth. Mixolydian: flat seventh (folk flavor). Locrian: flat fifth (dark, no
   tonic triad).
4. Modes suit loop-based music because each supplies a fixed atmosphere over a drone tonic.
5. Two derived modes worth knowing: Spanish Phrygian (sharp third) and the harmonic scale
   (Lydian with flat seventh).
6. Using all seven modes in one key means drawing from the harmonic form of the chromatic scale —
   twelve notes referenced to one tonic, a composer's palette.
