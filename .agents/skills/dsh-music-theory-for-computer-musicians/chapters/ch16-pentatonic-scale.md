# Chapter 16 — The Pentatonic Scale

## Core Idea
"Mode concerns the type of scale used and its characteristic features," and major/minor is only
"an inheritance from the classical music traditions of the west." The pentatonic scale — five
notes instead of seven — is "a truly global musical scale," "the earliest known musical scale,
predating the seven-note major or minor scale by thousands of years." Its defining property: by
omitting F and B (from C) it removes the sharp minor seconds E/F and B/C, so **all five notes
played simultaneously still sound harmonious. There is no harshness or dissonance in the sound at all.**

## Frameworks Introduced

### The Gapped Scale
The pentatonic scale built on C uses **only five white keys, missing out F and B**: **C D E G A**.
"The pentatonic scale is sometimes known as the **gapped scale**." Because of the gaps between
E and G and between A and C, the minor seconds are gone — hence its use in children's music: "They
can play any of the notes in any combination, and it still sounds good."

### Interval Pattern and the Five Modes
The scale's interval pattern is **tone, tone, minor third, tone, minor third**. "Because of the
asymmetrical pattern of intervals in the scale… five modes are created, each with its own
particular character." Like the diatonic modes, the tonic note can be moved around.

"There are 12 keynotes, which means that there are potentially **60 possible pentatonic modes**."
Figure 16.7 gives a formula in semitone steps for working out any mode on any keynote.

### Added Note Chords
"Musicians often use the pentatonic scale to enrich basic triads and give them a more complex,
colorful sound. These chords are called **added note chords**."
- Of the five notes **C D E G A**, three form a **C major triad: C, E, G**.
- The remaining two are the added notes: **A gives the added sixth chord**; **D gives the added
  ninth chord**.
- "When combined, together they produce a **chord of the added sixth/ninth**." (Audio Track 36.)

### Suspended Chords
"Pentatonic influences also inspire the use of **suspended chords**. A suspended chord is when
**the third is swapped for a note that lies on either side of it**." These "offer a refreshing
alternative to ordinary triads." (Hewitt notes the European practice of creating suspended chords
through linear suspensions as another application.)

### Neighbor Notes for Improvisation
"When improvising, the pentatonic scale offers important **neighbor notes** to the chord tones…
These are used to embellish and enhance the melodic interest of the music."

### Pentatonic Harmony Strategies
1. **Pure pentatonic harmony is limited** — "the pentatonic scale only contains two complete
   triads — C major and A minor."
2. **Borrow from the diatonic scale.** "To make up for this, you can harmonize pentatonic tunes
   using chords from the seven-note diatonic scale. This can be extremely effective, with the
   other two notes — F and B — being used as harmony notes to complete chords **II (Dm), IV (F —
   the subdominant), III (Em), and V (G — dominant)**." Examples: "Amazing Grace," and "the
   stately horn theme of the slow movement of Dvorak's New World Symphony."
3. **Bare fourths and fifths** for an Oriental flavor, as in traditional Chinese music.
4. **Quartal and quintal harmony** — "building up chords by fourths and/or fifths." **"A five-note
   quintal chord uses all notes of the pentatonic scale to give a beautiful, stately harmony"**
   (Track 38, sequenced on a motion synth patch).

### The Five Modes and the Five Elements
"Like the major and minor modes with their emotional affinities (**'mode' comes from the ancient
Greek word for 'mood'**), different modes are suitable for the expression of particular emotions.
A parallel to this lies in traditional Chinese music, where music using the pentatonic scale was
developed into a high art. Here the five modes were linked to the **five elements** that, in
**Taoist philosophy**, were considered to rule the universe. Associated with each element were
characteristic emotions, moods, colors, and so on." (Figure 16.5.)

## Key Concepts
- **"A scale for 'the people.'"** "Many ancient peoples developed and used the pentatonic scale
  independently of one another. The seven-note scale only developed with the emergence of high
  civilizations, such as Egypt, China, Mesopotamia, Greece… People not involved in this process —
  in other words, tribal peoples such as the American Indians — tended to stay with the pentatonic
  scale. For this reason, the pentatonic scale is and always has been a scale for 'the people,' as
  opposed to for the pomp and ceremony of the high state."
- **Pentatonic core with passing notes** — "Some of the most beautiful tunes in the world have a
  pentatonic core. The missing notes — F and B — are touched upon only in passing."

## Mental Models
- **Remove the dissonance, remove the risk.** Take out the minor seconds and every simultaneity
  works. That is why the scale is safe for children, for improvisation, and for stacking into
  quintal chords.
- **Five notes, five modes, twelve keys.** The rotation principle from the diatonic modes applies
  identically, just with five rotations instead of seven.
- **Two triads is a constraint, not a dead end.** The response is to borrow diatonic chords, or to
  abandon triadic harmony for quartal/quintal stacks.

## Anti-patterns
- Expecting a full triadic harmonic vocabulary from a pentatonic scale — there are only two
  complete triads in it.
- Sticking to "common triads — that is, unadorned major and minor chords," when added-note and
  suspended chords "can give your tracks an edge."

## Worked Example
**Exercise 3:** "Build a pentatonic scale on the following keynotes: D ___ ; F ___ ; Bb ___."

Worked with the chapter's own interval pattern — **tone, tone, minor third, tone, minor third**:
- **D:** D → (tone) E → (tone) F# → (minor 3rd) A → (tone) B → (minor 3rd) D.
  **D E F# A B**
- **F:** F → G → A → C → D → F. **F G A C D**
- **Bb:** Bb → C → D → F → G → Bb. **Bb C D F G**

**Exercise 5:** "Identify three added note chords that could be used in the following pentatonic
scale: **F G A C D**."

Applying the chapter's own analysis of C pentatonic to F pentatonic — the three triad tones are
**F, A, C** (an F major triad); the two remaining notes, **D and G**, are the added notes:
- Chord 1: **F added sixth (F A C + D)**
- Chord 2: **F added ninth (F A C + G)**
- Chord 3: **F added sixth/ninth (F A C + D + G)**

**Exercise 2:** "Label and identify the intervals between each of the notes of this G pentatonic
scale." G pentatonic is **G A B D E**: G–A tone, A–B tone, B–D minor third, D–E tone, E–G minor third.

**Exercise 4** builds all five modes on C by rotating the pattern; **Exercise 6** is the chapter's
capstone task: "Compose a lead using the pentatonic scale. When you have done so, add a harmony
based on quintal chords — chords composed of superimposed fifths."

## Key Takeaways
1. Five notes (C D E G A from C major); the gapped scale omits F and B and with them the minor seconds.
2. Every combination of its notes is consonant — hence its use in children's music and improvisation.
3. Pattern: tone, tone, minor 3rd, tone, minor 3rd. Rotating it gives five modes; × 12 keynotes = 60 modes.
4. Three of the five notes form a major triad; the other two give added sixth, added ninth, and
   added sixth/ninth chords.
5. Suspended chords replace the third with a neighbor note.
6. Only two complete triads exist in it — borrow diatonic chords (Dm, Em, F, G) or use
   quartal/quintal harmony instead.
7. A five-note quintal chord uses the entire scale at once.
