# Chapter 11 — The Harmonic and Melodic Minor Scales

## Core Idea
"The minor key is much more complex than the major. This is because the minor scale occurs in a
number of different forms." The major mode has one form; the minor has three. Hewitt explains the
extra two **as fixes for specific defects**: the natural minor gives a weak dominant, so sharpen
the seventh (harmonic minor); the sharpened seventh creates an awkward augmented second, so
sharpen the sixth as well on the way up (melodic minor). "The variations of the minor scale are
thus due to musicians changing elements of it to suit their purposes."

## Frameworks Introduced

### The Tetrachord System
"Ancient Greek musical scales developed from the **tetrachord: a series of four notes spanning a
fourth** (for example, C D E F). The scales used today use two such tetrachords — a **lower
tetrachord** and an **upper tetrachord**. Between them they make up the eight notes of the
octave." The tetrachord system "is very good for understanding and portraying differences of mode."

### The Harmonic Minor Scale
- **Lower tetrachord: identical to the natural minor.** **Upper tetrachord: the seventh note (G in
  A minor) is sharpened to G#.**
- The upper tetrachord "has an exotic sense owing to the charm of its intervals. This is largely
  due to an interval we have not encountered so far — the **augmented second between notes F and
  G#**."
- **The harmonic reason for G#:** with G natural the dominant is Em, giving **v–i (Em–Am)**, which
  is "quite mellow… a more quiescent feel." With G#, the dominant becomes a **major E chord**,
  giving **V–i (E–Am)**, which is "much more incisive and powerful," "primarily because the G# —
  only a semitone below the tonic — rose nice and smoothly up to the tonic." (Audio Track 30.)
- Hence the name: "the harmonic minor scale, which is named after its characteristic harmony."

### The Melodic Minor Scale
- Classical composers found the natural minor's harmonies "not incisive enough for the dramatic
  kind of narrative favored by classical composers." Sharpening the seventh solved the harmony
  problem, but "in some melodic contexts that exotic-sounding upper tetrachord did not sound right."
- **Solution: sharpen the sixth as well**, giving "a smooth stepwise motion up to the tonic —
  exactly like the upper tetrachord of the major mode."
- **To counterbalance that major feeling, on descent the minor mode was re-established.** So the
  melodic minor "is different in its ascending and descending forms": ascending has sharp 6 and
  sharp 7; **descending is identical to the natural minor**.
- Status: "As a holdover from classical times, the melodic minor mode plays a larger role in
  theory than in practice." But: **jazz musicians use the ascending form so much that it is
  sometimes called the jazz minor scale**; some of its seventh chords "are beautiful"; and
  **Hawaiian traditional music uses the melodic minor mode to good effect**.

### The Four-Step Learning Procedure (applied to any new scale)
1. **Learn to play the scale up and down** until familiar. (Appendix A gives correct fingering and
   the best way to play it.)
2. **Learn the chords** — the same as the natural minor's "except for one crucial difference —
   the note G is always sharp." Practice them **as arpeggios**: root with the thumb, third with
   finger three, fifth with finger five (the little finger).
3. **Develop three-chord progressions** starting with the primary chords **i, iv, V**.
4. **Bring in secondary chords**, and re-run your natural-minor progressions **substituting chord
   V for chord v**. "Sometimes chord V is a much better option, especially at the cadence, because
   it gives a more final sense of closure on the tonic chord."

## Key Concepts
- **Augmented second** — the F–G# gap in the harmonic minor's upper tetrachord; the interval that
  gives it its exotic quality and, as a side effect, the awkwardness the melodic minor was
  invented to fix. Full explanation in Chapter 12.
- **Leading note** — the sharp seventh, a semitone below the tonic, that pulls up to it.
- **Melodic minor descent** — plain white keys in A minor; "when you get to the top, come back
  down again using only the white keys."
- **Convergence in practice:** "as your knowledge increases and you gain more experience writing
  in the minor mode, the three forms will tend to merge into an overall minor scale complex."
- **The major mode gets modified too** — "by borrowing elements from the minor mode, as seen in
  the **harmonic major mode** and the **major/minor mode**" (Chapter 22).

## Mental Models
- **Defect-driven design.** Each minor form is a repair: weak dominant → sharpen 7; awkward
  augmented 2nd → sharpen 6 ascending; unwanted major feeling → revert on descent.
- **Two poles, again.** The passive tonic pole and active dominant pole from Chapter 8 are exactly
  what motivates the harmonic minor's existence.
- **Direction-dependent scale.** The melodic minor is the book's only scale whose content depends
  on whether you are going up or down.

## Anti-patterns
- Using chord v (minor dominant) at a cadence when a decisive close is wanted — V (major) is
  usually stronger there.
- Treating the melodic minor as an equal-status everyday scale; Hewitt is explicit that it "plays
  a larger role in theory than in practice."
- Learning the scale without the chords — steps 2 through 4 of the procedure are where the
  musical payoff is.

## Reference Tables

### The three minor forms in A (ascending)
| Form | Notes | Distinguishing feature |
|---|---|---|
| **Natural minor** | A B C D E F G A | Pattern T S T T S T T |
| **Harmonic minor** | A B C D E F **G#** A | Sharp 7; augmented 2nd F–G# |
| **Melodic minor (ascending)** | A B C D E **F#** **G#** A | Sharp 6 *and* sharp 7; stepwise to tonic |
| **Melodic minor (descending)** | A G F E D C B A | Identical to natural minor |

### Tetrachord comparison
| Scale | Lower tetrachord | Upper tetrachord |
|---|---|---|
| A natural minor | A B C D | E F G A |
| A harmonic minor | A B C D | E F G# A |
| A melodic minor (asc.) | A B C D | E F# G# A |

### Cadence comparison in A minor
| Scale form | Dominant chord | Cadence | Character |
|---|---|---|---|
| Natural minor | Em (E G B) | **v–i**: Em–Am | "quite mellow… more quiescent" |
| Harmonic minor | E major (E G# B) | **V–i**: E–Am | "much more incisive and powerful" |

### Chords of A harmonic minor
The chord set is that of A natural minor with **G always sharpened**, which changes any chord
containing G: chord v Em becomes **E major (E G# B)**, and the chords built on the third and
seventh degrees are likewise altered. Figure 11.7 presents all seven in staff format with guitar

## Worked Example
The chapter's central worked comparison, **V–i versus v–i in A minor** (Figure 11.4, audio
Track 30):

| | Natural minor | Harmonic minor |
|---|---|---|
| Progression | v – i | V – i |
| Chords | **Em – Am** | **E – Am** |
| Dominant chord notes | E G B | E **G#** B |
| Motion into the tonic | G → A (a whole tone) | G# → A (a semitone) |
| Effect | Mellow, quiescent | Incisive, powerful; "rose nice and smoothly up to the tonic" |

The chapter's instruction: "sequence it and listen carefully to the two progressions."

**Exercise 1**, answered from the chapter's own text: "Although there is only one kind of major
mode, there are **three** types of minor mode: the natural minor, **harmonic** minor, and melodic
minor. The difference between the natural and harmonic minor is that the harmonic minor mode has
a sharp **seventh** in order to give a leading note up to the **tonic** degree. The ascending form
of the melodic minor mode also has a sharp **sixth**, while on descent it is identical to the
**natural** minor mode."

## Key Takeaways
1. Three minor forms exist because the natural minor has two specific defects, fixed in sequence.
2. Harmonic minor = natural minor with a sharp 7; it exists to make the dominant major and the
   cadence decisive.
3. The sharp 7 creates the augmented second (F–G# in A minor) — the source of the scale's exotic color.
4. Melodic minor = sharp 6 and sharp 7 ascending, natural minor descending. Called the **jazz
   minor** in its ascending form.
5. Substitute V for v at cadences when you want closure; keep v when you want mellowness.
6. The tetrachord (lower + upper, four notes each) is the tool for comparing modes.
7. Learn any scale by: play it → learn its chords as arpeggios → primary-chord progressions →
   secondary chords.
