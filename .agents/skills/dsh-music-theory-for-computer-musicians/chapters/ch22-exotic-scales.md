# Chapter 22 — Exotic Scales

## Core Idea
"If you are ever looking for inspiration or for a fresh new sound, exotic scales are a good place
to start. **If you use exotic scales, you'll never get into a creative rut.**" But exotic scales
are not just alternative note pools to build triads on — most of them "have the character of
musical modes," so "they often work best in the melodic dimension, where there is a different kind
of harmony at play — **the harmony of the interval**."

## Frameworks Introduced

### Two Kinds of Exotic Scale
| Kind | Origin | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Imitative** | "Music writers' attempts to imitate or emulate the native music of faraway places" | The **Hungarian minor mode**, "otherwise known as the **Gypsy scale**, often used to suggest an Eastern European atmosphere"; the various **Japanese pentatonic scales**, which differ from the familiar pentatonic "because they use semitones" |
| **2. Invented** | "Simply invented at one time or another by composers for particular musical purposes" | The **Mystic scale** — "a scale of six notes based on the harmonic series, originally developed by **Alexander Scriabin**. Using this exclusively, Scriabin created a totally unique sound unlike anything anybody had ever heard before!" |

### A Mode Is a Collection of Intervals, Not Notes
"Pure modal music often employs **drones** and such to fix the pitch of the tonic in the listener's
mind. Indian music is a good example. The sitar upon which much of the music is played has a
series of resonating strings that continually sound the modal tonic and its fifth. Each note of
the mode is then heard as a harmony note with that tonic, and from this harmony, the note gains
its modal coloring. **From this perspective, a mode is not a collection of notes, but rather a
collection of intervals, each of which contributes its own character to the mix.**"

Therefore the chromatic scale referenced to a fixed tonic (Table 22.1) is the working tool:
"Each interval, as considered in relation to the modal tonic, has its own character, place, and
significance."

### The Transposition Note
> "Although the examples… were built in the key of C, they can be used in any key you want — as
> can any of the scales/modes that follow. In this context, note C is simply a familiar reference
> point upon which to construct, compare, and view the scales. **Should you want to try them out
> in other keys, work out the number of semitones between each note and build up the scale
> accordingly.**"

### The Neapolitan Transformation
"Their name comes from the fact that they all have a **flat second degree**, like the Phrygian
mode. The flat second is a very evocative interval and, when used, it can bring great color and
expressiveness to music. **You can turn any mode or scale into a Neapolitan simply by flattening
the second degree.**"

Hewitt's notes on the four Neapolitan modes shown in Figure 22.1 (built on C):
- The first is **the harmonic minor mode with a flat second**. "The presence of the flat second
  gives the mode a distinct Eastern sense."
- The second "offers a compromise between the Dorian and Phrygian modes."
- Useful chord combinations despite the awkward harmony: "The mix of **Bbm, Eb, and F** in the
  **Neapolitan Dorian** mode is a good example, as is the mix of **Bbm and C** in the
  **Neapolitan Mixolydian** mode."

### The Scale-Analysis Method (Exercise 1's three questions)
Hewitt's own procedure for making any exotic scale usable — "To use an exotic scale effectively,
you must collect important information beforehand":
- **A. What are the intervals formed by each note of the scale with the keynote (tonic)?**
- **B. What are the salient harmonies of the scale?** "Look for conspicuous triads that can be used
  to help add and create harmonies."
- **C. What are the salient melodic features that define the scale?** "These are groups of two or
  three notes that give the scale its particular character. You can discover these by playing the
  scale on your keyboard and listening carefully for its strongest and most characteristic
  features. For example, the strongest feature of the Neapolitan group of modes is the flat second
  between the first and second degrees. In the Neapolitan major modes, this creates a note group
  of **C Db E**, which is a very strong and recognizable feature of those modes."

## Key Concepts
- **Hemitonic versus anhemitonic pentatonic** — "hemitonic five-note scales — that is, pentatonic
  scales that **use semitones** (as opposed to **anhemitonic** scales, which are pentatonic scales
  **without** semitones)."
- **Blue notes** — "the various inflections of ordinary scale notes." In the blues **hexatonic,
  seven-note, and nine-note** scales, "these are the notes **Eb, F#, and Bb**."
- **Melas** — the Carnatic system of Hindustani music "uses a set of **72 basic scales called
  melas**." "These are best used melodically over a drone or sustained notes."
- **Artificial scales** — invented scales. "In addition to studying and collecting exotic scales —
  which itself is interesting — you can try to invent your own."
- **Authentic tuning caveat** — for Middle Eastern scales, "to be more authentic, in some cases you
  would need to use a proper **Arabian form of tuning**" (Chapter 25).

## Mental Models
- **Inflection defines character.** Every exotic scale in the chapter is described as a familiar
  scale with one or two altered degrees. "What make these scales so interesting are their
  characteristic **inflections** — sharps or flat notes that make them different than the regular
  major and minor scales. The use of these inflections gives music written within these scales its
  own unique character."
- **Drone first, chords second.** Exotic scales "don't always offer suitable harmonies"; a
  sustained tonic (or tonic + fifth, sitar-style) is what makes their intervals legible.
- **Harmony can generate a scale.** "Some types of exotic scales result from the use of certain
  characteristic types of harmony" — the whole tone scale is a scale made entirely of augmented triads.

## Anti-patterns
- Thinking about exotic scales "in a limited chordal sense" — most of them reward melodic use.
- Expecting a leading note where there is none: the whole tone scale has "no sense of a tonic"
  because there are no semitones.

### Eastern European scales — Hewitt's own definitions by inflection
| Scale | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Hungarian Major** | "A distinctive **sharp second and fourth and a flat seventh**" |
| **Hungarian Minor** | "Basically the same as the **harmonic minor** scale except that the **fourth has been sharpened**" |
| **Gypsy I** | "The same as the **natural minor** scale, but again with the **fourth sharpened**" |
| **Gypsy II** | "Like a regular **major** scale except that the **second and sixth degrees have been lowered by a semitone**" |

| Gypsy II (major, b2, b6) | C Db E F G Ab B |

### The Whole Tone Scale
- "As its name suggests, this scale is built up **entirely from whole tones**. **Every chord within
  that scale is an augmented triad.**"
- "Originally popular with early 20th-century Impressionist composers **Debussy and Ravel**, the
  harmony it produces is quite unique. **The lack of semitones gives music written in this scale a
  wandering, abstract quality.**"
- "Because of the lack of semitones, and therefore the absence of a leading note, **there is no
  sense of a tonic** in the whole tone scale. To impart at least some sense of a tonic, the
  **leading whole tone scale** is sometimes used. The semitone below C suggests a leading note and
  therefore persuades the listener that the scale does have a tonic."

Built on C: **C D E F# G# A# (C)** — six notes, all a whole tone apart.

### Exotic pentatonic variants
| Scale family | Hewitt's description |
|---|---|
| **Ancient Egyptian pentatonic** | "Basically the same as **pentatonic mode 4**, except that the **second and fifth degrees were flattened**" — a hemitonic five-note scale |
| **Japanese pentatonic scales** | "Similar hemitonic variants… in traditional Japanese music" |
| **Balinese Pelog** | "One of the most exotic forms of pentatonic scale… Although the Pelog version is similar in profile to the ancient Egyptian scale, it is tuned in a certain way that sounds very strange to our ears. Yet when used on traditional Balinese instruments, it sounds quite beautiful. This tuning often comes as one of the alternative tunings presented on modern-day synths, such as the **Korg Triton**. Check it out when you can, being careful to use a **Balinese metallophone program** that will show this tuning at its best." |

## Worked Example
**Exercise 1 — the chapter's own scale-analysis worksheet, worked on the scale it supplies:**

> Name: **Syrian** · Keynote: **B** · Type: **Exotic Hexatonic** · Pitches: **B C D# E G A# B**

**A. Intervals formed by each note with the keynote B** (using Table 22.1's interval names,
referenced to B as tonic):
- B → **Unison** (given)
- C → **minor second**
- D# → **major third**
- E → **perfect fourth**
- G → **minor sixth**
- A# → **major seventh**
- B → **Octave** (given)

Note the scale has **no fifth degree at all** — it skips from the perfect fourth to the minor
sixth. That absence is part of its character.

**B. Salient harmonies** — conspicuous triads inside B C D# E G A#:
- **B augmented (B D# G)** — root, major third, augmented fifth; all three notes are in the scale.
- **E minor (E G B)** — all three notes present.
- The scale therefore leans on an augmented tonic sonority with a minor subdominant available.

**C. Salient melodic features** — following Hewitt's own model (the Neapolitan's flat second
giving the note group C Db E):
- The **flat second B–C** at the very bottom, exactly the Neapolitan/Phrygian inflection.
- The **augmented second C–D#** (three semitones spelled as a second), the harmonic-minor-style
  gap of Chapter 11.
- Together these make the opening note group **B C D#** — a semitone followed by an augmented
  second — which is the scale's most recognizable signature.

**Exercise 2:** "Complete the whole tone scale using the keynote given below. Underneath, list the
augmented triads that come from it. Note: **Ab** ___ ___ ___ ___ ___"

Whole tones from Ab: **Ab Bb C D E F#**.
Augmented triads (stacked major thirds, and since every chord in the scale is augmented there are
two distinct ones):
- Triad 1: **Ab C E**
- Triad 2: **Bb D F#**

**Exercise 4** turns the method around into invention: "Devise artificial scales using the
following criteria. A. A pentatonic scale that uses two minor chords. B. A hexatonic scale that
has no whole tones. C. An octatonic scale that uses four major triads."

## Key Takeaways
1. Two kinds of exotic scale: imitations of non-Western traditions, and scales invented by composers.
2. Exotic scales are usually best used melodically over a drone — a mode is a set of intervals
   against a fixed tonic, not just a note pool.
3. Table 22.1 (the chromatic scale as intervals from a tonic) is the reference tool for building
   and analyzing any of them.
4. Flatten the second degree of anything and you get a Neapolitan mode — the most portable
   transformation in the chapter.
5. Every scale here is described by its **inflections** — which degrees are raised or lowered
   relative to a familiar scale.
6. The whole tone scale is all augmented triads, has no leading note and hence no tonic; the
   leading whole tone scale adds one back.
7. Analyze any new scale with three questions: intervals from the tonic, available triads,
   signature two- or three-note melodic groups.
