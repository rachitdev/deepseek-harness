# Chapter 6 — Intervals

## Core Idea
An interval is "the relationship between notes in terms of their pitch — that is, how high or low
they are." Intervals are the building blocks of everything: "leads, basses, pads, harmonies,
arpeggios, and so on." The same interval appears in two dimensions — **melodically** (horizontal,
successive notes) and **harmonically** (vertical, simultaneous notes) — and is described the same
way in both.

## Frameworks Introduced

### Melodic versus Harmonic Intervals
| Type | Dimension | Where it occurs |
|---|---|---|
| **Melodic** | Horizontal | Between successive notes of a lead melody or bass line |
| **Harmonic** | Vertical | Between notes struck at the same time in a chord |

### The Counting Rule
**Include the starting note.** From C to G there are five notes counting both ends —
C D E F G — so the interval C–G is a **fifth**.

### The "First" (Unison / Prime)
The first is counted as an interval: two notes of the same pitch sounding at the same time.
Also called a **unison** or a **prime**. On **polyphonic** instruments it would not usually occur
("there is not much point in duplicating the same note"); on **monophonic** instruments — such as
the human voice — it does occur, as when two singers sing the same note.

### Simple versus Compound Intervals
- **Simple intervals** lie within the range of an octave: first, second, third, fourth, fifth,
  sixth, seventh (and the octave itself). Heard in succession on Track 11.
- **Compound intervals** are bigger than an octave: a simple interval plus one or more octaves.
  An **eleventh is a compound fourth**; a **twelfth is a compound fifth**; a **tenth is a
  compound third**.

### The Subtract-Seven Algorithm
To reduce a compound interval to its simple form, **subtract 7** and keep subtracting until the
number is below seven.
- 12 − 7 = 5 → a twelfth is a compound fifth.
- A twenty-fourth (C3 to E6): 24 − 7 = 17, 17 − 7 = 10, 10 − 7 = 3 → **a compound third**.

## Key Concepts
- **Concords** — notes that blend well. **Discords** — notes that "tend to disagree." The reasons
  are taken up in Chapter 8.
- **Harmony / sonority** — the relationship the ear perceives when two notes are heard together.
- **Polyphonic** — capable of producing more than one note at a time; **monophonic** — only one.
- **Production usage:** in late-'90s dance music, **tenths (compound thirds) were used to great
  effect in the breakdowns of trance tracks** (Figure 6.7 shows a trance breakdown on the Korg
  Triton "Pretty Pad Patch," sequenced in Cakewalk SONAR in both piano roll and score edit
  formats; audio Track 12).

## Mental Models
- **Intervals are the painter's palette.** (Stated in Chapter 8, applying to this chapter's
  material: "Intervals are for the musician what the colors of the palette are for the visual artist.")
- **Same relationship, higher octave.** A compound interval duplicates a simple relationship —
  C to F — an octave up; that is why the subtract-seven reduction is valid.
- **The ear analyses automatically.** "When one note is heard after another, or even when two
  notes are played at the same time, the ear and mind analyze their relationship."

## Anti-patterns
- Forgetting to count the starting note, which shifts every interval name by one.
- Learning interval names without learning their *sound*: "this knowledge includes being able to
  hear and recognize intervals by their characteristic aural qualities."

## Reference Tables

### Simple intervals in the C major scale (from C)
| Interval name | Notes | Notes counted |
|---|---|---|
| First (unison/prime) | C–C | 1 |
| Second | C–D | 2 |
| Third | C–E | 3 |
| Fourth | C–F | 4 |
| Fifth | C–G | 5 |
| Sixth | C–A | 6 |
| Seventh | C–B | 7 |
| Octave | C–C | 8 |

### Compound intervals and their simple equivalents
| Compound | Subtract 7 | Simple equivalent |
|---|---|---|
| Ninth | 9 − 7 | Second |
| Tenth | 10 − 7 | Third |
| Eleventh | 11 − 7 | Fourth |
| Twelfth | 12 − 7 | Fifth |
| Twenty-fourth | 24−7=17, 17−7=10, 10−7 | Third |

## Worked Example
**Exercise 5:** "Complete the following list of fifths in the C major scale: A. C — G;
B. D — ___; C. E — ___; D. F — ___; E. G — ___; F. A — ___; G. B — ___."

Applying the chapter's counting rule (count five letters inclusive, staying on white keys):
- D: D E F G **A**
- E: E F G A **B**
- F: F G A B **C**
- G: G A B C **D**
- A: A B C D **E**
- B: B C D E **F**

(The B–F fifth is the one the next chapter identifies as the odd one out — the diminished fifth,
*diabolus in musica*.)

**Exercise 6**, worked the same way within the white-key scale:
- A. A fifth above D = **A**
- B. A ninth above F = a compound second, i.e. G an octave up = **G**
- C. A compound third above G = **B** (an octave up)
- D. A seventh below A = **B**
- E. A fourth below B = **F**
- F. A sixth above E = **C**
- G. An octave above D = **D**
- H. An eleventh above C = a compound fourth = **F** (an octave up)

**Exercise 7** (the ear-training drill, reproduced as the chapter states it): "Sequence a random
series of a dozen varied simple intervals using only the white notes of the keyboard. Make each
interval last a single bar. Use an acoustic piano patch. Leave a gap of two bars between them.
After you have done this, turn around with a sheet of paper and try to identify each of the
intervals. Do this as often as necessary until you can recognize the intervals by their qualities."

(The book's printed answers were a separate download from www.courseptr.com/downloads; the above
are worked from the chapter's own counting rule and the C major scale.)

## Key Takeaways
1. An interval is a pitch relationship; melodic and harmonic uses are described identically.
2. Always count the starting note — C to G is a fifth because C D E F G is five notes.
3. Simple = within an octave; compound = larger. Subtract 7 (repeatedly) to reduce.
4. The first/unison/prime counts as an interval and matters on monophonic sources.
5. Compound thirds (tenths) are a specific production technique for trance breakdowns.
6. Interval knowledge must be aural as well as intellectual.
