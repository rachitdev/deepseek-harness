# Chapter 10 — Melody and Motives

## Core Idea
A melodic line has **two axes — pitch (vertical) and time (horizontal)** — and that is exactly how
a sequencer window is laid out. But "you can construe any random succession of pitches to be a
melodic line. But there is more to it than that." What gives a line identity is the **motive**:
"a short snippet of melody that is readily recognizable to the ear because of its distinctive
rhythm." Hewitt's central compositional instruction: **work on the rhythm first**, then choose
intervals to match the emotional context.

## Frameworks Introduced

### The Two Axes of Melody
| Axis | Dimension | Sequencer element |
|---|---|---|
| Pitch | Vertical | Keyboard down the left side; horizontal lanes extending from it |
| Time | Horizontal | Bar numbers and bar divisions above the window |

### Motive → Phrase → Structure
- **Motive** — "the shortest piece of catchy music that it is possible to write. It is the strong
  rhythmic identity that makes it catchy." Motives "generally employ two or three notes played
  with a strong, readily identifiable rhythm."
- **Phrase** — two motives balancing each other "in a question/answer type of relationship"
  combine to form a larger unit.
- Motives "are usually the building blocks of a greater structure." In loop-based music they
  "create a sense of forward motion, impetus, and drive" (Figure 10.5's looped chordal stab motive).

### The Motive-Writing Procedure
1. **Work on the rhythm first.** "Create a strong, distinctive rhythm that the ear will
   immediately recognize." Figure 10.6's two dance bass motives are cited as examples especially
   suited to bass lines; "the driving rhythm gives both motives a sense of forward motion and impetus."
2. **Choose intervals by emotional context.** Hewitt's diagnostic questions:
   - What does the motive represent?
   - Is the energy rising or falling?
   - Is it major or minor?
   - Does the motive energy leap erratically, or is it smooth and stepwise?
3. **Build your own interval-to-feeling catalog.** "Experiment with the intervals we've looked at
   so far. What is the difference between a rising major third and a rising minor third? What is
   it about the rising minor sixth that evokes such a strong feeling of angst? And why does the
   rising major sixth sound so content?" The judgments may be subjective — "but that does not
   matter. If an interval causes you to feel something, it is liable to cause the same feeling in
   your listeners."
4. **Listen analytically.** For each motive you hear, ask: "Why has the writer used this motive?
   What do the intervals suggest?"

### Motivic Treatment (the film-music model)
"Film composers use motives all of the time, linking particular motives to certain characters. The
way they treat the motive then informs you of the emotional context." John Williams's **"Jaws"
motive**: a **rising semitone played low down on double basses** creates a strong sense of threat;
tension is built during the shark attacks by **continually repeating the motive, getting louder
and faster** as the shark approaches.

## Key Concepts
- **Melodic interval** — the pitch change between successive notes; the raw material of the line.
- **Motivic memorability** — "So memorable are some of these motives that they can often get stuck
  in our heads. Advertisers know this well… Entering the subconscious, they also carry with them
  that engendered link to the intended product."
- **Scale dependence** — "The motives you do use will draw their notes from a particular scale.
  Your ability to write motives thus also depends upon your knowledge of scales."

## Mental Models
- **Rhythm is the identity, pitch is the color.** A motive is recognizable "because of its
  distinctive rhythm" — hence rhythm first, intervals second.
- **Question and answer.** Two statements of a motive balance each other; that balance is what
  makes a phrase.
- **Beethoven's Fifth is the reference case.** "The best-known example of a motive is in
  Beethoven's Fifth Symphony: da - da - da - daaaa….! But this principle doesn't just apply to
  classical music. It is a universally recognized principle behind melodic composition."

## Anti-patterns
- Treating melody as any random succession of pitches — "a good melodic line has some craft behind
  its construction."
- Choosing intervals before establishing a rhythmic identity.
- Writing motives without knowing the scale they draw from.

## Worked Example
The chapter's own analysis of the **Mission Impossible theme**, reproduced as it appears:

- The underlying rhythm of the track is based on a **10/8 motive** and is "very distinctive."
- The opening — played on **flutes, strings, and bass synth** — "uses three pitches in the first
  motive and another three as the motive repeats in the next bar."
- "Together, the two statements of the motive balance each other in a question/answer type of
  relationship. In this way, the two motives combine to form a larger unit. This is called a
  **phrase**."

This is the chapter's model for every motive analysis it then asks the reader to perform.
**Exercise 3** applies exactly that model to a given two-bar melody: (A) identify the scale it
uses; (B) "using a pencil, identify and mark out the motives used, assigning to each identified
motive a letter (A, B, and so on)"; (C) "mark out and indicate the phrase structure of the
melody, giving each phrase a letter."

**Exercises 4–7** then run the chapter's rhythm-first procedure twice as a paired drill:
- Ex. 4: build a four-bar rhythm in **compound quadruple time** from given motives.
- Ex. 5: using that rhythm, compose a four-bar lead **around the motive of a rising fifth, in
  C major**.
- Ex. 6: build a four-bar rhythm in **quadruple time** from given motives.
- Ex. 7: using that rhythm, compose a four-bar melody **around the motive of a falling third, in
  A natural minor**.

Note the design: the same procedure, once in a major key with a rising interval and once in a
minor key with a falling one — Hewitt's "energy rising or falling / major or minor" questions
turned into an exercise.

**Exercise 1 and 2** answer from the text: the two axes are **pitch** (vertical) and **time**
(horizontal); **motives** generally employ two or three notes played with a strong, readily
identifiable **rhythm**.

## Key Takeaways
1. Melody lives on two axes, pitch and time — the sequencer window is a literal picture of this.
2. A motive is a two- or three-note cell whose identity comes from its rhythm.
3. Two motives in question/answer relation make a phrase; phrases build structure.
4. Write the rhythm first; then choose intervals to fit the emotional intent.
5. Build a personal catalog of interval → feeling associations; subjectivity is fine because it
   transfers to listeners.
6. Study existing songs motivically — Hewitt supplies a six-song starter list.
