# Chapter 13 — Chordal Inversions, Octave Doubling, and Spacing

## Core Idea
Knowing *which* chord to play is only half the job; the other half is **how to score it**. The
same triad can be inverted (a different note in the bass), doubled at the octave (thickened), and
spaced closed or open. These three choices are what give a progression "spread, coherence, and
logic," and together they produce **voice leading** — "this smooth, flowing connection between the
different voices."

## Frameworks Introduced

### The Jobs of the Three Triad Notes
| Note | Job |
|---|---|
| **Root** | "The most important note of a chord. Without a root, it would be impossible for the ear to tell which chord one is. So the root is the note from which the chord acquires its identity." |
| **Fifth** | "Gives the chord strength and backbone… a very strong and stable interval." |
| **Third** | "Gives the chord richness and fullness. In popular music harmony, the third is often called the **sweet note**." Also decides major versus minor. |

Diagnostic consequences: "when the third is absent, the chord has a bare, empty quality. When the
third is played on its own, the chord also sounds incomplete — it has richness but no backbone."

### The Three Positions of a Triad
| Position | Bass note | Character |
|---|---|---|
| **Root position** | Root | "Very strong and stable harmony compared to inverted chords. Consequently, most songs tend to end with a root position chord." |
| **First inversion** | Third | Less stable, but frequent in modern popular and electronic music |
| **Second inversion** | Fifth | Same |

**The find-the-inversions procedure:**
1. Locate the root position chord.
2. **To get first inversion, place the lowermost note up an octave.**
3. **To get second inversion, place the lowermost note of the first inversion triad up an octave.**

### Slash Chords
"In popular music song sheets, inverted chords are often represented by the note letter indicating
the root, followed by the note letter that represents the bass" — **C, C/E, C/G**. "For this
reason, inverted chords are sometimes referred to as **slash chords**. Slash chords give more
flexibility to the bass line. The bass can take any of the three notes of the chord. The freedom
this offers enables smoother and more melodic bass movement."

### Octave Doubling
Model case: a whole orchestra playing an A minor triad — flutes and piccolos high, strings spread
from bass to middle, double bass/contra-bassoon/tuba very low. "Writing a minor chord effectively
for these forces would necessitate the use of far more than three notes. To solve this problem,
certain notes are doubled at the octave."

**Rule: "The best notes to double tend to be the root and fifth because these give the chord its
identity and backbone."**

Figure 13.6's example: root doubled **twice**, fifth **once**, third **once** — "a chord of seven
notes that, because of octave doubling, still only uses root, third, and fifth of the triad," with
"a nice, even spread of notes over the pitch register." (Audio Track 32.)

### Spacing
"Spacing concerns the way the notes of the chord are spread within a balanced sonority."
- **Closed spacing** — "the notes of a chord are crowded together as closely as possible."
- **Open spacing** — "the chord is given a wider spread over the register."
- "Each single chord can be spaced in a variety of ways, closed and open. Each type of spacing
  will contribute to the overall effect of that sonority." (Audio Track 33.)

### Voice Leading
Figure 13.9 contrasts two versions of the same progression:
- **Version 1** ignores inversions, doubling, and spacing: "awkward, blocky, and very unmusical."
- **Version 2** takes them into account: "smooth, musical, and coherent. Notice the **sustained
  notes obtained by exploiting notes that the chords share in common**. The use of this technique
  helps knit the chords together."

Test: "Imagine if Figure 13.9 was scored for three vocalists. The arrangement on the left would be
very awkward to sing and would sound disjointed. The arrangement on the right would sound
smoother because each individual melodic part falls much easier to the voice." (Audio Track 34.)

### The Ear-Training Method
"To get a good grasp on inversions, doubling, and spacing, take a single chord and arrange it in
many different possible ways. Then listen to the chords and ascertain their strengths and
weaknesses. In this way, you will soon learn the best positions for your chords."

## Key Concepts
- **Harmony** — here defined as "this working together of different layers or tracks within the
  song": main lead, bass line, accompaniment (guitar strums, keyboard arpeggios), pads, strings.
  Figure 13.1 shows piano, strings, synth, and bass all resolving to an E minor chordal harmony.
- **Triads as the base unit** — "all other more complex chords used in chord progressions (such as
  sevenths, added sixth chords, ninths, and so on) build upon this foundation of triadic harmony."
- **Static harmonic field** — "Some forms of modern music rely on fairly static harmonic fields —
  that is, they are based on a single chord."
- **Bass and lead priority** — "in harmony — especially with many styles of popular music — the
  most important contributors are the bass and the lead."

## Mental Models
- **The chord progression is the glue between tracks.** It is "the factor that allows them all to
  work together."
- **The bass note chooses the position.** Which of the three chord tones you put underneath is a
  compositional decision with a stability consequence, not an accident.
- **Doubling is orchestration, not addition.** A seven-note chord can still be a plain triad.
- **Shared notes are the seams.** Hold over what the two chords have in common and the joint disappears.

## Anti-patterns
- Writing block chords in root position throughout — "awkward, blocky, and very unmusical."
- Omitting the third and wondering why the chord sounds bare and empty.
- Doubling the third rather than root and fifth when you need weight.
- Ignoring shared notes between consecutive chords and so producing disjointed part-writing.

## Reference Tables

### Positions and slash-chord symbols for a C major triad
| Position | Notes bottom-up | Bass note | Symbol |
|---|---|---|---|
| Root position | C E G | C (root) | **C** |
| First inversion | E G C | E (third) | **C/E** |
| Second inversion | G C E | G (fifth) | **C/G** |

### Doubling preferences
| Note | Double? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Root | Yes — best | Gives identity |
| Fifth | Yes — best | Gives backbone |
| Third | Sparingly | The "sweet note"; carries major/minor quality |

### Exercise 7's voice-leading checklist (the chapter's own tips)
- A. Make sure each chord is a complete triad.
- B. To ensure smooth voice leading, look out for shared notes that can be carried over through
  successive chords.
- C. Score for at least four separate parts, meaning that with each chord, one or more of the
  notes needs doubling.

## Worked Example
**Exercise 4:** "In the spaces provided, write out the inversions of the following chords."
The book supplies the pattern with its own worked first line, then six to complete. Applying the
chapter's procedure (move the lowest note up an octave to advance one inversion):

| Given | Asked for | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| A C E (root position Am) | *Example:* First inversion | **C E A** |
| C E G | Second inversion | **G C E** |
| F A C | First inversion | **A C F** |
| E G# B | Second inversion | **B E G#** |
| C E G# | First inversion | **E G# C** |
| G C E | Root position | **C E G** |
| A D F | First inversion | **D F A** |

Note two of the book's deliberate traps: **G C E** is already a second inversion of C major, so
its root position is **C E G**; and **A D F** is already a first inversion of D minor, so
"first inversion" of the *chord it represents* is the position given — the exercise trains you to
identify the chord from a non-root arrangement before naming positions.

**Exercise 1:** "A triad has three notes, which are called the root, the ___, and the ___" —
the **third** and the **fifth**.

**Exercise 5:** "There are two kinds of chord spacing: **Closed** and **open**. **Closed** spacing
is where the notes of a chord are crowded together as closely as possible, while **open** spacing
is where the chord is given a wider spread over the register."

**Exercise 6** is the chapter's core practical drill, reproduced: "Sequence the following chords
in as many different types of spacing as you can think of. An acoustic piano patch would be
suitable for this, or even a string orchestra patch. Feel free to double notes at the octave to
give a full and rich-sounding sonority… A. A minor triad — root position; B. C major triad —
first inversion; C. F major triad — second inversion; D. D minor triad — root position."

worked from the chapter's own procedure and definitions.)

## Key Takeaways
1. Root = identity, fifth = backbone, third = sweetness and major/minor quality.
2. Three positions per triad, determined by which chord tone is in the bass; root position is the
   most stable and is where songs usually end.
3. Move the lowest note up an octave to advance one inversion.
4. Slash chord notation (C/E, C/G) names the inversion by its bass note and frees up the bass line.
5. Double root and fifth for weight; a seven-note chord can still be a three-note triad.
6. Closed spacing = crowded, open spacing = spread; both are expressive choices.
7. Good voice leading exploits notes shared between consecutive chords.
