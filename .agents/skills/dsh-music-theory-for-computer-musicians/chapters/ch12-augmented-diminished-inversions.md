# Chapter 12 — Augmented and Diminished Intervals and Interval Inversions

## Core Idea
There is a **third family** of intervals beyond perfect and major/minor: the **augmented and
diminished** family. Augmenting adds a semitone to a perfect or major interval; diminishing
subtracts a semitone from a perfect or minor interval. Adding this family completes the system —
**28 intervals in total** — and every one of them pairs with an **inversion** that has the
opposite quality.

## Frameworks Introduced

### Augmentation and Diminution
- **Augmented** = "increased in size by a semitone." You can augment **any perfect or major
  interval**. "This rule doesn't apply to minor intervals because augmenting a minor interval
  gives a major interval."
- **Diminished** = "a perfect or minor interval that has been shrunk by a semitone." **Only
  perfect and minor intervals can be diminished.**
- Either the top note is raised/lowered or the bottom note is lowered/raised — both routes work
  (Exercises 1 and 2 make this explicit).
- Notation consequence: representing a **diminished third** requires a **double flat sign** in
  score edit view — "the note concerned is flattened twice and therefore is lowered by two semitones."

### How Many Forms Each Interval Takes
| Interval class | Forms available | Count |
|---|---|---|
| **Seconds, thirds, sixths, sevenths** (imperfect) | diminished, minor, major, augmented | 4 each |
| **Prime, fourth, fifth, octave** (perfect) | diminished, perfect, augmented | 3 each |

"In this way, you can see that our musical system recognizes a total of **28 intervals**."

### The Three Families of Intervals
A. **Perfect**
B. **Major / minor**
C. **Augmented / diminished**

### Interval Inversion
"Each interval has what is called its **inversion**." C up to G is a perfect fifth; G up to C is a
perfect fourth — "a different interval… but it uses the same notes. It is thus like an 'upside
down' perfect fifth."

**The inversion rules — inverted intervals have opposite qualities:**
- Major ↔ minor
- Augmented ↔ diminished
- Perfect ↔ perfect
- The interval numbers of a pair always sum to 9 (fifth ↔ fourth, third ↔ sixth, second ↔
  seventh, prime ↔ octave), which is what "upside down" means in practice.

### Where These Intervals Live
- **Diminished fifth B–F** in the C major scale — "the so-called *diabolus en musica*." Semitone
  count proof: the perfect fifth C–G has **seven** semitones; the diminished fifth B–F has **six**.
- **Augmented fifth** between the **third and seventh degrees of the harmonic minor scale** (in
  A harmonic minor: C to G#).
- **Augmented second** F–G# in the harmonic minor (Chapter 11's exotic interval).
- Figures 12.3 and 12.6 show the full sets of augmented and diminished intervals occurring in the
  A harmonic minor scale.

## Key Concepts
- **Double flat** — lowers a note by two semitones; required to spell diminished intervals.
- **Semitone counting as verification** — "You can easily verify this by doing a simple semitone
  count." This is the chapter's reliable test for any interval's quality.
- **Why this matters downstream:** knowledge of these intervals is "an essential foundation for
  the understanding and use of the augmented and diminished triads," of "artificial and exotic
  scales," and of "complex harmony." Knowledge of inversions is "of special importance when it
  comes to harmony," because it underlies chordal spacing, octave doubling, and chordal inversion.

## Mental Models
- **Three families, one system.** Perfect (fixed), major/minor (two-way variable),
  augmented/diminished (the stretched and squeezed versions of both).
- **Inversion flips the quality.** Turn an interval upside down and every attribute reverses
  except "perfect," which is its own mirror.
- **Building blocks, not trivia.** "All three of these [scales, melodic motives, harmonies] use
  these intervals as their building blocks."

## Anti-patterns
- Trying to augment a minor interval — you just get the major interval.
- Trying to diminish a major interval — you just get the minor interval.
- Spelling a diminished third without a double flat, which misnames the interval even though the
  sounding pitch is right.

## Worked Example
**The chapter's own worked verification — perfect versus diminished fifth (Figure 12.5):**

Earlier (Chapter 8) all the fifths in C major were shown to be perfect *bar one*: B–F. This
chapter proves why by semitone count.
- **C to G (perfect fifth):** C→C#→D→D#→E→F→F#→G = **7 semitones**.
- **B to F (diminished fifth):** B→C→C#→D→D#→E→F = **6 semitones**.
- Six is one fewer than seven, so B–F "is a diminished fifth because it is smaller than the
  perfect fifth by one semitone." This is the *diabolus in musica*.

**Exercise 1 and 2**, answered from the chapter's own definitions, using the chapter's own
example interval **G–D**:
1. "When an interval is **augmented**, it is increased in size by a semitone. Therefore, the
   perfect fifth — G to D — can become an augmented fifth by **sharpening** the top note D or
   **flattening** the bottom note G." (G–D# or Gb–D.)
2. "When an interval is diminished, it is **decreased/shrunk** in size by a semitone. Therefore,
   the perfect fifth — G to D — can become a diminished fifth by **flattening** the top note D or
   **sharpening** the bottom note G." (G–Db or G#–D.)

B. **Major/minor (imperfect)**; C. **Augmented/diminished**.

**Exercise 7:** "When inverted, a major interval gives a **minor** interval (and vice versa), and

## Key Takeaways
1. A third family completes the interval system: augmented (+1 semitone) and diminished (−1 semitone).
2. Only perfect and **major** intervals can be augmented; only perfect and **minor** intervals can
   be diminished.
3. Imperfect intervals have four forms, perfect intervals three — 28 intervals in total.
4. Every interval has an inversion whose number completes 9 and whose quality is the opposite.
5. Perfect inverts to perfect; major↔minor; augmented↔diminished.
6. Semitone counting is the reliable check; B–F = 6 semitones = diminished fifth.
