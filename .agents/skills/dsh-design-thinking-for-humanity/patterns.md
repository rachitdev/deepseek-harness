# Patterns — Design Thinking for Humanity

The book's reusable moves, grouped by lens. Each is stated as **When / How / Trade-offs**.

---

## Part 0 — Framing and orientation

### Convert the spotlight into a floodlight
**When** — Your process is human-centered but the environment, the supply chain, and the long-term consequence are outside the frame.
**How** — Promote the ecosystem "from a passive backdrop into a critical stakeholder." Give non-human and future stakeholders a profile, a name on the stakeholder map, and a human steward assigned to speak for them.
**Trade-offs** — Slows convergence and multiplies the constraint set; a team without a facilitator will drown in stakeholders. Mitigate by making the boundary an explicit, revisitable decision.

### Run the Impact Checklist before touching a tool
**When** — At project start and at each major milestone.
**How** — Fifteen questions, three per lens, scored yes / partly / not yet. Cluster the "not yet" answers; the block with the most weak answers names the lens and toolbox to open next.
**Trade-offs** — It certifies nothing. Treated as a compliance gate passed once, it becomes theater. Its value is conversational and diagnostic.

### Reverse-engineer strategy from the preferable future
**When** — The problem's root causes sit outside a 1–5 year planning horizon.
**How** — Anchor at 20+ years with a preferable future, then work back through the 10-year possible/plausible band (exponential and systems thinking) into 1–5 year action (design thinking). Ecological thinking sits on the outer ring as non-negotiable context.
**Trade-offs** — Long-horizon anchoring is hard to defend against quarterly incentives; pair it with no-regret moves that pay off in any scenario.

### Never accept "resilient" as the design target
**When** — A recovery plan restores the pre-shock state.
**How** — Rank the four responses — decay, robust, resilient, exponentializing — and design for the fourth: the system that uses disruption as a catalyst and bounces *forward*.
**Trade-offs** — Bouncing forward means accepting change the organization may have been trying to avoid; robustness is politically easier to sell.

## Part 1 — Futures thinking

### Use the future as a laboratory, not as a forecast
**When** — Leadership demands a single-point prediction.
**How** — Build multiple plausible scenarios and wind-tunnel the strategy through them. Test for *resilience across scenarios*, not for *accuracy of one*.
**Trade-offs** — Multiple scenarios feel indecisive; the answer is to converge on no-regret moves and named signposts, not on one favored future.

### Hunt for no-regret moves
**When** — You must act now despite unresolved uncertainty.
**How** — Identify interventions valuable in every scenario. In the case study, low-cost soil sensors qualify — they pay off whether the future is techno-food, bio-economy, or soil-health nexus.
**Trade-offs** — No-regret moves are, by construction, not the boldest moves. They buy option value, not transformation.

### Scan for weak signals, not for news
**When** — Horizon scanning keeps returning consensus trends.
**How** — Source from scientific journals, patent filings, niche blogs, art projects and expert interviews. Test each signal: *Is this novel? Does it have the potential to grow?*
**Trade-offs** — High noise ratio; requires sustained scanning rather than a one-off workshop.

### Deconstruct with CLA, then reconstruct with it
**When** — A problem keeps returning after being "solved."
**How** — Drop through litany → systemic cause → worldview → metaphor/myth, then build a new solution back up through the same four layers.
**Trade-offs** — The deeper layers are contested and slow; teams comfortable at the litany level will resist.

## Part 2 — Design thinking

### Explore the problem space before any solution is attempted
**When** — Always; especially when a solution has already been named in the brief.
**How** — First diamond for the problem space ("solving the right problem"), second for the solution space ("solving the problem right"), with a futures-guided *future space* prepended.
**Trade-offs** — The most frequently skipped discipline, because it delays visible output.

### Expand empathy in scope, not only in depth
**When** — Your research is rich about one user and silent about everyone else affected.
**How** — Move from the persona to the Systemic Empathy Map: what the *system* says, does, thinks and feels. Give non-human stakeholders their own profiles.
**Trade-offs** — Abstraction risk — a system does not literally feel. Keep at least one real person's story anchoring the map.

### Aim the How Might We at a leverage point
**When** — Drafting the HMW after synthesis.
**How** — Test the question: does it address a symptom or the structure that produces the symptom? A symptom-level HMW yields "an elegant solution to the wrong problem."
**Trade-offs** — Leverage-point HMWs are harder to prototype and often implicate policy, governance, or business model rather than product.

### Treat policy and governance as design inputs, not fixed constraints
**When** — The blocker to a systemic solution is institutional.
**How** — Prototype the policy alongside the product; validate institutional change, not just user adoption. "Institutional change, not user adoption, gates systemic change."
**Trade-offs** — Long feedback loops; needs allies inside institutions before it can be tested.

### Treat the story as a prototype
**When** — The data is correct and nobody moves.
**How** — Build the narrative on the Narrative Design Canvas — deconstruct the present ("as-is"), design the future ("to-be"), inspire action (the call) — and test it like any other prototype.
**Trade-offs** — Narrative is easy to slide into spin; the Litmus Test and Ethical Consequences Scanner are the guardrails.

### Scan consequences before committing
**When** — A solution looks unambiguously good.
**How** — Run the Ethical Consequences Scanner along consequence paths, then apply the Litmus Test: does it centralize power or democratize it; does it extract or regenerate?
**Trade-offs** — Generates uncomfortable findings late in a sprint. That is the point.

## Part 3 — Ecological thinking

### Move from "less bad" to "more good"
**When** — The sustainability story is a list of reductions.
**How** — Test the solution against regeneration, not harm-reduction: does it *actively restore* the ecosystems it touches? Track handprint, not only footprint.
**Trade-offs** — Regenerative claims demand measurement infrastructure that reductions do not.

### Diagnose the doughnut before designing in it
**When** — Setting the constraint envelope for a solution.
**How** — Map the challenge between the social foundation and the ecological ceiling; downscale the global doughnut to the local context before designing.
**Trade-offs** — Downscaling requires local data that may not exist; be explicit where you are estimating.

### Let the LCA hotspot write the brief
**When** — You have a lifecycle map and limited redesign budget.
**How** — Find the stage with the largest impact and treat it as the design brief. Do not distribute effort evenly across the lifecycle. The use phase is the stage most often overlooked.
**Trade-offs** — Optimizing one hotspot can shift the burden elsewhere; re-run the LCA after the redesign.

### Identify the principle, not the shape (biomimicry)
**When** — Borrowing from nature.
**How** — Emulate at the level of *process* or *ecosystem*, not just *form*: define the design challenge as a function, find nature's champions for that function, abstract the principle.
**Trade-offs** — Genuine biomimicry is slow research; shape-copying is fast and mostly cosmetic.

### Make structural support beat persuasion
**When** — The desired behavior depends on people choosing well.
**How** — Design nudges, social norms and feedback loops so the sustainable choice is easy, attractive and socially rewarding. Focus on a single behavior at a time.
**Trade-offs** — Nudging alone is not a strategy; without structural change it shifts responsibility onto individuals.

## Part 4 — Systems thinking

### Make the boundary a conscious choice
**When** — Beginning any systemic analysis.
**How** — Debate and agree the boundary explicitly, check it ("broad enough to capture root cause, narrow enough to be actionable"), and be prepared to expand it as understanding deepens.
**Trade-offs** — A wider boundary buys insight and costs actionability. There is no correct answer, only a made decision.

### Assign a steward to every silent stakeholder
**When** — Non-human or future stakeholders are on the map.
**How** — Give each one a named team member whose job is to speak in its voice — e.g. a designated advocate for "future generations." Stress-test with the Silent Stakeholder question.
**Trade-offs** — Role-play can become tokenism; rotate the roles and back them with data.

### Map what is missing
**When** — The system map looks complete.
**How** — In a different color, mark the missing connections, the broken feedback loops, and the excluded stakeholders. "This often reveals the most potent areas for a design intervention."
**Trade-offs** — Absence is inferential; verify with the people actually excluded.

### Narrate every loop
**When** — A CLD has been drawn.
**How** — Name each loop and tell its story in one sentence. Mark delays with `//`. Then answer: which loop is dominant right now — the engine or the brakes?
**Trade-offs** — Narration simplifies; a story that is too clean hides the delay that makes the system unpredictable.

### Climb the leverage hierarchy before choosing
**When** — You have a list of candidate interventions.
**How** — Rank them: numbers < buffers/stocks < material flows & delays < loops < information flows < rules < goals < paradigm. Reject the shallow ones unless they buy time for a deep one.
**Trade-offs** — Deep interventions are slower, more political and more likely to fail; shallow ones deliver visible short-term relief. Sequence them deliberately rather than substituting one for the other.

### Change the goal instead of tuning the parameters
**When** — The system keeps resisting tweaks.
**How** — Rewrite the system's definition of success. "If a system aims to 'maximize yield,' it will resist sustainability. By rewriting the goal to 'maximize soil health,' the entire system… will naturally self-organize around this new North Star."
**Trade-offs** — Goal change requires legitimacy you may not yet have; usually needs the transition strategy to run first.

### Close the loop on the insulated actor
**When** — Decision-makers are shielded from their own consequences.
**How** — Deliver impact data directly back to the source; make invisible metrics (soil health, labor conditions) visible to consumers and investors. "Often more effectively than regulation."
**Trade-offs** — Transparency is only as good as the data's credibility, which is why trust is itself a leverage point.

### Hunt for the asymmetrical relationship
**When** — Analyzing a value network.
**How** — Find the stakeholder giving significant value and receiving little. "These imbalances are often sources of systemic instability and represent potent leverage points."
**Trade-offs** — Rebalancing usually means someone currently benefiting gives something up — a political problem, not an analytical one.

### Stress-test the ecosystem with four questions
**When** — The Win-Win-Win canvas is filled in and looks good.
**How** — Run all four: Decoupling · Silent Stakeholder · Orchestrator's Ego · Success as a Threat.
**Trade-offs** — Failing one of these late is expensive; run them early, when the canvas is still cheap to change.

### Start with a Minimum Viable Ecosystem
**When** — The solution requires many partners.
**How** — Identify "the smallest possible configuration of partners that can create and deliver a core piece of the 'win-win-win' value proposition," and test the core assumptions there before scaling.
**Trade-offs** — A too-small MVE may not demonstrate the systemic effect that justifies the design.

### Orchestrate as a steward, not a commander
**When** — You hold the central position in an ecosystem.
**How** — Maintain the health of the whole, foster trust between partners, keep the ecosystem true to its MTP, and design governance so the network survives your departure.
**Trade-offs** — Ceding control slows decisions and reduces the orchestrator's capture of value — which is the intended effect.

### Design the transition, not only the destination
**When** — A regenerative solution is designed but not adopted.
**How** — Fill the four quadrants: power & politics (blockers, unlikely allies), economics & incentives (the valley of death, the safe harbor), narrative & persuasion (a different story per audience), pathways to legitimacy (the minimum viable transition).
**Trade-offs** — Political work is unglamorous and unattributable. But: "A brilliant regenerative solution that is politically unviable or economically threatening to powerful incumbents will inevitably fail."

### Subsidize the risk of crossing the valley of death
**When** — Adoption requires a period where the adopter is worse off.
**How** — Design a financial bridge — floor prices, yield insurance, premium pricing, transition subscriptions — that carries the adopter across. In the case study, the Safe Harbor Contract.
**Trade-offs** — Someone must fund the bridge; the pattern works only when a downstream beneficiary (a Scope 3 buyer, an insurer, a government) can be shown the return.

### Recruit the unlikely ally
**When** — The incumbent coalition looks impregnable.
**How** — Find stakeholders who appear opposed but hold a hidden long-term interest in resilience; build the demand alliance around them.
**Trade-offs** — Alliances with incumbents can dilute the mandate; guard the MTP as the non-negotiable.

### Run the transition through an OODA loop
**When** — The plan is written and the ground keeps moving.
**How** — Observe political and economic signals → Orient against the CLD to catch unintended consequences and leverage shifts → Decide by impact on resilience assets and the MVT → Act as a high-impact experiment → repeat.
**Trade-offs** — Continuous re-planning can become drift; the MTP is what keeps the loop anchored.

## Part 5 — Exponential thinking

### Frame human, generate machine, validate machine, curate human
**When** — Using AI anywhere in the innovation process.
**How** — Follow the Exponential Innovation Cycle: humans define MTP and Doughnut guardrails and translate values into computational constraints; AI explores the solution space; digital twins simulate; humans apply ethical judgment and select.
**Trade-offs** — Two mandatory human phases slow the cycle. That is the price of not scaling a mistake.

### Demand 10X positive impact, not 10X growth
**When** — Assessing whether a solution deserves to scale.
**How** — Add a regenerative/positive-impact dimension to the disruption scorecard. Ask: "Does it generate net-positive externalities for society and the planet? Does it fit within the Doughnut boundaries?"
**Trade-offs** — Rules out otherwise attractive ventures; the book's position is that this is the intended function.

### Use the MTP as a kill filter
**When** — A profitable or technically exciting opportunity appears.
**How** — Ask "Does this get us closer to achieving our MTP?" If no, reconsider it "regardless of how profitable or technologically interesting it may seem."
**Trade-offs** — Only works if the MTP is authentic and leadership will actually enforce a no.

### Think in doublings and watch for convergence
**When** — Assessing whether a trend is genuinely exponential.
**How** — Look for a measurable metric doubling (or a cost halving) at a consistent rate — cost per sequenced genome, chip processing power, battery energy density. Then watch where two exponential curves converge.
**Trade-offs** — Rigor here costs speed and rules out most of what marketing calls exponential.

### Use the deceptive phase as the inclusion window
**When** — A technology is growing slowly and looks unimportant.
**How** — Co-design with marginalized communities now, "ensuring the initial architecture is inherently inclusive and avoids creating new forms of bias," before the disruptive knee makes the architecture unchangeable.
**Trade-offs** — Investing in a technology nobody yet takes seriously is a hard internal sell.

### Push impact analysis to the third ring
**When** — First-order benefits are being presented as the case for a technology.
**How** — Ask "If this happens, then what happens?" recursively. First-order impacts "are often the intended benefits that are featured in marketing materials"; the risk lives in rings two and three.
**Trade-offs** — Third-order effects are speculative; use them to design guardrails, not to make predictions.

### Scale empathy 1x → 100x → 1,000,000x
**When** — A solution designed for one person is about to reach many.
**How** — Map the individual persona, then the community dynamics at 100x (status, friction, new interactions), then society at 1,000,000x (new cultural norms, power shifts, how people will hack the system).
**Trade-offs** — Emergent dynamics cannot be verified in advance; the output is a watchlist and a set of governance choices, not a forecast.

### End every ethics analysis in a mitigation
**When** — The Exponential Ethics Compass is filled in.
**How** — For each significant negative impact, design a concrete guardrail or a change to the solution. "The goal is not just to list risks but to actively design guardrails."
**Trade-offs** — Some risks have no acceptable mitigation, which converts the analysis into a veto.

### Simulate before you release
**When** — A failure would scale to millions in milliseconds.
**How** — Test in digital twins: compress decades of impact into minutes, inject external shocks (pandemics, supply chain collapse, extreme climate events), and hunt second-order effects such as biodiversity collapse from monoculture efficiency or workforce displacement.
**Trade-offs** — Simulation fidelity is finite and a twin can be wrong in ways that look confident.

### Institute the ethical pause and reserve the right to say no
**When** — At the Curate & Refine step, and at every major scaling decision.
**How** — Stop, apply the Litmus Test, interrogate data lineage, demand explainability. If the answer is negative, veto or redesign "regardless of its profit potential."
**Trade-offs** — The pause has an organizational cost and requires someone with the standing to enforce it. "The capacity to build it does not justify the right to scale it."

### Engineer the movement, don't hope for it
**When** — Adoption must spread faster than an organization can push it.
**How** — Define the core narrative and member identity, design low-friction onboarding that grants immediate belonging, then engineer the viral loop through incentives, shareable output, and community platforms.
**Trade-offs** — Viral mechanics can be gamed and can attract members loosely bound to the purpose; the MTP is the filter.

### Finish on the Resilience Blueprint — and keep it alive
**When** — Work across all five lenses has accumulated.
**How** — Fill the nine blocks in lens order: the future we want (futures) → the human need (design) → the regenerative engine (ecological) → the systemic strategy (systems) → the path to scale (exponential). Keep the Costs block honest, each cost paired with its mitigation.
**Trade-offs** — A one-page synthesis compresses away nuance; treat it as an index into the underlying work and "a living document that can be updated as you learn."
