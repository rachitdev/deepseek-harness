# Chapter 10 — Ecological Thinking Toolbox

*Pages 202–217. Six tools, plus the extension "Scaling to Systemic Context."*

## Core Idea

These tools move a team "beyond simply 'doing less harm' to actively creating solutions that restore and revitalize the social and ecological systems they are a part of." They cover three jobs: understanding material and production impact (LCA, Design for Durability), framing within a safe and just space (Doughnut), and generating regenerative designs and behaviors (Biomimicry & Permaculture, Regenerative Business Model Canvas, Sustainable Behavior Design Canvas).

## Frameworks Introduced

### The Doughnut Economics Model (Kate Raworth)
*Steps:* (1) **Understand the social foundation** — the inner ring, "a set of critical social outcomes, derived from the UN's Sustainable Development Goals … food security, housing, education, health, social equity"; the goal is that "no one is left in the 'hole' in the middle." (2) **Understand the ecological ceiling** — the outer ring, the nine planetary boundaries (climate change, biodiversity loss, freshwater use, air pollution, land conversion, freshwater withdrawals); the goal is to avoid overshoot. (3) **Design for the doughnut** — the space between the two is "the safe and just space for humanity"; design solutions, business models and systems that operate entirely within it.

*Expert tips:* **Use it as a diagnostic tool** first — map a city, region or industry: "Where are the most significant social shortfalls? Where are the most critical ecological overshoots?" **From "less bad" to "more good."** **Downscale the doughnut** — run a workshop where a team or community builds its own "City Doughnut" or "Business Doughnut"; contextualizing makes it a tool for local action.

### Biomimicry & Permaculture
*Steps:* (1) **Define the design challenge as a function** — e.g. "How can we create a building facade that passively cools itself in a hot climate?" (2) **Identify nature's champions** — "biologize" the problem: "How do desert animals or plants stay cool without air conditioning?" leading to termite-mound self-regulating ventilation or heat-reflecting plant leaves. "The goal is to identify the underlying principle, not just to copy the form." (3) **Apply ecosystem principles (permaculture)**:
- **Stacking functions** — "How can this solution perform multiple functions (e.g. the facade not only cools but also harvests rainwater)?"
- **Creating no waste** — "How can the outputs of this system become inputs for another (e.g. the harvested water irrigates a green roof)?"
- **Integration, not segregation** — how the solution integrates into the larger beneficial system.

### Life Cycle Assessment (LCA) Simplified — the canvas
*1. Map lifecycle stages* — 1.1 Raw material extraction ("How are the raw materials acquired?") · 1.2 Manufacturing & processing ("What energy and resources are used to produce the final product?") · 1.3 Distribution ("How is the product transported to the user?") · 1.4 Use phase ("What is the environmental impact during the product's active use — energy consumption, emissions?") · 1.5 End-of-life ("Is it landfilled, recycled, or composted?").
*2. Identify 2–4 key impacts at each stage* — energy consumption, water use, carbon emissions, pollution, waste generation.
*3. Identify the hotspot(s) and focus* — **Hotspot selection** (which stage poses the most critical environmental challenge) · **Reasoning/evidence** (specific impacts: high energy use, toxic waste) · optional second hotspot · **Hotspot focus** ("Reduce the environmental impact associated with the ___ stage") · **Next actionable step.**

*Expert tips:* **The use phase is often overlooked** — "for many products, especially electronics or appliances, the most significant environmental impact occurs during the use phase due to energy consumption. Do not just focus on making the product out of sustainable materials; also focus on making it radically more efficient to use." **Use it as a comparative tool** — build simplified LCAs for two alternatives (plastic vs a new biomaterial) to decide which path is genuinely more sustainable. And explicitly: LCA exists to **avoid burden shifting**, "where solving a problem in one area (reducing manufacturing energy) creates a new problem elsewhere (using a material that is impossible to recycle)."

### Regenerative Business Model Canvas — four steps
1. **Define purpose** — "Set the audacious 'win-win-win' mission. State the net-positive impact the business aims to create for the planet, people, and society."
2. **Map the system** — identify living capitals (soil health, trust) as core assets; map partners, stakeholders and customer segments "you depend on and have a responsibility to enhance."
3. **Design regenerative flows** — (re)design the value proposition and key activities to deliver simultaneous ecological, social and financial value; channels and relationships that foster mutualistic, continuous engagement.
4. **Design regenerative value streams** — "detail costs, prioritizing reinvestment back into the living capitals. Define regenerative value streams where financial returns are explicitly linked to, and inseparable from, positive ecological and social outcomes."

*Expert tips:* **Think in systems, not silos** — "map the feedback loops between your activities, your impact on living capitals, and your financial success. The goal is to design a business where positive ecological and social outcomes are a direct driver of profitability." **Co-evolve with your community.** **Embrace an adaptive mindset** — treat the canvas as a living document.

### Design for Durability, Repair & Disassembly — the checklist
**1. Durability** — "Have we selected the most robust and long-lasting materials available for this product's intended use?" · "Is the design timeless, or is it based on a short-term trend that will quickly look dated?" (The aim: "objects that users form a lasting emotional connection with.")
**2. Repair** — "Can the product be opened and reassembled using standard, common tools?" · "Are key components modular and easily replaceable if they fail?" (Plus: standard fasteners instead of proprietary screws or glues; clear instructions; a reliable supply of spare parts.)
**3. Disassembly** — "Have we avoided using permanent glues or welds, favoring screws or clips instead?" · "Is the product made from a few, clearly marked **mono-materials** to make recycling simple and effective?"

### Sustainable Behavior Design Canvas — the six boxes plus feedback loops

| Box | Prompt |
|---|---|
| **Current behavior** (WHAT?) | "What is the current, undesirable default behavior we need to disrupt?" |
| **Target behavior** (WHAT?) | "What is the smallest, single action that would create the largest positive impact if adopted at scale?" |
| **Barriers** (WHY?) | "What immediate and structural friction prevents the target audience from doing this already?" |
| **Drivers** (WHY?) | "What deep human motivation or purpose does the target behavior fulfill, and what emotional benefit can we amplify?" |
| **Nudges** (HOW?) | "How can we make the desired action instant, effortless, and friction-free?" |
| **Social norm** (HOW?) | "How can we visibly celebrate the behavior of early adopters to make the new norm compelling and widely desirable?" |
| **Feedback loops** | "How can we immediately show the user the value of their action (e.g. carbon sequestered, water saved, community strengthened)?" |

*Expert tips:* **Make it easy** — "the single most important principle of behavior change … Before designing a complex incentive program, first focus on removing every point of friction." **Focus on a single behavior at a time** — success on one often creates positive spillover. **Test, learn, and iterate** — "Behavioral design is not an exact science; it is an experimental one. Always treat your interventions as prototypes."

### Extension — Scaling to Systemic Context

| Lever | The question | The design mandate | Leverage point |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. The identity mandate** | "How do we make the intervention a catalyst for a lasting identity shift rooted in purpose and legacy?" | "Design all dashboards, feedback loops, and communication to reinforce the desired regenerative identity (e.g. planetary steward instead of commodity producer)." | Shift the psychological goal "from anxiety reduction to a sense of profound purpose and collective efficacy." |
| **2. The policy lever** | "What single policy change would structurally mandate or incentivize this behavior at a community or state level?" | "Design the solution to integrate with a single, high-leverage policy (e.g. a carbon tax, a subsidy for ecosystem services, or new procurement rules)." | Make the regenerative choice "the easiest, most advantageous, and inevitable default by integrating policy directly into the system's architecture." |
| **3. The tipping point** | "What is the critical mass (threshold) we need to reach to trigger a nonlinear cascade of adoption across the entire system?" | "Amplify the success stories of influential early adopters (social proof) and leverage intergenerational vectors (e.g. children's engagement) to create powerful social contagion." | Narrative engineering — fund future storytelling that frames the transition "not as a costly risk but as a path to a more connected, healthy, and meaningful way of life." |

## Mental Models

- **Diagnose the doughnut before you design in it.** Shortfalls and overshoots are the brief.
- **Identify the principle, not the shape.** Biomimicry copies mechanisms, not silhouettes.
- **The hotspot is the brief for redesign.** Everything else is noise until the biggest impact stage is named with evidence.
- **Structural support beats persuasion.** "All micro-level behavioral interventions must be structurally supported by policy or system design to make the regenerative choice the inevitable default."

## Anti-patterns

- Sustainable materials on a product that is wildly inefficient in its use phase.
- Permanent glues, welds and blended composites — a disassembly failure designed in at the start.
- Changing many behaviors at once and measuring none of them precisely.
- Treating the Regenerative Business Model Canvas as a one-off document rather than a monitored, adapting one.

## Worked Example — LCA and behavior design on the Carbon Harvest sensors

The book applies this toolbox to the running case (Case Study Part 3, Ch 9) — reproduced here as the tool output rather than the narrative:

**Simplified LCA canvas, filled in for the soil-sensor network:**

| Stage | Mapped content |
|---|---|
| 1.1 Raw material | Extraction of materials for the sensors |
| 1.2 Manufacturing & processing | Energy used in sensor manufacturing |
| 1.3 Distribution | Logistics of deployment across thousands of farms |
| 1.4 Use phase | Operational energy — solar-powered |
| 1.5 End-of-life | Electronic waste at the end of a 10-year lifespan |

- **Hotspot selection:** End-of-life. *Reasoning/evidence:* "The biggest potential negative impact is not the operational energy (which will be solar-powered) but the creation of electronic waste at the end of the sensors' 10-year lifespan."
- **Hotspot focus:** Reduce the environmental impact associated with the end-of-life stage.
- **Next actionable step / design intervention:** Apply Design for Durability, Repair & Disassembly — "the sensors must be designed using modular components and fully circular, nontoxic materials." Checked against the tool's own list: mono-materials, screws or clips instead of glues, modular replaceable components.

**Sustainable Behavior Design Canvas, filled in for sensor adoption:**

| Box | Filled content |
|---|---|
| Target behavior | "Encourage farmers to install and trust the Carbon Harvest sensor network." |
| Barriers | Javier's skepticism of new technology; fear of data misuse; up-front cost and effort of installation. |
| Drivers | Desire for a new, stable income stream; the deep-seated wish to leave healthy land for his children. |
| Nudges | "Make the onboarding process incredibly simple, with a 'white glove' installation service." |
| Social norm | A community platform showing testimonials and real-time success stories from other respected farmers in his region. |
| Feedback loops | The app gives immediate, tangible feedback showing how regenerative practices improve soil health and increase regeneration credits. |

**Scaling extension applied:** *Identity* — dashboard leads with the Soil Health Score and watershed Net Water Balance, not profit. *Policy* — integrate with a subsidy for ecosystem services so lower-interest loans follow a good Soil Health Score. *Tipping point* — amplify influential farmers and use the gamified children's module as the intergenerational vector.

**A second, generic canvas example given in the tool page:** target behavior — "Increase the percentage of residents in a building who correctly sort their organic waste for composting." *Barriers:* inconvenient bin location, confusion about what to compost, lack of perceived benefit. *Drivers:* desire to help the environment, seeing neighbors do it, financial incentives. *Nudge:* "place a visually attractive compost bin right next to the main trash can." *Social proof:* share statistics on the building's collective positive impact. *Feedback loop:* "a smart bin that shows the weight of composted material and its environmental benefit."

## Key Takeaways

1. Frame with the Doughnut, measure with LCA, redesign at the hotspot, lock it in with Design for Durability.
2. Biomimicry has three levels; ecosystem emulation is the systemic one.
3. The behavior canvas is six boxes and a feedback loop — target one behavior, remove friction first.
4. Every behavioral intervention needs an identity, a policy and a tipping-point plan to become the default.
5. Regenerative business models tie financial return inseparably to verified ecological and social outcomes.
