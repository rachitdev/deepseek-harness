# Chapter 8 — Design Thinking Toolbox

*Pages 142–165. Six advanced tools for systemic interventions — three for the problem space, three for the solution space — plus the Future Persona Stereotypes method and the Ethical Litmus Test.*

## Core Idea

This toolbox is explicitly **a supplement, not a replacement**: "We do not discard foundational tools like Interview for Empathy or Brainstorming" from *The Design Thinking Toolbox*. These six are "specialized implements" that move a team "from human-centered to a Design Thinking for Humanity perspective."

## Reference Table — The six tools

| # | Tool | Space | Purpose in one line |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Future Persona** | Problem | Adapts the classic persona by profiling a person living inside a specific future scenario. |
| 2 | **Systemic Empathy Map** | Problem | Expands the empathy map from one individual to an entire ecosystem of stakeholders — "communities, partners, and even the planet." |
| 3 | **Wicked Problem Framing** | Problem | A structured canvas for deconstructing a wicked problem "without oversimplifying it," ending in a leverage-point HMW. |
| 4 | **Value Proposition for Humanity Canvas** | Solution | Evolves the Value Proposition Canvas by adding "value to society" and "value to the planet" — forcing a win-win-win. |
| 5 | **Future Storytelling & Narrative Prototyping** | Solution | Prototypes services, policies and behavioral change as a story instead of a mock-up. |
| 6 | **Ethical Consequences Scanner** | Solution | Futures-Wheel-style mapping of *negative* unintended consequences across social groups and time. |

## Frameworks Introduced

### Future Persona Canvas — the fields
- **Future scenario / persona name / quote.**
- **Profile:** *Demographics* — "How old is the person? What is their occupation and social role in this future society?"; *Bio* — backstory and the significant life events that led them there.
- **Their world:** *Key technologies* — "What technologies do they use daily? What new skills have they had to learn?"; *Societal norms* — "What are the new social rules or expectations in this future world?"
- **Values & motivations:** *Pains* — biggest frustrations, fears, challenges, "the core conflict they experience in their daily life"; *Gains* — hopes, desires, measures of success; *Jobs-to-be-done* — the fundamental task or goal.
- **Day in the life:** a narrative description of a typical day, plus **Tensions and contradictions** — "Where does their personal experience conflict with the promises of the future world?"

*Application steps:* (1) Select a future scenario from Scenario Planning & the 2×2 Matrix; (2) create the persona's profile; (3) write the day in the life, which becomes the input for Future Storytelling.

### Future Persona Stereotypes × Futures Wheel
Twelve archetypes used as creative shortcuts: **The Joker** (challenges conventions, injects levity, reveals absurdities), **The Lover** (deep connection, emotional and relational aspects), **The Hero** (overcomes great challenges, drives the story), **The Nurturer** (support, care, well-being and security of others), **The Rebel** (challenges the status quo, disrupts established norms), **The Explorer** (ventures into the unknown, new possibilities), **The Visionary** (envisions a radically different future, inspires with innovation), **The Innocent** (optimistic outlook, highlights the gap between the dream and complex reality), **The Loyalist** (preserves core values and relationships, an anchor of stability), **The Ruler** (order, governance, how power and control operate), **The Sage** (wisdom and truth through introspection; mentor or guide), **The Everyman** (common values and experiences of the general population).

*Method:* choose a future scenario → select a stereotype that creates tension in it → give it a future identity → **place a core decision at the center of a Futures Wheel** → map first/second/third-order consequences → synthesize the character narrative.

### Systemic Empathy Map — steps
1. **Identify key stakeholders** from Stakeholder Analysis & Mapping — "not only users and customers but also partners, employees, community members, and crucial non-human stakeholders like 'The Local Watershed' or 'Future Generations.'"
2. **Create a mini empathy map for each** — see / say / do / hear, then pains and gains.
3. **Map the systemic connections and tensions** — "Where does the 'gain' of one stakeholder create a 'pain' for another? Where are their interests aligned? Identifying these tensions and synergies is where the most powerful systemic insights are found."

### System Empathy Map Canvas (the aggregation layer)
Treats the whole system as a single entity with its own thoughts and behaviors. Columns: **Challenge focus** (scope) · **Who is impacted?** ("Beyond the direct user, list all stakeholders, environments, and future generations affected") · **System Thinks & Feels** ("What are the underlying assumptions, cultural norms, or deeply held beliefs within the system … the emotional, social, or environmental impacts and sentiments") · **System Says & Does** ("What are the prevailing narratives, policies, data, or media messages … the current behaviors, processes, actions, or policies"). Purpose: "reveal the deeply held cultural beliefs and institutional inertia that sustain complex problems."

### Wicked Problem Framing — the canvas and its four boxes
1. **The broad wicked problem statement** — "What is the overarching, multilayered, and contradictory problem we are trying to solve?" *Example:* "How might we build resilient food systems for urban populations in the face of climate change?"
2. **Stakeholders** — who is affected or has influence (farmers, city dwellers, governments, corporations, ecosystems).
3. **Conflicting values** — "What trade-offs are the key actors being forced to make (e.g. short-term profit versus long-term health)?" Examples given: food affordability vs ecological health; economic efficiency vs community resilience.
4. **Systemic drivers** — global supply chains, consumer habits, agricultural policies.
5. **Leverage point identification** — "Where can a small intervention create the greatest positive ripple effect that solves a pain for the persona?"

**The HMW formula:** *"How might we" + [action verb] + [intended action] + "for" [specific user/context] + "so that" [desired benefit].*
*Reframed example:* "How might we create a network of shared urban community gardens for low-income neighborhoods so that they can increase their access to nutritious food and build stronger community bonds?"

### Value Proposition for Humanity Canvas — structure
Left side, **The System Profile**, in three bands (Planetary / People / Society), each with Pains and Gains; right side, **The Value for Humanity Map** — the solution and its value creators for the planet, the people, and society.

*Steps:* (1) Define the system profile from the Future Persona plus the Systemic Empathy Map; (2) map the solution's value to the persona's pains and gains; (3) **expand to a win-win-win** by adding *Value to Planet* ("Does it use regenerative materials? Does it improve biodiversity?") and *Value to Society* ("Does it foster connection or strengthen social fabric?").

*Two critical shifts:*
- **Expand the "customer" profile beyond the buyer** — create separate, defined segments (and profiles with pains and gains) for the planet and society, including non-paying beneficiaries and communities.
- **Prioritize systemic impact over immediate revenue** — "Instead of ranking pain relievers and gain creators by revenue potential, change the criteria to focus on systemic impact": features that address planetary pains (carbon reduction) and equitable social gains (access for vulnerable groups) rank first.

The reformulated innovation question: *"We no longer ask, 'Does this make profit?' We ask, 'Does this create profit only by simultaneously increasing the health of the planet, the resilience of society, and the well-being of the user?'"*

### Future Storytelling & Narrative Prototyping — steps
(1) Choose the persona and the win-win-win value proposition; (2) define the narrative arc — **The Situation** (context and challenge), **The Intervention** (how they interact with the new solution/service/system), **The Resolution** (the new reality and positive outcomes for persona, society and planet); (3) create the narrative prototype as a written story, storyboard or comic strip, audio prototype, or video prototype.

### Ethical Consequences Scanner — steps and prompts
(1) Place the solution at the center; (2) brainstorm first-order negative consequences, asking: **"Who could be harmed by this?"**, **"What is the worst-case-scenario use of our product?"**, **"What existing jobs or systems might this disrupt?"**; (3) map second- and third-order consequences — "If that negative thing happens, what happens next?"

### Extension — The Ethical Litmus Test
**1. The question of equity (social impact)** — Who is unintentionally harmed, and who benefits most? Does it create a new social or digital divide? Is power centralized or decentralized — does it "consolidate power within a single organization or technology (like an AI agent), or does it democratize access"? Have we designed for a **"Just Transition"** that mitigates short-term harm to displaced communities or industries?
**2. The question of integrity (ecological impact)** — Does the solution violate the non-negotiable ecological boundary (biodiversity loss, freshwater depletion)? Is it truly regenerative "or just 'less bad'"? **Are we shifting the burden** — does solving carbon emissions create a worse problem elsewhere, such as materials generating toxic waste?
**3. The question of legacy (temporal impact)** — What is the long-term intergenerational impact, 50 years out? "Are we accelerating learning or dependency?" — does the innovation strengthen fundamental human capabilities (creativity) or automate them away? **"What is the myth we are replacing?"** — does it replace a limiting myth such as "endless growth" with a regenerative narrative?

## Mental Models

- **Tensions are the insight.** The value of a systemic empathy map is in the places where one actor's gain is another's pain.
- **Play the critic deliberately.** The scanner "challenges the team to think like a critic and rigorously explore what could go wrong."
- **A story is a prototype** for anything you cannot mock up — a service, a policy, a behavior change.
- **Non-human stakeholders get profiles too,** with their own pains and gains.

## Anti-patterns

- Ranking features by revenue potential inside a "for Humanity" project.
- Treating the planet as a constraint line item rather than a profiled stakeholder.
- Stopping the consequence scan at first order, where the harms still look manageable.
- Oversimplifying the wicked problem into a tidy statement in order to get to ideation faster.

## Worked Example — The Community Harvest Network (Value Proposition for Humanity Canvas, pages 156–159)

**Idea:** "A service that provides access to shared community garden plots, tools, and education for urban residents."

### The System Profile

| Band | Pains (systemic harms) | Gains (regenerative outcomes) |
|---|---|---|
| **Planetary** — the urban ecosystem | High carbon footprint from food transported long distances; loss of biodiversity due to concrete and lack of green space; stormwater runoff overwhelming sewer systems; organic food waste going to landfills | Increased local biodiversity; reduced food miles; improved soil health; creation of local, closed-loop material flows |
| **People** — urban families in low-income neighborhoods. *Jobs-to-be-done:* access affordable, fresh, healthy food; find safe, positive activities for their children; connect with neighbors and feel part of a community | High cost of fresh produce at supermarkets ("food deserts"); anxiety about food security and where food comes from; lack of green space and connection to nature; feeling of social isolation in a dense urban environment | Desire for their children to have a healthy start; desire to learn new, practical skills; hope for a stronger, safer neighborhood |
| **Society** — the urban community | Erosion of community trust; generational disconnect and loss of practical knowledge such as growing food; lack of neighborhood resilience to supply-chain disruptions | Stronger social ties and increased trust between neighbors; intergenerational knowledge sharing; greater local food sovereignty and resilience; increased sense of collective ownership and pride in the neighborhood |

### The Value for Humanity Map

**The solution: "The Community Harvest Network"** — a subscription-based service providing garden plots, tools, seeds and educational workshops, managed through a simple mobile app that also facilitates community interaction.

*Value creators — for the planet:*
- The gardens act as "carbon sinks" and biodiversity hubs in urban areas.
- On-site composting bins for all organic waste, "creating a closed-loop system."
- Seed kits prioritize native and pollinator-friendly plants.

*Value creators — for the people (urban family):*
- Ultra-local, affordable access to fresh, organic produce.
- Workshops on gardening and cooking, "building skills and confidence."
- App features a community forum and event scheduler to foster connection.

*Value creators — for society:*
- Gardens designed as safe, accessible community hubs, hosting harvest festivals and potlucks.
- Partnerships with local schools and senior centers for intergenerational gardening programs.
- "By localizing a portion of the food supply, we build tangible neighborhood resilience."

### The same solution through the Ethical Consequences Scanner
**Consequence path 1 — The "Gentrification" Effect**
- *First order:* "The community gardens are successful and make the neighborhood more desirable and aesthetically pleasing."
- *Second order:* "This success attracts wealthier residents and developers, causing local property values and rents to rise."
- *Third order:* "The original low-income residents for whom the project was intended are eventually priced out and forced to leave their own neighborhood. The solution inadvertently displaces the very people it was meant to help."

This is the pattern the toolbox exists to catch: a solution that succeeds on its own metrics while destroying its purpose two orders out.

## Key Takeaways

1. Six tools, split evenly across problem and solution space; each has a named predecessor tool it evolves.
2. The HMW formula (*verb + action + for + context + so that + benefit*) is the book's standard framing sentence.
3. Give the planet and society their own pains-and-gains profiles, then re-rank by systemic impact.
4. Always run the consequence scan to third order and finish with the Ethical Litmus Test.
5. When you cannot mock it up, tell it as Situation → Intervention → Resolution.
