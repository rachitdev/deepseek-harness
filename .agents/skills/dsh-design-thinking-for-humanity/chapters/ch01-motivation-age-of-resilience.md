# Chapter 1 — Preface & Motivation: The Age of Resilience

*Pages 10–28: "A Conversation on Systemic Evolution" and "Motivation for This Book."*

## Core Idea

Humanity holds exponential technological power (AI, synthetic biology, quantum computing) while its wisdom, methods, and institutions remain linear. Lewrick calls this gap "the most significant design failure of our era." Classic human-centered design thinking made this worse: it trained a generation of innovators **to scale** but not **to pause and assess**. The moment a solution scales exponentially, "its impact often outgrows its original ethical container."

The strategic mandate: evolve design thinking **from isolated optimization to systemic regeneration**. The book is positioned not as a philosophy but as "the essential upgrade" — a disciplined process for mediating the velocity of the exponential curve with deep wisdom. Lewrick names the era the framework is meant to open: **The Age of Resilience**, "the end of purely industrial extraction."

## Frameworks Introduced

**The five-lens integration (announced here, detailed in Ch 3).** Futures thinking, design thinking, ecological thinking, systems thinking, and the responsible application of exponential technologies. "Used individually, each lens is valuable. Used together, they are transformative."

**Spotlight → Floodlight.** Standard human-centered design "places the user/customer in a spotlight; the environment remains in the dark." Design Thinking for Humanity converts the ecosystem "from a passive backdrop into a critical stakeholder." The customer is no longer "the person paying or clicking" but "the complex web of relationships between people, nature, and technology."

**The three critical shifts** a practitioner must master:
1. **Deepen the human–nature relationship** — stop treating nature as a resource to extract; recognize human well-being as inseparable from planetary health.
2. **Strengthen your futures imagination** — move beyond prediction; chart bold pathways to unprecedented possibilities.
3. **Integrate ecological thinking** — make ecological integrity a *core constraint* of the innovation process, not a review gate.

**Convergence of disciplines / Fifth Industrial Revolution.** Convergence, not a single method: AI as partner (modelling ecosystems, drug discovery, bio-fuel optimization), systemic synergy (data, resources and value flowing between stakeholders, AI, and environment), and the result — "localized, community-driven initiatives that are globally connected and technologically empowered."

## Key Concepts

- **Finite game vs infinite game** — the book contrasts playing to win a bounded round with playing to keep the system in play; the infinite-game framing recurs in systems and transition tools.
- **Wicked problems** — interconnected, contested, no stopping rule; the class of problem the five lenses exist to attack.
- **Linear empathy** — design thinking's empathy "is often linear and isolated"; it optimizes for the immediate user and overlooks systemic consequences amplified at scale.
- **Win-win-win** — the target state: society, environment, and economy thriving together "rather than at each other's expense."
- **Bridging the gap** — "A deep chasm separates us from the regenerative future we desire. We cannot simply leap across; we must construct a bridge strong enough to carry all of humanity."

## Mental Models

- **The question is not what we can build.** "The fundamental question defining our time is not *what* we can build, but *why* and *how* we choose to build it."
- **The challenge is methodological, not technological.** "We do not need better code. We need a better framework for deciding where and why that code is deployed."
- **Design for fragility is what efficiency bought us.** "For decades, we designed for efficiency, and in doing so, we made our world fragile."
- **Prediction is a trap (Liedtka).** "Working from a single-point prediction is just about the most dangerous thing you can do." Futures work is about agency, not forecast accuracy.
- **Small experiments are risk *reduction*, not risk.** Reframe risk for executives: "The biggest risk today is ignoring the reality around us, or doing nothing, or doing the wrong thing efficiently."

## Anti-patterns

- **Optimizing short-term profit while calling it strategy** — designing for the next quarter cannot produce a regenerative future.
- **The human-centered spotlight** — intense focus on an individual's immediate needs that "blinds us to the larger context," ignoring systemic impact, environmental cost, and long-term consequences.
- **Treating policy and governance as fixed constraints** rather than as prototypes that can be tested.
- **Counting only what is easy to count** — resilience, trust, and ecological health are hard to count and therefore get dropped from dashboards.
- **Doing the wrong thing efficiently** — the most expensive failure mode in a risk-averse organization.

## Worked Example — The Liedtka Dialogue (five questions, pages 10–13)

The book opens not with a foreword but with a five-question dialogue with **Jeanne Liedtka**, professor emeritus, Darden School of Business, author of *Design Thinking for the Greater Good*. Reproduced as the book's own argument-in-miniature:

| # | Question posed by Lewrick | Liedtka's answer (condensed to her own terms) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **From user-centric to life-centric** — where does the classic user-centric approach fail at planetary scale? | "The 'human-centered' label can become a spotlight that blinds us to the larger context." Move from solving for the user alone to "solving for the user within an entire ecosystem," including the environment and future generations. |
| 2 | **Designing beyond the product** — how should leaders re-model policy and governance? | "See policy or strategy as a prototype rather than a rigid plan." "We would like to be able to test governance structures just like we test a new toaster." The design target shifts to "platforms for participation" and "ecosystems of value." |
| 3 | **Agency over prediction** — why is standard prediction dangerous? | "In a world of change, prediction is a trap." Leaders love "the illusion of certainty." The future "is not already out there waiting to happen — it's something we invent, and our agency matters." Without a clearly articulated preferable future, "organizations are just drifting." |
| 4 | **Experimentation as risk reduction** — how to convince risk-averse executives? | Large organizations "are designed for predictability and control." Reframe what risk means: "small, low-fidelity experiments are not risky; they are risk reduction strategies." |
| 5 | **Measuring systemic health** — what metric tracks regenerative initiatives? | Combine **"hard data" with "thick data"** — the stories that give numbers meaning. "Instead of just quarterly output, we should measure the **learning velocity** and the **adaptability** of the system." |

Her closing charge to the reader: approach systemic complexity "with profound humility, unwavering curiosity, and a relentless bias toward action."

## Key Takeaways

1. The gap between exponential potential and linear wisdom is a *design* problem, and the response is a method, not a manifesto.
2. Human-centered design is retained, not discarded — individual needs "are not ignored, they are expanded" to community, environment, and future generations.
3. Ecological integrity is a constraint on innovation, not a reporting obligation after the fact.
4. Success is redefined from market acceptance to **systemic resilience**.
5. Measure learning velocity and adaptability; pair hard data with thick data.
