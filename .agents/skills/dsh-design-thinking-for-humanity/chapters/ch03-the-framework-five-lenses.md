# Chapter 3 — The Framework: Five Lenses, Four System Responses, Seven Principles

*Pages 35–49. The Design Thinking for Humanity Wheel, the exponentializing system, the synchronized horizons, leadership roles, and the Resilience Blueprint canvas.*

## Core Idea

Design Thinking for Humanity is "a disciplined operating system for the 21st century," built from five deliberately chosen lenses on the **Design Thinking for Humanity Wheel**. The framework's ambition is stated precisely: not robustness, not even resilience, but an **exponentializing** system — one that "does not just survive a shock but grows stronger because of it," using volatility as fuel.

## Frameworks Introduced

### 1. The five lenses (the book's own definitions, page 39)

| Lens | Definition as given |
|---|---|
| **Futures Thinking** | Anticipating and exploring multiple plausible futures to inform present-day decisions and design — identifying trends, envisioning alternative scenarios, proactively shaping desired outcomes to build long-term resilience and adaptability. |
| **Design Thinking** | Empathizing with people to deeply understand needs, challenges and desires; the foundation for creative, human-centered solutions that deliver real value. |
| **Ecological Thinking** | Recognizing the interdependence of humans and nature; designing solutions promoting harmony and balance between social and ecological systems, minimizing negative impacts and creating regenerative outcomes. |
| **Systems Thinking** | Analyzing complex problems within their broader context to identify underlying causes and leverage interconnected relationships; a long-term perspective that addresses root causes. |
| **Exponential Thinking** | A nonlinear mindset leveraging accelerating technologies for rapid, global, restorative impact — audacious breakthroughs and 10x improvements "while maintaining alignment with deep ethical intent and collective human values." |

### 2. The four system responses to shock

| Response | Outcome label | Behavior |
|---|---|---|
| **Decay** | Lose–Lose | "The system collapses." Cannot withstand the shock; "the default path of fragile, extractive" systems. |
| **Robust** | Win–Lose / status quo | "The system resists." Absorbs shock without breaking but stays static; protects the status quo at the expense of necessary change. |
| **Resilient** | Win–Neutral / bounce back | "The system recovers." Returns to its original state — "it essentially restores the previous vulnerability." |
| **Exponentializing** | Win–Win–Win / bounce forward | "The system evolves." Uses disruption as a catalyst; emerges "stronger, more equitable, and more vibrant than before." |

Design criterion: never accept "resilient" as the design target if the pre-shock state was itself vulnerable.

### 3. Synchronized strategic horizons

The five disciplines form a cycle across time horizons rather than a menu:
- **Design thinking** → immediate action, **1–5 years**, the *probable* future.
- **Exponential thinking / systems thinking** → mid-term scaling, **10 years**, *possible* and *plausible* futures.
- **Futures thinking** → **20+ years**, the *preferable* future.
- **Ecological thinking** sits on the **outer circle** as "the non-negotiable context … not merely a separate lens but the foundation through which all others must be viewed," defining the planetary boundaries every solution must operate inside, at every horizon.

"We do not just solve today's problems; we reverse-engineer our strategy from the future."

### 4. The extended Double Diamond

"The well-known double diamond is extended with a crucial preliminary phase guided by futures thinking. This initial **future space** allows organizations to explore scenarios and define a clear vision before framing a specific problem." → *Future space → Problem space → Solution space.*

### 5. The seven Design Thinking for Humanity principles

1. **Embrace design's profound responsibility** — "do no harm" to people or planet; "the choice to participate carries the responsibility to be informed."
2. **Cultivate awareness and transparency** — champion open information; make consequences of design choices visible to users, customers and teams.
3. **Design for the ecosystem** — look beyond the product; proactively consider unintended consequences.
4. **Strive for elegant reduction** — "Resist the urge to solve problems by adding layers of complexity … complex systems increase the risk of unintended outcomes." Distinguish necessary complexity from unnecessary clutter; "true simplicity serves, not oversimplifies, reality."
5. **Center humanity in a planetary context** — diverse and inclusive teams; equitable solutions *within ecological limits*.
6. **Embody radical optimism** — acknowledge the challenges, "embrace the discomfort of change," see opportunity where others see obstacles.
7. **Leverage exponential technologies for scalable impact** — AI, biotech, renewables, deployed at the speed and reach the problems require.

### 6. Leadership roles

- **Resilience Leader** (enterprise/startup): sees beyond the present; builds robust systems and adaptable teams; fosters decentralized decision-making; leverages AI for innovation and efficiency; balances short-term action with long-term vision. Shift "from command-and-control to adaptability and holistic resilience," from prediction to "early sensing and scenario planning."
- **Systems Steward** (government/policy): guides policy with foresight and scenario planning; builds lean, multi-level governance networks; balances global interdependencies with systemic strength; leverages AI for adaptive policy; prioritizes planetary and human well-being "for generations."
- **Catalytic Entrepreneur** and **the Resilient Commonwealth** — citizens who "actively transition from being passive beneficiaries to engaged co-creators," driving change bottom-up.

### 7. Narrative design and the 1.5 °C trap

Measurement and narrative are treated as design objects. "Treat the story as a prototype to be tested and refined." And: **the 1.5 °C trap** — "To a scientist, this number represents a catastrophic tipping point. To the general public, it often sounds like a slightly warmer afternoon. This is the failure of data without narrative." Remedy: "Stop communicating in abstract degrees. Start communicating in tangible consequences — flooded cities, dried-up farms, and the loss of the places we love."

## Mental Models

- **If we do not measure these values, we cannot design for them** — hence Regenerative Ecological Metrics (Ch 9, p. 192).
- **Transdisciplinary or nothing** — "No single discipline can address these multifaceted tasks alone; a true fusion of expertise is essential," and the first job of such a team is to "translate specific jargon into shared meaning to prevent misalignment."
- **Human imagination × AI generative capability** — "Exploring the tensions between human intuition and machine recall creates the cognitive conditions necessary for novel, high-impact solutions."
- **Escape the gravity of the present** — anchor in a 10–20 year horizon to reach root causes.

## Anti-patterns

- **Mistaking robustness for resilience** — resisting change and calling it strength.
- **Designing for recovery to a vulnerable baseline** — "returning to that previous state is not a success."
- **Green labelling** — "merely slapping 'green' or 'responsible' labels on a product is no longer enough."
- **Data without narrative** — the 1.5 °C trap.
- **Using single tools instead of navigating the cycle** across horizons.
- **Adding complexity as a solution strategy** (violates elegant reduction).

## Worked Example — The Resilience Blueprint Canvas (pages 48–49, detailed pp. 296–297)

The canvas that unifies the whole book; the boxes and their literal prompt questions:

| Field | Prompt |
|---|---|
| **Massive Transformative Purpose** | "What is the single, audacious, and inspiring purpose that drives this entire endeavor?" |
| **Preferable Future** | "What does the world look like in 20+ years if this project is a profound success?" |
| **Systemic Challenge** | "What is the core 'wicked problem' this initiative is designed to address?" |
| **Value Proposition — Humans** | "How does this solution create tangible, meaningful value for the individuals and communities it serves? What deep human needs does it fulfill?" |
| **Value Proposition — Planet** | "How does this solution actively regenerate the ecosystems it touches? What is its net-positive ecological impact?" |
| **Value Proposition — Society** | "How does this solution build social equity, community resilience, and collective well-being?" |
| **Key Interventions and Solutions** | "What are the core products, services, policies, or platforms that will deliver the 'win-win-win' value?" |
| **Ecosystem of Partners** | "Who are the critical actors in our collaborative ecosystem required to make this solution a reality?" |
| **Responsible Path to Scale** | "How will this solution leverage exponential trends to create impact at a scale that matches the size of the problem, and what are the key ethical guardrails that will ensure this is done responsibly?" |
| **Costs** | "What are the financial, resource, and potential negative externality costs of implementing the solution?" |
| **Objectives and Key Metrics** | "How will we measure our progress toward creating value for people, the planet, and society?" |
| **Benefits** | "What are the quantifiable benefits for all stakeholders involved?" |

Note the canvas geometry: the three-part value proposition (humans / planet / society) sits in the **central column** — the win-win-win is structurally the middle of the canvas, not an appendix. A filled-in version appears in Chapter 15.

## Key Takeaways

1. Five lenses, one wheel; ecological thinking is the outer boundary condition, not a peer lens.
2. Target the **exponentializing** response, not merely the resilient one.
3. Prepend a **future space** to the Double Diamond.
4. Match lens to horizon: design (1–5y), exponential/systems (10y), futures (20+y).
5. Design the narrative and the metrics with the same rigor as the product.
