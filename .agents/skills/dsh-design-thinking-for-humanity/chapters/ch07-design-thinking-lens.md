# Chapter 7 — The Design Thinking Lens: Deep Empathy, Radical Collaboration, Storytelling

*Pages 120–141 plus Case Study Part 2 (pp. 137–141): the Problem Framing Funnel, wicked problems, the Design Thinking Mandate, the Narrative Design Canvas, and Javier Rodriguez.*

## Core Idea

"Design thinking is the discipline of empathetic intent." This chapter keeps the classic process — Understand, Observe, Define Point of View, Ideate, Prototype, Test — and **elevates the scope of every phase** to be life-centered. The single most important factor for success is unchanged: "A profound and disciplined exploration of the problem space … Without this rigorous up-front work, any team risks creating an elegant solution to the wrong problem."

The most critical juncture is named precisely: **the Define Point of View stage**, where the two diamonds meet.

## Frameworks Introduced

### The Problem Framing Funnel
The process does not start from a blank slate. **Insights from futures thinking** (scenarios, trends) → **framing activities** (Systemic Empathy Map, Wicked Problem Framing) → **iterated HMW questions focusing on system barriers**. "The HMW questions framed here are not aimed at solving a simple user need. They are designed for intervening in a complex system."

Two of the book's own iterated HMW examples (urban food deserts):
- *Focus on land tenure & policy:* "How might we partner with city officials and land trust organizations to establish permanent, policy-protected sites for community food production **so that** residents in urban 'food deserts' can build long-term economic assets that resist gentrification?"
- *Focus on financial viability & distribution:* how might we design a self-sustaining economic model for local food production that generates collectively managed value, thereby overcoming the fragility of short-term interventions and securing financial resilience.

### Classical Design Thinking vs the Design Thinking for Humanity Mandate

| Feature | Classical mandate | Design Thinking for Humanity mandate |
|---|---|---|
| **Primary focus** | Solving for the immediate needs and pain points of a specific user/customer | Prioritizing the vitality of the whole ecological and social ecosystem |
| **Core objective** | The "sweet spot" between user needs, market viability, technical feasibility | Designing systems that actively regenerate environments and withstand future disruption |
| **Target scale** | The interaction between a person and a product, service or experience | Mapping the interdependencies of an interconnected system |
| **Role of narrative** | Stories that map individual experiences and drive product adoption | Narratives that mobilize and catalyze collective, systemic action |
| **Time horizon** | Iterative improvements and near-term market success | Anticipating future scenarios and the well-being of future generations |
| **Definition of success** | Individual satisfaction, efficiency, commercial growth | Ability to solve "wicked problems" and promote planetary health |

### The four properties of wicked problems (the book's labels)
- **The symptom, not the disease** — "Every wicked problem is a symptom of deeper issues; solving one part risks unforeseen consequences elsewhere."
- **No stopping rule** — "no definitive formula, endpoint, or stopping rule."
- **The ambiguity mandate** — they require embracing ambiguity and navigating complexity with a human-centered approach.
- **Integrated complexity** — they require integrating systems thinking to understand causes and effects.

Wicked problems are best understood as **complex socio-technical systems**: "To address the issue of waste, a focus on recycling alone is insufficient. The design of products built for obsolescence and the economic incentives that reward disposable consumption must also be examined."

### Radical Collaboration — I Can Do / AI Can Do / We Can Do
- **I can do (the human role):** transdisciplinary teams across science, policy, business and design; the individual's contribution is "to ensure the problem is seen from every angle, identifying the true root causes, not just surface-level symptoms."
- **AI can do (integrating the co-creator):** hybrid teams; treat generative and specialized AI agents "not merely as passive tools for executing commands but as active and integrated team members," leveraging machine intelligence for deep context analysis.
- **We can do (the collaborative outcome):** trust and resilience; "engaging citizens as co-creators in the design process."

*Four moves:* 1. Shift mindset — view AI as an active co-creator, not a passive tool. 2. Cultivate trust — work openly and transparently to build the social fabric for collective action. 3. Ensure ownership — move from top-down direction to engaging citizens and users as co-creators. 4. Embrace fusion — integrate transdisciplinary expertise to achieve collective intelligence.

*Six collaboration practices:* (1) Frame the problem in a future context; (2) Build a shared systemic view — "a collectively built systems map where the needs of all stakeholders, including the planet, are visible"; (3) Default to deliberate trust — "in the face of uncertainty, teams must make a conscious and deliberate choice to trust first"; (4) Balance deep work with collective synthesis — protect time for both modes; (5) Foster psychological safety for wicked problems — "safe to be vulnerable, to respectfully challenge ideas (not people), and to learn from failure"; (6) Cultivate hybrid intelligence — blend human life experience and thinking styles with specialized AI agents given context and a clear mission.

### Shoshin — the beginner's mind
"The Japanese concept of a 'beginner's mind'": approach every situation with an open, accepting and profoundly curious perspective. "When the hierarchy of expertise is flattened, the team is empowered to see the problem with fresh eyes." It enables exploring all possible futures, uncovering new concepts for regeneration, breaking existing rules and behaviors, and leveraging exponential technologies for true innovation rather than mere efficiency. "In the face of wicked problems, we are all beginners."

### The Narrative Design Canvas (page 135) — three columns, nine prompts
**Deconstruct the present (the "as-is" story)**
- *The old narrative:* "What is the dominant and limiting story that currently perpetuates the unsustainable status quo?"
- *The core audience:* "Who believes this old story, and what are their underlying values, hopes, and fears?"
- *The hidden payoff:* "What benefit or sense of security does the old narrative provide that makes it so difficult to change?"

**Design the future (the "to-be" story)**
- *The new narrative:* "What is the compelling and regenerative new story that offers a tangible vision of a preferable future?"
- *The core message:* "If the audience could remember only one thing from our new story, what is the single, undeniable truth we want them to carry with them?"
- *The human connection:* "How does our new narrative connect with the deepest values of our audience and make the future feel not just possible but desirable?"

**Inspire action (the call)** — the shift from apathy to participation, the change we want the audience to make, and how to make it immediately actionable and sustain commitment.

Worked shift shown on the canvas: *"Overconsumption is part of the business model"* → *"Design for durability, not depletion."* And: *"Profit maximization comes first"* → *"Thriving planet, prosperous business."*

### Levels of Objectives and Change (the maturity ladder)

| Level | Stated position |
|---|---|
| **Compliance-driven** | "Our primary goal is to meet the bare minimum requirements, ensuring we stay out of legal trouble and maintain our license to operate." |
| **Reactive changes** | "We implement changes only when external pressures demand it, focusing on efficiency and managing our public image to stay relevant." |
| **Sustainable design** | "We strategically embed sustainability into our core operations and offerings, believing it is essential for long-term competitive advantage and shared value creation." |
| **Regenerative** | The ultimate ambition: actively enhance ecological and social systems, net-positive impact. |

### Integrating systemic resilience — the reordered constraint set
Classic design thinking seeks the sweet spot of **desirability, viability, feasibility**. Here, "any design innovation must first prove its positive or neutral impact on people and the planet before considering profit and production. The ecological boundary acts as a **non-negotiable constraint**." Innovation counts only when it is "desirable, viable, feasible, and built without violating the mandates of social, ethical, and ecological well-being."

**Policy as a core design input.** Three validation questions before scaling a prototype to societal level:
1. "Will the new mechanism be recognized and honored by governing bodies?"
2. "Will existing financial institutions integrate and honor it?"
3. "Is the foundational policy stable enough to avoid immediate political capture or reversal by opposing interests?"
"Failing to integrate early policy analysis creates the main failure points for social innovations."

## Mental Models

- **Empathy expands in scope, not just depth** — communities, future generations, and the planet as stakeholders; observation extends to "systemic patterns, resource flows, and ecological impacts."
- **Story before statistics.** The book's own before/after: *Before* — "The new community garden initiative resulted in a 115% increase in local vegetable consumption and a reported increase in neighborhood interaction over the last 12 months." *After* — "Meet Maria. A single mother who worried about affording fresh vegetables. Today, she and her son harvest their own food at the community garden, a place where he now plays with neighbors he knows by name. The garden did not just bring food; it brought the community together."
- **Narrative arc:** Situation → Complication → Resolution. Five essential elements: clear premise, authenticity and empathy, meaningful outcome and call to action, coherence and clarity, emotional resonance and connection.
- **Institutional change, not user adoption, gates systemic change.** "Large-scale change depends on institutional change, not just widespread user adoption."

## Anti-patterns

- **An elegant solution to the wrong problem** — the cost of shortening the first diamond.
- **HMW aimed at a symptom** rather than a system barrier.
- **Green-labelling the old narrative** instead of deconstructing its hidden payoff.
- **Validating only desirability** — skipping ethics, resilience and policy testing (Ethics Compass, Wind Tunneling).
- **Treating AI as a passive tool** and thereby losing the co-creator role.
- **Recycling-only thinking** — attacking a symptom while the obsolescence design and incentive structure remain untouched.

## Worked Example — Case Study Part 2: Javier Rodriguez and the Carbon Harvest Platform

**Future Persona.**
- **Name:** Javier Rodriguez. **Demographics:** 55, male, runs a multigenerational family farm in a region facing increasing water scarcity. **Role:** a respected, pragmatic farmer and community leader.
- **Bio:** skilled farmer, has managed the family's land for more than 30 years; deeply committed to his community and the long-term health of his land; under immense financial pressure from volatile commodity prices and rising input costs (fertilizer, water); "skeptical of complex new technologies but open to practical solutions that offer clear, tangible benefits."
- **Pains (fears & frustrations):** trapped in a system "that is both economically and ecologically unsustainable"; constant financial pressure from volatile markets, loss of autonomy, deep anxiety about the future health of his land; greatest fear — "this downward spiral will make it impossible to pass a viable and healthy farm on to his children, breaking a multigenerational legacy."
- **Gains (hopes & desires):** resilient prosperity in which he is "rewarded for his stewardship of the land" with stable, diversified income; agriculture "more in tune with nature"; to be a respected leader pioneering a new way of farming.
- **Jobs-to-be-done:** (1) "Ensure the long-term viability and profitability of my family farm" — resilience against economic *and* environmental shocks. (2) "Pass on a healthy, productive farm to my children" — a legacy of healthy land, against an economic reality that "forces him into short-term degenerative practices."

**Wicked Problem Framing → HMW.**
> "How might we (re)design a simple and trustworthy system for farmers like Javier to directly monetize the ecological value they create through regenerative practices, so that they can increase their economic resilience and become the heroes of a regenerative future?"

**Ideation — Creative Mash-Up.**
- *Human need:* direct, fair compensation — reward farmers like Javier directly for their stewardship.
- *Future trend:* verifiable Earth-observation data — "make the invisible visible, and therefore quantifiable."
- *The idea:* **The Carbon Harvest Platform** — a farmer-centric digital platform using real-time data from soil sensors and satellites to issue credits monetizing sequestered carbon, improved soil health and increased biodiversity. It "transforms Javier from a vulnerable commodity producer into a steward of planetary health," with an income stream not dependent on regulation or grants.

**Ethical Consequences Scanner** (the platform placed at the centre):
- *First-order negative consequence:* "The platform could create a new 'digital divide,' benefiting large, tech-savvy agribusinesses while leaving smaller, less-connected farmers like Javier behind."
- *Second-order negative consequence:* "further consolidation of farmland and the marginalization of the very communities the platform was intended to help."
- *New design constraint (the output that matters):* "The platform must be co-designed with farmers like Javier and include a 'high-touch' support network to ensure it is accessible and equitable for farms of all sizes" — specifically a network of on-the-ground agronomists and community leaders who co-design the platform and provide hands-on training, not a help desk.

**Value Proposition for Humanity Canvas.**
- *Value for people (Javier & farmers):* a new revenue stream based on stewardship; data to improve farm resilience and profitability.
- *Value for the planet (the soil):* a direct financial mechanism to rapidly accelerate the adoption of regenerative practices — healthier soil, sequestered carbon, restored biodiversity.
- *Value for society (global community):* increased resilience of the global food system; a transparent way for corporations and individuals to invest in verified regeneration.

**Future Storytelling & Narrative Prototyping — "a day in the life."**
> Javier starts his day by opening the Carbon Harvest app instead of checking commodity prices. The dashboard shows that the cover crops he planted increased his soil's water retention by 5%, automatically issuing new "water resilience credits." Later, a video call with a young tech entrepreneur in a distant city connects him to a team whose company is buying his credits to meet corporate climate goals. He finally sees a clear path to passing a thriving, healthy farm to his children, embracing his new role as a respected steward of a vital planetary resource.

**Validating systemic robustness.** Three questions applied to the credit system itself, not the personal narrative: Will governments recognize the credits? Will banks and financial bodies honor them? Is the foundational policy stable enough to resist capture or reversal?

## Key Takeaways

1. Keep the six-step process; expand the scope of every phase to life-centered.
2. Frame HMW questions at system barriers (land tenure, policy, financial viability), not at user friction.
3. Run an Ethical Consequences Scan *before* finalizing a concept; its output is a new design constraint.
4. Deconstruct the old narrative's **hidden payoff** before offering a new one.
5. Validate the institutional system — governance, finance, policy stability — not only desirability.
