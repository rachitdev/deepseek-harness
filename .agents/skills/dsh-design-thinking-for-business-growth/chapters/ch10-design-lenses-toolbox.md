# Chapter 10 — The Design Lenses Toolbox

*Pages 172–185 ("Design Lenses Toolbox": Project Phases and Planning Horizons; Typical Trajectories of Ecosystem Initiatives; Simplified "Problem 2 Growth and Scale" Framework)*

## Core Idea

This is the hinge of the book: the four design lenses laid out as a guided but iterative process, with planning horizons, the fits they deliver, and the assumption-testing discipline that runs through all of them. The framing caveat is stated up front — "the presented procedure via the four lenses, as a guided and structured process, may be perceived as quite a rigid approach. But anybody who has already used the combination of design thinking and systems thinking knows that the respective step is more to give orientation to the teams so they know where they currently are in the process and in terms of solution maturity. **In general, we work iteratively in all design lenses.**"

The lenses exist for a social reason as much as a methodological one: they "help in the co-creation process to establish a common language with other actors and team members, use similar methods and tools, and to reflect upon the joint approach."

## Frameworks Introduced

**The four design lenses:**

| Lens | Purpose | Output |
|---|---|---|
| **#1 Design thinking** | Gives center stage to customers and their needs; brings together different experiences, opinions, and perspectives on a problem statement and creates first prototypes | Final prototype; MVP requirements |
| **#2 Lean start-up** | Helps implement first solutions and focus on the most necessary functions and experiences; first market experiences improve the MVP iteratively; saves time, work, and money; the MVP becomes the basis for initial talks or co-creation with other potential actors | Validated MVP |
| **#3 Ecosystem design** | Starting point is an initial hypothesis of a value proposition already tested through design thinking and lean start-up; focus stays on the customer while the selection of the right actors becomes important, taking their needs into account. "The configuration of the system with its actors, capabilities, and customer access is critical to success" | MVE |
| **#4 Scale** | Requires sophisticated and targeted governance plus meaningful elements to expand the value proposition incrementally; optimize customer interaction points and increase frequency; acquired data becomes another asset; new versions of business models evolve (e.g. monetization of B2B services or data) | Exponential growth |

**Simplified "Problem 2 Growth and Scale" framework** — the five fits and their artifacts:

```
Start → Customer needs → Problem/solution fit → Product/market fit → System/actors fit → Ecosystem/coevolution fit
                          (Vision/final prototype)     (MVP)              (MVE)            (Black ocean)
```

**Solution maturity criteria per lens.** Feasibility runs through all four, in four escalating forms; the other criteria change:

| Lens | Criteria | Feasibility form |
|---|---|---|
| #1 Design thinking | **Desirability** (focusing on customers and their needs) + **Viability** (creation of an innovative and sustainable business model) | *Feasibility 1* — use of contemporary, effective, and applicable enabler technologies |
| #2 Lean start-up | **Desirability** (validation of customer needs on the basis of individual functions and experience) + **Viability** (validation of the business model and value of the offering) | *Feasibility 2* — testing and validation of selected technology components |
| #3 Ecosystem design | **Adaptability** (focusing on the needs of the actors and their ability to create a value proposition together) + **Value enhanceability** (shaping sustainable value streams and benefits for all actors) | *Feasibility 3* — specification of technology components and interfaces for the realization of an ecosystem approach |
| #4 Scale | **Captivateability** (focus on increasing the frequency of customer interaction, share of wallet, and retention/bond to the system) + **Rhythmicability** (exploitation of network effects and scale effects) | *Feasibility 4* — building out, professionalization, and leverage of the technology components |

The book's summary: "While feasibility infuses all design lenses, desirability + viability expand into value enhanceability + adaptability in the business ecosystem design lens. For exponential growth, rhythmicability + captivateability are key. Dynamic systems then come full circle, and we're back at design thinking because customer needs are decisive for the expansion of the value proposition." Note also: "Technical feasibility normally does not pose a problem any longer today. Obstacles can be statutory provisions or outmoded core applications that are not compatible with new technologies."

**Critical vs not so critical assumptions.** A 2×2 matrix: *critical ↔ not critical* against *known ↔ unknown*. Place individual assumptions on the matrix, discuss them on the team, and **examine those in the top right quadrant** (critical + unknown). The legend classifies assumptions by criterion — desirability (e.g. customer wishes and needs), viability (e.g. costs of physical and digital touch points), feasibility (e.g. standardization in voice interaction through to special algorithms), plus adaptability, value enhanceability, rhythmicability and captivateability as the later lenses add them.

Example critical questions given: *When was the last time we "walked in our customer's shoes"? What triggers a real WOW! effect in customers? When and where do customers use an offer?*

## Key Concepts

- **No universal project plan exists.** Every growth initiative is different and initiators have different capabilities. At best, research comes first — outlining customer needs, then examining in-house capabilities and skills to reach greater certainty and awareness of potential topic areas, and learning about growth and ecosystem initiatives already in the market.
- **Assumptions evolve across lenses.** In each lens, different assumptions about customers, actors, and individual value streams are questioned and initial findings validated. Over time the view changes; new assumptions are added, particularly about which actors might join and how they benefit. The same applies to scaling: assumptions must be tested before making an operational contribution. Then "the loop starts again with the expansion of the value proposition, where again the assumptions are checked within the framework of design thinking."
- **Why not start with the ecosystem?** "From practice we know that in most cases it is necessary to go through the previous design lenses again before the design of the actual ecosystem can be tackled. When the preceding steps have been left out, companies often tend to see customers as actors in the system and not as the absolute center of all activities/value propositions." The additional advantage: ecosystem considerations then won't focus too much on one's own company, more on explore than on exploit.
- **The permitted shortcut.** "Design thinking for business growth teams who already have successfully identified the customer needs and have carried out the required validations by means of an MVP **can immediately start with section #3 ecosystem design**."
- **Ecosystem design can send you backwards.** "Frequently, through intense exploration and dealing with the actors in the system, completely new ideas for products or services up to new value propositions evolve in business ecosystem workshops. This can even mean that the original solution is completely discarded and that the findings result in a new, shared value proposition."
- **Blueprints do not exist.** An initiator must do without a blueprint for certain topic areas or industries, "even though decision makers frequently express their wish for such blueprints." Even historical analysis is difficult — the BCG Henderson Institute's 2019 work on building business ecosystems is cited, with lack of structured data as the reason.
- **Success is not permanent.** "Even successfully initialized ecosystems are not successful forever if governance and leadership leave much to be desired. In addition, the underlying strategy must be constantly reassessed." During continuous development, new players are constantly added while others become irrelevant to creating the value proposition; initiators over time take financial stakes in other actors, or partners are taken over 100% or by majority share — usually driven by the desire to own customer interfaces or data points and use them long term.
- **Trajectories as subway lines.** Business ecosystems typically develop along one of several pathways and branches with regard to their ability to capture and retain market share; "in the figure shown as 'subway lines,' it is important to put in switches at the right points for growth and scaling." One named failure branch is **insufficient configuration**. (The figure itself is largely image-only in the source; the labels beyond this are not reliably recoverable.)

## Mental Models

- **Where you are, not what you must do next.** The lens tells the team its position in the process and its solution maturity — orientation, not sequence enforcement.
- **Transitions are fluid.** "It is important to point out that the transitions between design lenses are fluid, and a sharp delineation is not possible. Depending on the resolution level of prototypes, MVPs, and MVEs, steps can be accelerated or skipped."
- **Don't think from the company's point of view but from the customer's.** Stated as a full-page instruction in this section.
- **Most initiatives fail at implementation.** "From the identification of the customer needs all the way to a solution provided by several actors — most initiatives fail with implementation. Each of the transitions between the design lenses as well as the mindset and the management of the varied interests of the actors are critical to success. Many ecosystem initiatives lack the governance and skills needed for the fast scaling of a solution."

## Reference Tables

**Planning horizons of a business ecosystem initiative** — the book's own six-phase example table (p. 173):

| | Research | Design thinking | Lean start-up | Ecosystem design | Implement and run | Scale |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Duration** | ~6 weeks | ~6–10 weeks | ~10–12 weeks | ~12–16 weeks | >16 weeks | Open end |
| **Output** | Topic areas / customer needs; existing capabilities and skills/relationships | Final prototype; vision prototype; requirements for MVP | MVP; willingness to pay; validation of assumptions | MVE; value streams; business model; actors in the system | Realization plan; product portfolio; fast learning and adaptation cycles; scaling the IT | Expansion of the value proposition; innovation by actors |
| **Description** | Define features and framework conditions of the solution; definition of the design principles | Visualize the prototype and demonstrate the experiences and functions of the product or service | Release of the possible functional scope; focus on critical functions and experiences; measurement and iterations | Validation of the value proposition; definition of the actors and their roles; definition of value streams | Implementation of the ecosystem initiative at the level of culture, capabilities, IT, governance, leadership; laying down a road map | Needfinding for value proposition expansion; adjustment of the ecosystem strategy; reassessment of IT requirements; orchestration |
| **Team** | Internal team; strategy experts; multidisciplinary; cross-company; first potential partners/ecosystem actors | Business ecosystem/design team; users/test groups; co-creation with first potential partners/ecosystem actors | UX designers; software developers; potential partners/ecosystem actors; testing with customers and potential partners | Business ecosystem design team; co-creation with other actors | Orchestrator; implementation partners and suppliers; actors | Design thinking, ecosystem design, and growth hack teams; IT, software developers; partners, actors, and suppliers |
| **Artifact** | Ideas, suggestions, foresight | Vision prototype with critical functions and experiences | MVPs, MMF, backlog | Part of the value proposition | Realization of the value proposition | Expanded value proposition |

Additional timing notes from the surrounding prose: "In a design thinking sprint, solid results can be achieved in 6 weeks. Usually, it takes longer." "Depending on the complexity, MVPs are usually ready for testing in 4 to 10 weeks." "This design phase [ecosystem design] is one of the most demanding and lasts from 12 to 16 weeks or more."

**Begin with the customer — the five dimensions and their critical success actions** (p. 181):

| Dimension | Relevant approaches | Actions critical to success |
|---|---|---|
| **Customer needs** | Design thinking | Explore the needs; derive from it the tasks to be performed; use critical functions and experiences |
| **Prototypes (MVPs, MMFs, MVEs)** | Lean start-up; ecosystem design | Validate functions, experiences, etc.; iterative development of target systems; viewing the business models (multidimensional) |
| **Leadership** | Future ways of working; ecosystem leadership | Establishing a new mindset; management of a wide range of interests; permanent development of differentiating factors (value proposition) |
| **Scaling** | Scale; agile development | Use of the first-mover advantage for radical disruption; fast scaling; permanent and sustainable investment in ecosystem building |
| **Dynamic development** | Co-creation | Expansion of the ecosystem based on customer needs; increase commitment to the system; data for innovation and validation |

**What the section says to expect from each lens as its named goal:**

| Lens | Goal stated in the lens intro |
|---|---|
| #1 | Develop solutions that meet the needs of the customers; the final prototype and critical items constitute the basis for MVP specifications |
| #2 | Reflect on initial validations of the value proposition and added value for the customer on the basis of an MVP |
| #3 | Create an MVE that positions the ecosystem in its minimal manifestation efficiently on the market, with transparency about whom the values accrue to in a fair value exchange |
| #4 | Realize exponential growth with the right operational model and purposeful governance that uses all the levers to achieve the business model goals |

## Worked Example

**Sequencing a real initiative against the planning horizons table** — using the book's own figures and its rules for where budget and decisions land.

*Weeks 0–6, research.* Outline customer needs, examine in-house capabilities and skills, learn about growth and ecosystem initiatives already in the market. Team is internal plus strategy experts, multidisciplinary, cross-company, and may already include first potential partners. Define the **design principles** here (Ch 5's pyramid tool).

*Weeks 6–16, design thinking.* Several iterations and rounds of customer feedback to create meaningful vision prototypes and prototypes for function and experience. The book notes the friction point: "there are various decision-making bodies that affect the initiatives in the conventional settings with the respective gates at rigid deadlines. **Ideally, decision makers release the respective budgets for the next steps immediately after realization**, e.g. after the presentation of the final prototype in design thinking."

*Weeks 16–28, lean start-up.* "For the lean start-up phase, it is advisable to be aware of the assumptions that need to be checked since without market validation, assumptions will remain just that. Typical examples of assumptions exist with regard to the willingness to pay on the part of customers, which needs to be checked." Depending on complexity, MVPs are ready for testing in 4–10 weeks. The MVP then "becomes a **flagship product to persuade potential actors** of the value proposition and vision" — its second job, beyond validation.

*Weeks 28–44, ecosystem design.* The most demanding phase, 12–16 weeks or more. It "provides information on the value streams, the business model of the ecosystem, and on how individual actors benefit from participating." Output: the MVE — "the MVE shows how the system works in its smallest manifestation."

*Week 44 onward, implement and run.* ">16 weeks", requiring more or less time depending on complexity and dependencies. Culture, capabilities, IT, governance, leadership, plus a road map.

*Open end, scale.* "Some ecosystems reach the threshold of exponential growth faster than others. While some initiatives reach this threshold as early as after one year, others need several years to get to this milestone. Likewise, there are many ecosystem initiatives that fail to grow through network effects and meaningful expansions of the value proposition."

*The standing obligation across all of it.* "What is important is to adapt the systems constantly and exploit the innovative strength of the other actors to be successful. Governance and orchestration must keep pace with the dynamics of customers and the market. This includes, above all, the expansion of the value proposition to enlarge the market area, stabilize the market position, and address new customer needs."

*Total, research through first MVE:* roughly **44–60+ weeks** on the book's own numbers, before implementation begins.

## Key Takeaways

- Four lenses, five fits, four escalating forms of feasibility, and two changing criteria per lens — this is the book's core operating model.
- The lens tells you where you are, not what you are forbidden to skip; transitions are fluid and resolution-dependent.
- Skipping to lens #3 is permitted *only* if customer needs are identified and validations done via MVP.
- Test critical + unknown assumptions first, from the top-right quadrant of the assumptions matrix.
- On the book's own numbers, research → MVE is roughly a year; ecosystem design alone is 12–16 weeks and is "one of the most demanding" phases.
- Most initiatives fail at implementation and at the transitions between lenses, not at ideation.
