# Chapter 1 — Motivation and the Ten Mindshifts

*Pages 18–31 ("Motivation for This Book", "Mindshifts")*

## Core Idea

Design thinking for business growth is "the realization of new, unique offerings to customers **by means of different actors in an ecosystem**." The book's opening claim is that the age of business ecosystems expands the traditional view of business models and growth, and that this requires a genuine paradigm shift rather than an extension of existing practice. Ten named mindshifts define that shift; they are the book's entry test. Teams that have not made them revert to old patterns the moment they start collecting ideas about an ecosystem — the deliberations pivot on their own company's products and services instead of on customers and their current and future needs.

## Frameworks Introduced

**The Ten Mindshifts.** Each is stated as "Shift: from X to Y."

| # | From | To | What it means |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Product-centered / company-centered | Customer-centered | Focus is not one's own company or an existing product but customers, their current and future needs, and the tasks they want to perform |
| 2 | Linear | Iterative | The whole procedure — customer needs → prototypes → MVP → MVE — is iterative; prototypes are improved incrementally, rejected, or developed further |
| 3 | Analysis | Symbiosis | Traditional strategy is bounded by existing thought patterns about industries and competitors; well-designed ecosystems treat other actors, *including competitors*, as indispensable partners |
| 4 | Silos | Coevolution | Product categories, traditional segmentation, and core competencies give way to coevolution that lets actors share new markets, regions, and segments |
| 5 | Elements | Unique value propositions | Uniqueness emerges from meaningfully linking different skills, products, and platforms; a single market participant usually cannot realize or scale such a proposition |
| 6 | Decoupling | Networking | Ecosystems are agile network structures serving customer needs efficiently through channels that fit the customer |
| 7 | Isolation | Relationships / value streams | The view shifts from internal financial flows to value streams and relationships in systems, constantly in motion |
| 8 | Individual business model | Multidimensional view of business models | Alongside one's own model, explore the possibilities for the other actors; the more attractive their opportunities, the greater their willingness to implement and develop the proposition |
| 9 | Red/blue ocean | **Black ocean** | Configure so that other systems or companies cannot compete with the same or similar value proposition |
| 10 | Command and control | **Initiate and orchestrate** | Command-and-control mechanisms run aground against the dynamics and complexity of rapidly growing ecosystems |

**Four framing elements of the mindset** (stated as the "To the Point" summary of the motivation section):
- **Using variable mental states** — combining design thinking and systems thinking to arrive at scalable offerings, from problem statement through MVP/MVE.
- **Applying a design mindset** — working in iterations and experiments, with function and customer experience center stage.
- **Addressing hitherto unknown market opportunities** — explored from customer needs, developed with other actors through co-creation.
- **Ambition to realize a "black ocean" strategy** — giving actors the best framework for realizing the value proposition and making it difficult for rivals to stay in competition.

## Key Concepts

- **Business ecosystems do not create themselves.** Actors do not automatically connect and start radical collaboration. It is necessary to identify exactly who the potential partners are, what their roles are, what skills they bring, and how potential co-competition will be handled.
- **Ecosystem capital.** Ecosystems with high capital-market return rely largely on expansion of growth initiatives plus intellectual and ecosystem capital, monetized primarily from structures and connections to the respective actors.
- **The term's origin.** "Ecosystem" was originally a natural-science term for a biological community of interacting organisms and their physical environment; since the 1990s it has been used more generally for a complex network or interconnected system.
- **Who is inside an ecosystem.** Interactions are numerous, diverse, and multidimensional, involving established companies, start-ups, investors, service providers, technology providers, data brokers, payment systems, universities, and government agencies.
- **Foundational disciplines.** Design thinking, systems thinking, lean start-up, business ecosystem design, and scale methodology together constitute the foundation for design thinking for business growth.
- **Not a rigid framework.** Depth and breadth of application differ for each initiative and must be adapted. For complex projects, the book recommends appointing a skilled facilitator who coordinates, has methodological expertise, and moderates the respective actors.

## Mental Models

- **Coevolution is a natural process, not an option.** Because actors in the system constantly advance together, anybody sticking to silo thinking and clinging to existing structures will not be successful in the short or long term (mindshift #4).
- **The customer is not one actor among many.** "When it gets really bad, the customer is no more than one actor out of many in the system, instead of them taking center stage." This is the diagnostic for a failed mindshift #1.
- **Systems thinking as risk control.** Because ecosystems are usually very complex, systems thinking shapes sub-areas and keeps risks under control by applying the MVE approach — the same form of validation the lean start-up world knows through MVPs.
- **Prior digital transformation is a head start, not a substitute.** Companies that have already undergone major change processes because of digital transformation are in a better position, having already made one or another mindshift; but practice shows dedicated strategy or ecosystem-design teams reverting to old thinking anyway.

## Anti-patterns

- Treating the customer as one actor in the system rather than the center (failed mindshift #1).
- Linear procedure: building the complete ecosystem before validation instead of improving prototypes incrementally (failed #2).
- Using competitor analysis to find ecosystem fields of action — learning about competitors instead of creating symbiosis (failed #3).
- Protecting existing business models and prioritizing short-term financial optimization over viable systems (referenced repeatedly; contradicts #8).
- Looking only at one's own business model in an ecosystem play (failed #8).
- Aiming for stable linear growth patterns rather than agile realization of exponential growth.

## Worked Example

**The book's own six design principles for business growth** (the illustrated spread following the motivation section, pp. 12–13) — reproduced as the concrete artifact the chapter offers a practitioner:

1. **Using varying "design lenses."** Use the appropriate design lens depending on the starting point of considerations about the business ecosystem. Concentrate on customer needs and relevant experiences and functions in the early stages. Validate the solution with an MVP and use the value proposition to begin your ecosystem considerations. Design the business ecosystem across multiple loops and prepare the organization for exponential growth.

2. **Use momentum in the market and speed.** New ways of working: use agile working methods. Work with short design cycles, prototypes, and iterations. Make "fail fast" a principle in the design of MVPs and MVEs.

3. **Collaboration on interdisciplinary teams.** Transform the organization toward a collaborative culture. Be open to co-creation approaches and leverage existing assets and skills. Make use of the design thinking and systems thinking skills of the teams, depending on the situation.

4. **The art of letting existing structures go.** Include other actors in the ecosystem as partners on equal footing — they are part of delivering a unique value proposition to the customers whom you *share*. Think in value streams instead of risks. Change the perspective from "Yes, but" to "Yes!... and."

5. **Doubling the heartbeat.** Make it a principle that doing it is more important than talking about it. Share initial results, even if not final, with potential actors in the ecosystem. Always focus on the minimum viability of the product and the ecosystem.

6. **Selection of forward-looking team players, organizations, and companies.** Work together across company boundaries with people who are willing to learn and are optimistic. Learn via the people with T-shaped profiles in your own and in other organizations. Be willing to accept mistakes and see every instance of failure as an opportunity for reflection and learning.

These six principles map one-to-one onto the four design lenses (principle 1), the lean/agile operating rhythm (2, 5), the organizational transformation (3, 6), and the mindshift work (4).

## Key Takeaways

- The ten mindshifts are prerequisites, not aspirations; each has a concrete failure mode observable in a design team's behaviour.
- Mindshifts #9 (black ocean) and #10 (initiate and orchestrate) are the two that most distinguish this book from platform-economy literature.
- Ecosystem capital — the wealth of relationships between actors — is the asset the whole method is trying to build.
- Design thinking, systems thinking, lean start-up, ecosystem design, and scale are treated as one continuous toolkit, not five separate methods.
- Design principles for the initiative should be agreed by the team (and ideally with project sponsors and cross-company partners) at an early stage; see Ch 5 for the pyramid tool that produces them.
