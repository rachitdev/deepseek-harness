# Chapter 6 — Where to Play

*Pages 110–123 ("Where to Play?")*

## Core Idea

Because dealing with business ecosystems is entirely new for many companies, an **inventory** of existing strategies, capabilities, and partnerships usually marks the beginning. That inventory is commendable — it harmonizes later ecosystem target images with existing initiatives — but the chapter's real argument is that classical environmental analysis must be *re-founded* for ecosystems. The relationship between a company and its environment is different, because **business ecosystems can be shaped**: an initiator or orchestrator chooses or shapes the environment that matters most in the end, rather than searching for suitable adaptations to a given environment.

## Frameworks Introduced

**Initiatives/industry matrix** — inventory of what the company is already doing.
- *Purpose*: outline which other strategic initiatives are planned; include what new products and services should be launched; identify which programs, products, or initiatives are planned in individual submarkets, other regions, or countries.
- *Procedure*: (1) all initiatives across the entire company are written down by the responsible persons, or by workshop participants from different departments; (2) initiatives are placed on the matrix. Axes may vary — X axis for general labeling (**isolated function/manifestation/initiative ↔ networked**), reach divided into **own industry ↔ cross-industry**.
- *Reading rule*: "Initiatives that already are of a networked and cross-industry character are especially intriguing."

**Cooperation/industry matrix** — inventory of existing relationships.
- *Purpose*: analyze which partnerships and connections already exist; determine whether they are same-industry, industry-related, or completely outside the sector; identify capabilities and skills already on hand in an existing partner network; check whether suitable suppliers exist among partners and business relationships.
- *Procedure*: (1) write down all cooperative projects, supplier relationships, and partnerships; (2) place connections on the matrix. X axis for **focus ↔ diversification**; reach divided into own industry ↔ cross-industry. Categories used on the vertical: partnership, cooperation, supplier.
- *Reading rule*: "Some partnerships and cooperative projects are more used for EXPLOIT (left side of matrix); while others are designed to drive diversification in terms of the industry and the services to be provided."

**Map of topic areas** — the ecosystem landscape.
- *Purpose*: visualize the current situation of a business ecosystem in one topic for specific initiators or competitors; explore the relationships and respective participation of actors in one or more ecosystems; outline and discuss a planned value proposition through different dimensions and potential actors.
- *Procedure*: (1) start with companies that appear as initiators of such systems and represent their activities, topics, and connections to other actors; (2) alternatively look at what capabilities and skills are needed for a particular topic area and plot them on the map. Exploration of customer needs or a (re)design of an ecosystem yields more input.
- *Caution*: dimensions should always be adapted to the objective and strategic considerations, "since otherwise, maps become confusing quite quickly."

**Customer-centered analysis of topic areas** — the what-if procedure. Four steps:

| Step | Question | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **What are the current needs?** | Examines the status quo, the current reality. The "What if..." question should be based on a solid assessment and empathetic understanding of what is happening today before exploring potential topic areas |
| 2 | **What if?** | Automatically generates ideas; leads directly to potential topic fields; helps to think consciously outside the box |
| 3 | **Where might a "Wow!" be created for the customer?** | Based on first ideas for topics, corresponding assumptions can be made that are later reviewed over the course of design thinking |
| 4 | **What works?** | Usually answered in an iterative process through design lenses #1–3, validated by customer feedback |

*What the topic areas map helps the team do*: outline new topics roughly starting from basic needs; leave existing industry boundaries behind; start the creative process with an initial round of ideation; think in holistic solutions instead of concentrating on individual functions, products, or services; create new topic areas that so far have not been identified by pure analysis of existing ecosystems.

*Range of starting points*: from basic needs such as **energy, food, and water** through to complex needs such as **belonging to a community** or **exploiting one's own potential to the fullest**.

**PESTLE adapted for ecosystems.** Result is used as input for other tools (SWOT, SOAR, risk assessment for business model design) or, as in this book, as the basis for contextualizing an ecosystem strategy. Variations noted: PEST, STEEPLE, STEER, STEEP.

## Key Concepts

- **Most companies will operate in several ecosystems** in the future, assuming different roles — initiator, orchestrator, or participating actor.
- **Classic strategy tools contextualize.** PESTLE and "right to play / how to win" help make strategic options accessible to decision makers and management bodies. Establishing or participating in an ecosystem "is a strategic option and a vital element that will affect the business model and the future decision on technologies."
- **Discard regional fences.** When conceiving an ecosystem, existing fences separating regions and countries should be discarded, since many digital services and experiences do not have such artificial boundaries.
- **The risk in the inventory.** Companies have various options to position themselves in emerging ecosystems, but there is a risk of falling back into old ways of constantly striving for negotiating power. Customer needs, existing capabilities, and appetite for new disruptive business models must take center stage.

## Mental Models

- **Environmental Analysis 2.0.** Business ecosystems are more open, diverse, and multidimensional than known environmental, industry, and competition analyses. Think outside your own industry; apply ecosystems thinking.
- **Capabilities span the whole arc.** Skills required cover a wide range: understanding customer requirements; the ability to realize initial prototypes *together with other companies* and test them in MVEs; and — for participation as an actor — thinking in business ecosystems, assuming new roles, and creating the technical prerequisites for connecting with the other actors.

## Anti-patterns

- Conventional strategy exercises that are rigid and geared only to the familiar dimensions of one's own sector.
- Considerations limited to corporate alliances or individual corporate acquisitions for goal attainment.
- Using PESTLE unmodified: "it would be wise to adapt the questions to the requirements of business ecosystems."
- Not knowing your own house: "In many cases, the company doesn't even know where long-standing cooperation already exists that would provide quick customer access, data, or technology for the design of business ecosystems."

## Reference Tables

**Typical questions regarding one's own company:**
- What are the company's strengths and weaknesses (SWOT analysis)?
- What is the core business today?
- What skills and capabilities exist in the company?
- What is its current market role? What market role can be assumed without supplementing one's own business? What market roles are conceivable in the future?
- How high is the value of customer and transaction data?
- What kind of data can the company provide and which data does it require for a comprehensive view of customers?
- To what extent is the mindset of the decision makers open for participation in a business ecosystem?
- Do employees have the ability to think in business ecosystem structures, business models, and value streams?
- Are there any business relationships, partnerships, or shared networks with other companies that can be used? What companies are they?
- What functions, manifestations, and initiatives are in place or being planned that can pay into a specific ecosystem topic?

**Typical questions regarding the environment:**
- Which business ecosystems emerge locally and which globally?
- How do individual industries develop, and where does new value creation occur in cross-industry structures?
- What do other industries do in terms of business ecosystems, and how can the services, products, or data provided by the company create added value in the respective system?
- How large is one's own industry/sector in the respective ecosystems, and how high is the penetration rate of the integrated network economy?
- How large is the growth potential in the individual systems?
- To what extent have local and international competitors themselves begun to initialize or participate in business ecosystems?

**PESTLE for ecosystems — all six dimensions with the book's typical questions:**

| Dimension | Scope | Examples | Typical questions |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Political** | Political or politically motivated factors that might affect the project | Government policies, political stability/instability, bureaucracy, corruption, foreign trade policies, tax policies, trade restrictions, labor law, environmental law, copyright and consumer law, fair-competition regulation, subsidies, funding initiatives | Which government policies or political groups might be beneficial or harmful to the success of the ecosystem? Is the political environment stable or likely to change? |
| **Economic** | Macroeconomic forces | Economic trends, growth rates, industry growth, seasonal factors, taxation, inflation, interest rates, exchange rates, international trade, labor costs, consumers' disposable income, unemployment rate, availability of loans, monetary policy, raw material costs | Which economic factors will affect our further courses of action? Is the ecosystem affected by the current economic performance? How do changes in similar industries or ecosystems affect prices, revenues, and costs? |
| **Social** | Social aspects, attitudes, and trends affecting the business/ecosystem and target market | Attitudes and shared beliefs about health, work, leisure, money, customer service, imports, religion, cultural taboos, environment, population growth and demography, family size/structure, immigration/emigration, lifestyle trends | In what way do consumers' values and beliefs affect purchasing behavior? Do human behavior or cultural trends play a role for the ecosystem? How do customer needs change? |
| **Technological** | Technology influencing how products and services are made, sold, scaled, communicated | Technology and communication infrastructure, consumer access to technology, emerging technologies, automation, laws addressing technology, research and innovation, IP regulation, technology and development of competitors | What innovations and technological advances are available or will be available in the future? How might they affect the design, governance, and scaling of the ecosystem? |
| **Legal** | Current and future legal and regulatory requirements | Laws on consumer protection, work, health and safety, antitrust, intellectual property, data protection, tax, discrimination; international and national trade regulations and restrictions, advertising directives, product labeling and safety standards | What regulations and laws apply to the desired value proposition and new business models (e.g. in terms of data)? Do they help or hinder the project? Are the laws and regulations understood in **all the dimensions in which the business ecosystem will operate**? |
| **Environmental** | Environmental influences on the ecosystem, services provided, environment, natural resources | Weather, climate change, carbon footprint, environmental requirements, environmental laws and goals, recycling and waste disposal policies, endangered species, support for renewable energy | How does the physical environment affect the ecosystem and vice versa? What are the ramifications of the climate, weather, or geographical location? |

*Procedure*: address the typical questions per dimension; the answers help explore risks and opportunities in an early project phase; the results should be made available to **all** teams involved in designing the ecosystem and defining the ecosystem strategy.

## Worked Example

**The customer-centered topic area analysis, run on residential/living needs** — the map the book presents (pp. 55, 116–117) as the concrete output of the topic areas tool.

The premise: "Traditional industries and offerings are absorbed in new value propositions for customers." The map arranges customer-facing *topic areas* in the center and lists the traditional *industries* that get absorbed into them around the edge.

**Topic areas identified for ecosystems:**
wealth, security and administration · vacation and travel · safety, well-being · lifelong learning · residential living · areas for sustainable living · adventure/experience · digital life · payments and transactions · marketplace (B2C) · marketplace (B2B) · mobility · education · restaurants · automotive · logistics · online commerce · peer-to-peer · virtual meetings · insurance companies · health.

**Traditional industries feeding them:**
art/culture · automotive · banks · construction/architecture · consulting · education · chemical · service · IT · food/agriculture · purchasing · electrical/electronics · energy · finance · R&D · health/social/care · commerce · crafts and trades · real estate · industrial · Internet · insurance · public administration · marketing/advertising/PR · market research · mechanical engineering · media · medical/pharmaceutical · medical technology · printing/packaging · HR and recruitment · law · seminars/trade fairs · other industries · sports/beauty · tax consultants/auditors · telecommunications · textile · tourism/catering · clubs · transport/traffic/logistics.

**How this drives the what-if question.** The chapter's illustrated prompt is literally "WHAT IF OUR POTENTIAL CUSTOMERS...", to be completed against a topic area. The mechanism: pick a topic area from the map, ask what if such experiences, functions, or products were made possible by a combination of actors and skills — what system would emerge? The book's own observed instances of this having already happened: digital providers of payment solutions offer car sharing; department stores open their doors to e-commerce giants; insurance companies take care not only of financial wellness but, across industries, of mental fitness; car makers become orchestrators for car-sharing and suppliers of micromobility.

The same map is what makes the "who is initiator / orchestrator / has a strategic initiative here?" grid answerable — the topic-field-by-industry matrix on p. 117 is the map turned into a stocktake.

## Key Takeaways

- Where to play begins with two inventories (initiatives, cooperations) and one landscape (topic areas map), then a what-if to escape the inventory's gravity.
- The reading rule for both matrices is the same: the interesting cells are networked and cross-industry.
- Basic needs (energy, food, water) through self-actualization are legitimate starting points for topic-area exploration — the book explicitly ranges that far.
- PESTLE must be re-questioned for ecosystems, and its output shared with every team, not filed with strategy.
- The decisive difference from classical analysis: the ecosystem's environment is **chosen and shaped**, not given.
