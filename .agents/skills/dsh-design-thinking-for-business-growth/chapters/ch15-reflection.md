# Chapter 15 — Reflection

*Pages 287–296 ("Reflection on Design Thinking for Business Growth")*

## Core Idea

"In all agile and iterative procedural models, it has proven worthwhile to reflect on each step at different levels." This chapter defines **three levels** of reflection — the specific initiative, the meta level, and the personal level — and supplies four tools for running them. Its argument for necessity: "If you don't check regularly and discuss openly how the collaboration works, it is impossible to ensure that the team is really acting in concert and that the greater ambition of the business ecosystem is achieved."

The personal level is the one this book adds to standard retrospective practice, because the mindshifts are personal: reflection is where you find out whether you, as ecosystem leader, "act as an ecosystem leader within the framework of initiate and orchestrate or do I fall back into old patterns?"

## Frameworks Introduced

**Three levels of reflection:**

| Level | Scope | Typical questions |
|---|---|---|
| **Initiative** (view of the specific initiative) | The project currently being worked on | How satisfied are we with the result of the last MVE? Have we achieved our goals? If not, why? How can we improve collaboration on the team and with the other actors? How can we optimize the results, responsibilities, and future implementation activities within the scope of co-creation? |
| **Meta level** (larger view of the change and the procedure) | The approach itself, across initiatives | What is ecosystem design? What are the advantages and disadvantages? What are the challenges entailed in the change? What is the difference from other approaches? How can different approaches be combined? Where are use cases in one's own environment? How can it be introduced across the board? What is the immediate benefit? |
| **Personal** | The individual's own mindshifts | How has your personal view of topic areas and growth changed? Which paradigm shift is easy to make, and where are there mental hurdles? How serious is your personal willingness to change for a new kind of ecosystem leadership? To what extent do the ecosystem design team, decision makers, and partners in the ecosystem fall back into old thought patterns? How can I personally contribute to the change? |

**When to reflect.** After completing a micro cycle in design thinking; after every sprint during agile development of an MVP; after each design lens; during the transition to the next design lens; and — for more extensive projects — during the project as well.

Initiative-level lessons-learned prompts (the standard set): What went well in the project? Which methods have been used? How? Why? What was not so good? What should be done differently in project work in the future? What are the key learnings from the initiative (positive and negative)?

## Key Concepts

- **Reflection is not only about outcomes.** The chapter's opening questions are about collaboration: "How well do we actually work together? How do we get along on the team? Could some things be improved? And if so, what things?"
- **Dynamic environments need regular intervals.** "In a dynamic environment in particular, it is important for both the individual and the team to reflect at regular intervals about things and find out where the team stands and how collaboration may be improved."
- **Mindshifts demand leaving the comfort zone.** Personal reflection concerns "the design of a business ecosystem and the associated mindshifts that demand questioning existing hypotheses and leaving the comfort zone both as an individual and an organization."
- **A feedback ritual.** The "I like, I wish" tool is meant to establish a ritual "in which only 'I like,' 'I wish' are allowed."
- **Feedback as a gift.** "Any feedback should be seen as a gift. The gift must be wrapped in such a way that the positive basic mood is maintained."

## Mental Models

- **Empowerment as the success test.** The chapter's epigraph: *"If you want to win in the 21st century, you have to make sure you are making other people more powerful. Empower others. Make sure other people are better than you are. Then you will be successful."* — Jack Yun Ma, founder and CEO, Alibaba.
- **The failure mindset.** The sailboat retrospective "promotes the failure mindset in which mistakes are not seen as failures but as an opportunity to change and learn."
- **Get to causes, not symptoms.** In the sailboat's clustering step: "Get to the bottom of things, i.e. identify causes, so that not only the symptoms are tackled. The aim is also to address unpleasant issues and create the basis for improvements."
- **Do not argue with feedback.** "Starting a discussion as the recipient of the feedback should be avoided. It would change the mood, and the positive attitude is lost."

## Anti-patterns

- Falling back into old thought patterns — the explicit thing personal reflection is designed to detect, in oneself, in the design team, in decision makers, and in ecosystem partners.
- Ad hominem criticism — what "I like, I wish" is designed to avoid.
- Tackling symptoms rather than causes in a retrospective.
- Not checking regularly, so that the team's real alignment is unknown.

The canvas's overlay structure maps its own fields to the strategy questions: *reflection on lenses* on the left, *reflection on strategy* on the right, with the strategy side reading **where to play? / how to configure? / how to win?** — closing the loop back to Chapters 5–7.

**Feedback capture grid** — four quadrants:

| ✚ I like | Δ I wish |
|---|---|
| Things you like or find remarkable | Constructive criticism |
| **? Questions** | **💡 Ideas** |
| Questions that have arisen from experience | Ideas that arise from the experience or presentation |

What it achieves: collect and appraise experiences made in the project in a structured way; learn from experience and use it in the next project; facilitate a positive attitude toward mistakes and appreciate progress; identify and document findings, make them applicable and finally usable. Use: "records the current status at any time and uses the lessons learned to tackle the next phase or the next project." Note: "not only the things that went well are entered but also constructive criticism. And there is lots of space for open questions and ideas that have come up in the process and while working together." The outcome is that members of the respective design and implementation teams **agree on actions and improvements and implement them in the agreed form**.

**Retrospective sailboat — the four metaphor elements:**

| Element | Meaning | Example given |
|---|---|---|
| **Island (goal, vision)** | The shared common vision and motivation the team has | "Nicest insurance company in the world, and the customer will love it" |
| **Wind (accelerating factors)** | What gave us some wind at our backs | Regular consultations and daily meetings |
| **Anchor (inhibitory factors)** | What didn't work so well on the team and slowed us down | Lack of rules, undefined roles, no suitable tools |
| **Cliffs (environmental factors)** | Hazards and risks against which the team has no power | New laws; market, new technologies, competitors |

Five-step procedure:
1. **Opening** — introduce the goals and sequence of the retrospective session.
2. **Collect information** — on Post-its stuck to the corresponding fields. Typical questions: What happened recently? What was good? What gave us wind (WIND)? What slowed us down (ANCHOR)? What are the hazards and risks against which the team has no power (CLIFFS)? What shared vision and motivation does the team have (ISLAND)? Everybody reads their Post-its aloud.
3. **Cluster and prioritize findings** — select and go into greater depth on the most important topics; identify causes, not symptoms; address unpleasant issues.
4. **Define measures** — document precisely anything to be changed or tried out in a following iteration.
5. **Conclude the retro** — everybody gives brief feedback on the retro session, e.g. with a feedback capture grid. "Ultimately, the group should part from one another with good feelings."

Retro agenda as the book sets it: *goals and procedure → gather information → develop insights → define measures → conclusion (feedback on the retro)*.

When it applies: frequently used with scrum for sprint reflection; "also very suitable after each design lens or after one step or subproject has been completed"; usable as a debriefing after joint facilitation of a workshop, a co-creation session, or after interviews with potential actors for a planned business ecosystem.

## Worked Example

**Running "I like, I wish" (and "I wonder") on an ecosystem design review** — the chapter's own worked feedback exchange (p. 294).

*Where it applies.* "Feedback is needed in every iteration and every design lens. It is used for the improvement of prototypes, stories, business models, and entire business ecosystems." The tool is "particularly suitable for sensitive projects. By maintaining a positive mood, a relationship that is based on partnership evolves between the feedback provider and the feedback recipient. It can be used in the context of reflecting on the collaboration as well as for a specific result."

*The book's own example phrasings, on collaboration:*
- "**I like** how you motivated us to conduct another customer survey."
- "**I wish** not only digital channels would be used but physical ones as well."

*The book's own example phrasings, on an ecosystem design result:*
- "**I like** the various options for interaction a potential customer has in the ecosystem."
- "**I wish** that geo data and location data would be used as well to see where the customer actually is."
- "**I wonder** why we haven't been participating in a business ecosystem up to now."

Note what each of these does. The *I like* names a specific design decision (interaction options in the ecosystem), not the person. The *I wish* proposes a concrete addition to the value streams (geo and location data) rather than criticizing what exists. The *I wonder* raises the strategic question — the one that belongs to the meta and personal levels of reflection — without accusing anyone of having failed to raise it.

*The three-step procedure:*
1. Communicate the rules for feedback clearly and encourage a positive mood.
2. Write the feedback down, express your thanks, and use it to improve the collaboration, prototypes, and entire business ecosystems.
3. Create space for new ideas and possibilities with "**I wonder...**"

*What it achieves:* establish a feedback ritual in which only "I like," "I wish" are allowed; appreciate even small successes achieved in an iteration, with a prototype, or in a test; use reflection as the basis of continuous improvement; give and receive written and spoken feedback.

*The one rule that makes it work:* "**Important:** Starting a discussion as the recipient of the feedback should be avoided. It would change the mood, and the positive attitude is lost. The application of this tool aims at avoiding ad hominem criticism and maintaining a positive mood."

## Key Takeaways

- Three reflection levels: initiative, meta, personal — the personal level tests whether the ten mindshifts are actually being lived.
- Reflect after each micro cycle, each sprint, each design lens, and at every lens transition.
- The reflection canvas's four top fields (design lenses, digital fluency, ecosystem leadership, market opportunities) and four bottom fields (enabler technologies, big data analytics/AI/ML/DL, capital and assets, governance) mirror the ecosystem capability stack from Ch 7.
- Feedback capture grid: I like / I wish / questions / ideas — and it must end in agreed actions, not notes.
- Sailboat: island, wind, anchor, cliffs — with cliffs explicitly reserved for what the team cannot control.
- "I like, I wish, I wonder" is a ritual with one hard rule: the recipient does not argue back.
