# Chapter 8 — Ecosystem Leadership

*Pages 149–162 ("Ecosystem Leadership")*

## Core Idea

"Ecosystem leadership is the **networking of all actors involved in value creation**." It is based on the idea of initializing a functioning system and orchestrating it with a clear-cut target image — mindshift #10 made operational. The chapter argues that ecosystem innovation is more than defining new business models: prototypes of *entire systems* are needed, i.e. MVEs by which key risks are tested before roles such as orchestrator or actor are assumed. In transformation terms, "minimum viable" means every important element for a well-functioning ecosystem must be thoroughly examined — and transformation is the constant, not the exception, because ecosystems evolve dynamically.

## Frameworks Introduced

**Six qualities of successful eco-leadership:**
customer centered · digitally adept · ambitious · patient · open to new relationships · team oriented.

The book adds a characterization: eco-leaders are **relationship builders** who also understand the technologies required to realize such ecosystems, and are "courageous and ambitious."

**Ecosystem leadership and team maturity — the four-stage progression:**
1. Without a defined strategy
2. Clear responsibility for ecosystem projects
3. Ecosystem and innovation teams close to the market
4. Leverage of actors in the ecosystem for customer-centered innovation

**Innovation team matrix** — placing innovation teams by complexity of knowledge:

| | Market complexity: high | Market complexity: low |
|---|---|---|
| **Knowledge on technology: low** | Move information about the technology to where market and customer knowledge are located | Exchange of information on a higher level is usually sufficient |
| **Knowledge on technology: high** | Move and exchange knowledge through a **rotating workforce** and temporary co-allocation | Move information about the market and the customers to where the technology is located |

**Three leadership transformation domains** (the "Ecosystem evolution" wheel):
- **Transformation of business models** — transform traditional models toward value streams enabling revenue from data, products, services, and combinations; build ecosystem capital through relationships of the actors and utilization of network effects; focus on customer experience with new approaches to branding and customer interaction.
- **Transformation of the organization** — build new capabilities or venture/ecosystem units that rethink existing partnership/alliance models; foster design thinking and systems thinking skills to design MVPs and MVEs in iterations; build digital skills and close gaps in the use of new technologies; establish a cooperative culture allowing targeted orchestration.
- **Transformation of the culture** — toward an entrepreneurial mindset with new agility that is **not limited to IT**.

**T-shaped ecosystem design teams.** The horizontal bar is breadth of knowledge (team members from many cultures, disciplines, systems); the vertical bar is specialist know-how (a system, an industry, a discipline). Collaboration capability and interface expertise are extremely important. Named skills across the T for design-thinking-for-business-growth teams: build empathy · prototyping · industry know-how · strategic foresight · design thinking · systems thinking · storytelling · lean start-up · UI design (software development teams add scrum master).

**Team-of-teams approach.** The shift is from **scalable efficiency** (traditional organization, "doing things right") to **scalable adaptability** (ecosystem play, "doing the right things to reach the target image"). In the case of unpredictable changes, adaptability is by far the most vital capability.

Five crucial values for a team of teams:
1. **Confidence** in the skills and decisions of the other teams and their members.
2. **Transparency** of tasks, goals, and results across all teams.
3. **Personal relationships** in the network structures — at least one team member must know one team member from each other team.
4. **Empowered execution** — decentralization of responsibility and actions.
5. **Collective mindset** for radical collaboration, flexibility, and adaptability to new situations.

**The North Star cascade** — four altitudes of target image, each with its own questions and its own fit:

| Level | Content | Questions | Fit |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Target image** | Greater ambition, new value streams + digital enablers fully effective | How is the goal to be achieved? Who is to be served? What is the value proposition? | **Coevolution fit** |
| **MVEs** | New actors, testing of new value proposition, design of a functioning system | What capabilities does the system need? Which roles can be assumed by which actors? Do all actors benefit from participating? | **Actors fit** |
| **MVPs** | Improvements without changing the grand vision | What is the customer willing to pay? Can we validate the assumptions about customers and functions? | **Market fit** |
| **Prototypes** | Get a first idea of potential solutions | What are the customer needs? Which customer problem do we want to solve, and how? | **Problem/solution fit** |

## Key Concepts

- **What the initiator must demonstrate.** Initialization and participation both begin with a clear vision. The initiator demonstrates a compelling value proposition making it attractive for others to participate. For a would-be actor, the vision must clearly show how the company benefits (leverage of skills, positive impact on core business, entirely new sources of income) and how it can cash in on growth long-term.
- **Autonomy of implementation teams** is required — including open talks with various potential actors and a culture of doers reflecting an entrepreneurial mindset. Traditional companies often define **venture units** or dedicated **ecosystem units** to increase implementation speed.
- **Team composition.** Convincing methodological expertise from the design thinking and systems thinking toolbox, plus big data analytics, UX design, and IT expertise. **Enterprise architecture becomes a major issue**, since interactions from the ecosystem must align with existing infrastructure and enable direct or indirect customer contact through increasingly digital — but also analog — interactions, applying multi-channel or opti-channel strategies.
- **Hiring rule.** "When selecting and hiring such team members, the mindset and the skills are more important than any job titles" — particularly for ecosystem design, because there are only a handful of companies with the right mindset and growth background from which suitable candidates may be poached.
- **VUCA → VUCA-response.** Volatility → Vision; Uncertainty → Understanding; Complexity → Clarity; Ambiguity → Agility.
- **Thinking in real options.** Depending on market situation and customer needs, determine the next steps and objectives without losing sight of the target image.
- **The biggest challenges.** Moving beyond existing market and product segmentations; changing business models to include direct *and* indirect revenue sources; establishing governance structures that allow step-by-step implementation of the ecosystem strategy.

## Mental Models

- **Velocity, not haste.** "It's not about haste but about velocity. Too often, teams hurry and hasten and leave out important steps of the design lenses just to be finished a couple of weeks earlier. In some cases, taking a shortcut may be the right thing to do, but then there is a high risk that the solution isn't radical enough and there is no crisp execution."
- **Two steps backward.** "Taking two steps backward and pondering what would really generate an impact and is desired by the customers is the key to success."
- **Chainsaw and chisel.** "For successful design thinking for business growth, you sometimes need a chainsaw, sometimes a chisel. The team should know and sense exactly which tool to use to reach the objectives."
- **Impact before action plan.** "Don't start with an action plan. Don't build a kanban board full of tasks. Decide what kind of impact you want to have and then work toward it, performing measurements along the entire way. Use feedback from the real world to make a real difference."
- **Mindful transformation** through rotating teams — ensuring dialog across departmental silos. Software development teams, for example, also need market and customer knowledge to validate MVPs and define use cases.

## Anti-patterns

- Command and control mechanisms applied to rapidly growing ecosystems.
- Governance modelled on partner management.
- Teams working without specific objectives or target user benefits — they need far more time in each development step.
- Skipping steps of the design lenses to finish weeks earlier.
- Setting OKRs that can be 100% achieved: "If OKRs are 100% achieved, the objectives were not ambitious enough."
- Leaders unwilling to accept the risks of change, modify organizational structures, or set the parameters of future ways of working.

## Reference Tables

**OKRs for design thinking for business growth — rules of thumb:**

| Parameter | Value |
|---|---|
| Near-term objectives | e.g. one quarter |
| Long-term objectives | 12 or more months |
| OKR cycle length (vs MBOs) | two to four months |
| Healthy plan | maximum **five objectives per quarter with four key results** |
| Target attainment | **80% goal attainment = outstanding performance**; 100% means objectives were not ambitious enough |
| Individual objectives per person | 1–2 "individual objectives" plus 2–4 "individual near-term objectives" |
| Moonshot guidance | OKRs based on ambitious stretch goals (e.g. 80% achievement is excellent) |

**Seven-step cascading strategy with OKRs:**
1. Align the vision with the objectives.
2. Decide which objectives have a high impact/priority.
3. Break the team objective down into initiatives and let each team member derive individual objectives.
4. Set key results for each initiative and individual objective, and start working on it now.
5. Start to deliver the defined key results.
6. Define tasks.
7. Plan your activities.

The cascade runs **Vision → Initiatives → Goals → Strategies → Tactics → Action plans**.

**Typical questions for growth initiatives** when setting OKRs:
- How can we change or adjust the mindset?
- How can we bring rigor to the metrics?
- What is important?
- How do we solve user/customer needs? How do we ensure that users/customers continue to get value?

**The OKR prioritization quadrant** (urgent × important logic used in the tool):
- *Important + urgent*: set it up and measure.
- *Important + not urgent*: work on initiatives and measure results; focus on what is important; create better alignment through transparency.
- *Urgent + delegable*: delegate it — what can be delegated to free up time and resources?
- Then: what's next? What is no longer important? Free up time.

Stretch OKRs within the organization, across business units, **and across business ecosystem partners**. Track progress throughout all design lenses. Create OKRs together with the team and allow feedback to improve.

## Worked Example

**OKRs for a business ecosystem design initiative** (pp. 160–161) — the book's own filled-in example.

*Top level — Business ecosystem strategy in a specific topic area.* The framing metaphor for the top-level objective is **"Building the umbrella."**

*Team-of-teams level.* The team objective at this level is captured as **"Kick-start radical collaboration."**

*Individual level.* Individual long-term objectives (1–2 per person).

The explanations the book attaches to each level:

- **Creating a team-of-teams culture** has been set as a **long-term objective for the entire team** and constitutes a defined part of the transformation and application of the desired mindshifts. (It is deliberately not a quarterly target — the culture change is the long horizon.)
- **The team objectives are aligned to the overall strategy** — e.g. the ambition to create or participate in a specific topic area of a business ecosystem based on new or changed customer needs and/or behaviors. (This is the direct link back to "where to play.")
- **OKRs are also used to define 1–2 "individual objectives" and 2–4 "individual near-term objectives."** The individual objectives reflect the way in which every team member will contribute individually to the team objectives.
- **All objectives are transparent to all team members.** In addition, all teams trust in the work and professionalism of the other teams. (This operationalizes team-of-teams values 1 and 2.)

*Why OKRs specifically.* The chapter's argument: design thinking for business growth initiatives move incredibly fast, and the latest insights, points of view, prototypes, and tests always deliver new results. Teams moving in such an environment need metrics that are more precise, focused on a specific objective and corresponding key results — which is why they can move faster and deliver more specific results. Following the design lens rhythm, new insights are gained after **each design lens or design sprint**, and those findings in turn influence the near-term or long-term objectives; objectives can be adjusted over the year.

*What the right OKRs depend on.* The maturity of the organization — specifically (a) the extent to which mindshifts have been completed so as to be able to think in business ecosystems, and (b) how much the chosen governance fosters the orchestration of such ecosystems. The chosen OKRs can take into account the desired mindset and type of organization, the way problems are quickly solved, and customer centeredness **across all growth phases within the "problems to growth and scale" framework**.

*Purpose of the transparency.* "Transparency about the objectives and key results on the team and across team boundaries aims solely at making the company's strategic topics and potential growth initiatives visible at all levels of the organization."

*The claimed result.* "Applying design thinking for business growth in combination with team of teams has proven to lead toward breakthrough innovations in many initiatives."

## Key Takeaways

- Eco-leadership = initiate and orchestrate with a clear target image; leaders are relationship builders who also understand the enabling technologies.
- Six eco-leader qualities: customer centered, digitally adept, ambitious, patient, open to new relationships, team oriented.
- Team of teams trades scalable efficiency for scalable adaptability, on five values including the concrete "at least one member must know one member of every other team."
- The North Star cascade ties each altitude of ambition to a specific fit: prototype→problem/solution, MVP→market, MVE→actors, target image→coevolution.
- OKR discipline: ≤5 objectives/quarter × 4 key results, 2–4 month cycles, 80% attainment as excellent, stretched across ecosystem partners too.
