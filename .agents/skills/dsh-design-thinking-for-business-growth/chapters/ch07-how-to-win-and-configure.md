# Chapter 7 — How to Win and Configure

*Pages 124–148 ("How to Win and Configure?")*

## Core Idea

Configuration is where an ecosystem strategy becomes specific. The chapter supplies two instruments — the **ecosystem play and win configuration framework** (a Lafley/Martin "playing to win" choice cascade adapted to ecosystems) and the **ecosystem configuration grid** (eight dimensions, each with three or four manifestations) — and then demonstrates them with three fully filled-in strategic manifestations. Its central warning: the dimensions are interdependent. "Each turn of an adjusting screw in the configuration has consequences for other dimensions. The dynamics are comparable to a **mobile** that is constantly moving and whose parts mutually affect one another."

## Frameworks Introduced

**The choice cascade, adapted** (after A.G. Lafley and Roger Martin, *Playing to Win*), with an added ecosystem-specific element:

| Element | Meaning in an ecosystem |
|---|---|
| **A winning aspiration** | The purpose of the ecosystem, the motivating aspiration of all actors, is to be successful |
| **Where to play** | A topic area where the goal can be achieved |
| **How to win** | The manner in which you can win in the chosen topic area |
| **Core capabilities** | The capabilities and expertise required for achieving the chosen target image |
| **Management systems** | Governance that makes it possible to expand existing capabilities, build new ones, and achieve the prioritized target image |
| **How to configure** *(added)* | Enhancing the understanding of roles in the ecosystem, including the shaping of customer interactions and offers |

**Ecosystem play and win configuration framework** — three panels:

| Where to play? | How to configure? | How to win? |
|---|---|---|
| *Definition of market opportunity, market area, and customer needs* | *Definition of the capabilities, roles, and governance* | *Definition of the procedure, tactics, and strategy* |
| Topic area, environment analysis | Capabilities | Ecosystem vision, exponential growth |
| Customer needs; customer interaction; customer relationships | Strategic focus; customer interaction; customer relationships; capabilities and skills (e.g. IT, data); product and service portfolio; organizational design; competitive strategy; roles | Product and service portfolio; roles in the ecosystem; organizational design and governance; multidimensional view of the business models |
| **EXPLOIT** — existing partnerships, initiatives, technologies, strategies | | **EXPLORE** — new partnerships, initiatives, technologies, strategies |

**Ecosystem configuration grid** — the tool that turns those panels into choices.
- *Purpose*: carry out the critical configurations; develop a configuration matching the initiative and role (e.g. initiator or active participant); address the elements critical to success from strategic focus through customer interaction to organizational design; describe configuration elements accurately to reach clarity on intention and goal attainment.
- *Structure*: each row is a **dimension** (e.g. customer relationship); each cell is a **manifestation** (e.g. digital-centered) with a short description (e.g. "digital first").
- *Procedure*: (1) results from where-to-play shape the configuration; (2) the configuration is closely linked to the actual definition of the project, chosen tactics, and strategies (how to win); (3) the configuration should contain all elements required to make an ecosystem vision a reality step by step, with dimensions and manifestations selected and described to fit the initiative.
- The dimensions and manifestations shown are "illustrative and exemplary"; specific dimensions should be developed individually for each ecosystem.

## Key Concepts

- **Corporate reality is a mixture.** Companies normally use both EXPLOIT and EXPLORE. EXPLOIT contributions (existing skills, core business activities, existing business models) can realize new revenue streams or address a larger market area and broaden competitiveness — but such considerations usually result in ecosystems for **linear growth only**, because it is not the ecosystem, multipliers, and other actors that take center stage but the transfer of one's own business model into an ecosystem.
- **EXPLORE greenfield.** Alternatively, companies create exploratory systems with a unique value proposition and new configurations of business models including the partners'. Configured correctly, these have black ocean potential; superiority arises from a business model design that makes participation attractive to all desired actors, surpassing familiar differentiations by price, technology, customer access, or brand. Such ecosystems usually emerge as a **greenfield approach**, far removed from traditional corporate structures and mindsets.
- **The playbook.** Experience from initiatives helps document moves that worked particularly well in a **playbook for business growth**, repeated when they fit. "If it doesn't work, improvise! The failure of past moves fuels the alternation of planned moves and improvisation."
- **S-shaped ecosystem evolution.** Three named strategic manifestations are derived from observing initiatives: **engaged participant**, **focused challenger**, **life cycle approach**. Companies typically move up an S-curve from digital transformation → solution integrator/bundler in a life cycle approach → greenfield ecosystem initiatives, or take a direct radical path to initiator/orchestrator — a step "only taken by companies and business leaders who have mentally performed and internalized all mindshifts."
- **Ecosystem capital vs tangible capital.** Traditional companies have a very high proportion of tangible and human capital; neither allows greater margins or exploitation of new growth opportunities. Companies that understand ecosystem capital and generate win-win through multidimensional business models can realize exponential growth.

## Mental Models

- **The mobile.** Turning one configuration screw moves the others; the configuration must be continuously adapted so that all elements are directly or indirectly connected.
- **Configurations are snapshots.** "Configurations change over the course of time since they are dynamic constructs. The following examples are only snapshots of business ecosystems that are currently evolving or have already been established in the market."
- **Digital fluency and enabler stack.** The chapter closes the strategy part with a summary illustration: design thinking / systems thinking / lean start-up / design + scale as the outer frame, and inside it **design lenses, digital fluency, ecosystem leadership, market opportunities** over **enabler technologies, big data analytics, capital and assets, governance**, all sitting on **corporate strategy**.

## Anti-patterns

- Sticking to EXPLOIT and expecting exponential growth: "Companies that bank on EXPLOIT and stick to their existing focus have a hard time generating exponential growth. Their growth through an ecosystem will be increased by a **factor of 2 at the most**."
- Configuring one dimension in isolation and ignoring the knock-on consequences (the mobile).
- Treating the illustrated configuration dimensions as a standard set rather than developing dimensions individually for the ecosystem.
- Governance modelled on established partner management: ecosystem governance "has nothing in common with the established ideas of partner management in businesses. It is more about the orchestration of employees and other actors in the system."

## Reference Tables

**Financial contrast: ecosystem capital vs traditional sectors** (research of Staeritz and Torrance, 2020, plus sector figures):

| | Return on investment | Margins | Price–sales ratio |
|---|---|---|---|
| Companies exploiting ecosystem capital | usually above 20% | over 40% | 10x |
| Banks | ~1.5% return on capital | 7–8% | 1.5x |
| Traditional retailing | less than 3% | 5–6% | 1x |

Exception noted: industries with a large share of intellectual capital (e.g. biotech, software providers).

**The eight configuration dimensions with their manifestations:**

| Dimension | Manifestations (left → right) |
|---|---|
| **Strategic focus** | **EXPLOIT** (known market opportunities: existing capabilities, products, customer interfaces, markets; or expansion of existing business models into an ecosystem) → **EXPLORE** (unknown market opportunities: emerging customer needs, new behaviors and markets, new product/service groups, or assuming new roles and business models) |
| **Customer interaction** (human-centered → digital-centered) | **Human led** (physical interaction across all transaction types and segments) · **Multi-channel / generic hybrid** (digitally supported or fully automated interaction across all transaction types and segments) · **Opti-channel / tailored hybrid** (optimized service model with custom-tailored interactions based on each individual customer's needs) · **Digital first** (minimizes physical interaction unless physical interaction is integral to the value proposition) |
| **Customer interface, customer relationships** | **Decentralized** (consistent digital customer interface without a personal contact person) · **Point-to-point** (targeted personal interaction for specific issues by subject matter experts) · **Hub-and-spoke** (individual service model with specific contacts for particular target groups, issues, or transaction types) |
| **Capabilities in terms of IT, data, infrastructure, AI** (sustainable growth → radical transformation) | **Expanding capabilities** (investments in digitization or replacement of critical skills) · **Investment in new capabilities (end-to-end)** (comprehensive transformation across large parts of the value chain with cutting-edge technology) · **New setup (greenfield)** (entirely new technology platform operating as a separate company or business unit, exploiting the full potential of new digital capabilities) |
| **Product and service portfolio** (comprehensive range → specific offers) | **Full service** (very wide and deep range delivered by your own company and other actors) · **Targeted products and services** (specific offers tailored to defined customer needs and sub-segments, new or expanding existing components) · **Life cycles and bundles** (services and products focused on customer needs in different phases of life — "cradle to grave") |
| **Organizational design** (product-centered → customer-centered) | **Business unit and product structure** (participation follows known product areas and business units) · **Customer-centered structure** (structures and processes designed so large parts of the organization concentrate on a unique customer experience, including digital channels and new incentives as "anchors" in recurring interaction) · **Transversal and agile design teams** (structure shaped by transversal collaboration of different design teams, allowing greater agility) |
| **Competitive strategy** | **Red ocean** (compete in an existing market, exploit existing demand, beat competitors) · **Blue ocean** (establish a new market, tap new demand, dodge competitors) · **Black ocean** (unique value proposition that could not be delivered by an individual company; satisfying new customer needs to generate an ecosystem of co-competition and coevolution) |
| **Roles in the business ecosystem** | **Actor** (capabilities or offerings integrated into the value proposition; innovates within principles and rules; adjusts parts of its activities, business models, and strategies) · **Initiator** (discovered a new customer need, begins initialization, often provides the budget for prototypes and MVP, often switches to orchestrator or becomes an actor in its own system) · **Orchestrator** (coordinates activities, lays down principles and rules, defines framework conditions for exponential growth, wants sustainable and targeted governance) |

Guidance: usually **three of the roles are of greatest strategic importance** and should be analyzed and defined in the ecosystem strategy. Companies may hold several roles across different ecosystems simultaneously.

**Consequences table — what changes when you choose** (selection):

| Selection of... | Examples | Possible consequences |
|---|---|---|
| Customer interactions | Addressing new customer groups/segments; price discrimination; physical, multidimensional, or 100% digital channels | Intensity of competition; types of customer segments; number of touch points, user experience design |
| Customer interfaces, customer relationships | Width and depth of offers per segment; decentralized, hub-and-spoke, or point-to-point support | Automation possibilities; know-how of experts; physical and digital presence |
| Capabilities (IT, data, infrastructure, AI) | Use of resources of low strategic importance; development of in-house technology | Availability of resources on the labor market; no need for partners in technology development |
| Roles in the business ecosystem | Function as initiator; role of orchestrator; participating actor with specific or general tasks | Amount of pre-investment for prototypes and MVPs; degree of responsibility for growth; degree of opening, adaptation, and provision of skills |
| Product and service portfolio elements | Full service with wide and deep range; specific offerings or amplification of existing offers; satisfying individual or a variety of needs | Number of different actors in the system; complexity of orchestration |
| Features of organizational design | Interaction/co-creation with new actors; direct marketing/bundling | Bargaining power of the stakeholders; no need for distributors |
| Competitive strategies (ocean) | Continuation of red ocean; strong differentiation via blue ocean; realization of black ocean with a unique value proposition | Degree of differentiation; opportunities for growth; radicalness of current and future innovations |

**Ecosystem evolution stages** (the S-curve panel, low → high complexity and access to new value streams):

| Stage | Characteristics |
|---|---|
| **Traditional companies with a strategic focus on digital transformation and digital channel expansion** (low) | Focus on improving digital customer interfaces; first initiatives in big data analytics; sponsoring and interaction with innovation ecosystems (e.g. start-up initiatives) |
| **Ecosystem player with a strategic focus on the role of solution integrator/bundler in a life cycle approach** (medium) | Focus on aggregation or integration of the services provided by business partners; provision of a digital marketplace/platform; use of new business models with new direct and indirect value streams |
| **Ecosystem player with a strategic focus on the role of initiator and orchestrator, based on the greenfield approach with new technology** (high) | Active role as ecosystem orchestrator; use of state-of-the-art information technology; definition of multiple value streams and business model options; data-driven service and product development with further options of monetizing data |

## Worked Example

**Three strategic manifestations, filled in on the configuration grid** (pp. 142–145). Each is a complete configuration; the difference between them is entirely in which manifestation is selected per dimension.

**1. Active participation / innovator**
*Description*: the participating actor role, characterized by bringing in-house capabilities to an ecosystem. Contributions may consist of customer contacts, products, or provision of channels to customers. Within the framework of the principles and rules, an actor has the opportunity to innovate and design.

| Dimension | Selected manifestation |
|---|---|
| Strategic focus | **Exploit** |
| Customer interaction | Human led → **Generic hybrid** |
| Customer relationships | Point-to-point → **Hub and spoke** |
| Capabilities (IT, data, infrastructure, AI) | **Expanding capabilities** |
| Product and service portfolio | **Targeted products/services** |
| Organizational design | **Business unit and product structure** |
| Competitive strategy | **Red ocean** |
| Roles in the ecosystem | **Actor** |

**2. Focused challenger**
*Description*: design of a digital ecosystem initiated by an established company to challenge the market with this offering and serve a particular market segment with targeted products. The design is customer-centered; the interaction offers a custom-tailored value proposition based on needs, together with the option to transfer the orchestrator role to another actor.

| Dimension | Selected manifestation |
|---|---|
| Strategic focus | **Explore** |
| Customer interaction | **Tailored hybrid** |
| Customer relationships | **Point-to-point** |
| Capabilities (IT, data, infrastructure, AI) | **Investments in new skills (end-to-end)** |
| Product and service portfolio | **Targeted products/services** |
| Organizational design | **Customer-centered structure** |
| Competitive strategy | **Blue ocean** |
| Roles in the ecosystem | **Initiator** |

**3. Trusted life cycle player**
*Description*: greenfield approach aiming to establish an ecosystem on the assumption of **digital first** for customer support. The life cycle of a customer or a service constitutes the basis. The ecosystem offers the appropriate (neutral) expertise as needed for a trustworthy response to customer inquiries. Data and agile internal structures help develop new and individualized products quickly.

| Dimension | Selected manifestation |
|---|---|
| Strategic focus | **Explore** |
| Customer interaction | **Digital first** |
| Customer relationships | **Decentralized** |
| Capabilities (IT, data, infrastructure, AI) | **New setup (greenfield)** |
| Product and service portfolio | **Life cycle and bundles** |
| Organizational design | **Transversal and agile design teams** |
| Competitive strategy | **Black ocean** |
| Roles in the ecosystem | **Orchestrator** |

Read as a set, the three show the coupling the "mobile" metaphor describes: black ocean ambition arrives together with greenfield capability build, transversal teams, digital-first interaction, and the orchestrator role — you cannot pick black ocean and keep a business-unit structure.

**Ping An as the empirical anchor.** The chapter cites Ping An — originally a conventional insurance company — as having "managed to evolve into an ecosystem orchestrator in 10 years," with its growth chart showing the shift in capital configuration from tangible/financial toward intellectual and ecosystem capital (market value plotted 2008 → 2013 → 2018 → 2023E → 2028E). Many currently successful ecosystem actors pursue a **balanced portfolio of EXPLORE and EXPLOIT**. See Ch 16 for the full Ping An case.

## Key Takeaways

- Configuration is eight coupled dimensions; changing one changes the others.
- EXPLOIT-only ecosystem plays cap out around 2x growth; ecosystem capital is where the >20% ROI / >40% margin / 10x price-sales figures live.
- Three named strategic manifestations — active participant, focused challenger, trusted life cycle player — give complete worked configurations to pattern-match against.
- Usually three of the six ecosystem roles carry the strategic weight; a company can hold different roles in different ecosystems at once.
- Configurations are snapshots that must be continuously re-adapted, not one-time decisions.
