# Chapter 11 — Lens 1: Design Thinking

*Pages 186–207 ("Lens #1: Design Thinking")*

## Core Idea

The design thinking mindset is "a key element and the basis for all reflections in design thinking for business growth." In the first two lenses in particular, **the focus must be on the customer alone**; expertise and industry knowledge should be of secondary importance. Thoughts on solving the customer problem should be free of prejudice, especially where clear ideas already exist about how it works today or how a solution might work in future. The lens ends with a final prototype and the requirements for one or more MVPs.

The book is explicit that this lens is a *summary*: those who want to dive deeper "are well advised to use *The Design Thinking Toolbox*," and for professionals and facilitators, "*The Design Thinking Playbook* is the first choice."

## Frameworks Introduced

**The design thinking canvas** — organized along the classic phase sequence (understand → observe → define point of view → find ideas → building prototypes → test), with a problem space and a solution space, plus framing fields.

| Field | Prompts |
|---|---|
| **Research starting point and trends** | How was the problem solved up to now? What trends and megatrends are emerging? How can the future be described with strategic foresight? |
| **Vision (prototype)** | How is the north star described? What needs are met? What are the benefits for the companies involved (in an ecosystem) and the customers? |
| **Requirements for MVP** | Which prototypes should be implemented as MVPs? What experiences/functions should be tested and validated first? What is the backlog of functions? |
| **Problem statement** | Where is the problem? |
| **HMW question** | What is the point of view that can be derived from the findings of the previous phases? |
| **Critical items** | Which critical experiences and functions are of great importance to the customer? What should be a part of a potential solution in the future? |
| **Final prototype** | Is there a problem/solution fit? Does the solution correspond to the customer needs? Is the solution implementable? Will the solution be economically successful? |
| **Description of the persona / user profile** | Who has the problem? What are the pains/gains for the customers, jobs to be done, and use cases? What are the current and future customer needs? |
| **Documentation of the prototypes with an exploration map** | What worked? What didn't work? Was the respective prototype able to meet and satisfy individual experiences and functions of the customer needs? |
| **Lean canvas** | How can the short concept be described? How do we prepare for the next design lens, including initial ideas on key figures, customer segments, early adopters? |

Repeated through the problem and solution spaces, at every phase: *What are the deeper needs of the customers? What drives them? What assumptions about the customers are confirmed? Which ones should be discarded?*

**Key questions about design thinking**, by criterion:

| Desirability | Viability | Feasibility |
|---|---|---|
| Who has the problem? How is it solved today? What tasks of the customer (jobs to be done) are to be solved? What pain and gain of the customers are known? What customer needs and emotions are relevant? What critical functions and experiences for the customer should the solution contain? How does the approach differ from known solutions? To what extent does the customer benefit match the values of the service? | How to make money with the solution? Alternatively: how can costs be saved with the solution? Where does added value for the company accrue? How can investments be justified? | What are important components for implementation? What kind of technology is needed? What skills are needed for design, building, and operation? Which systems are affected? What work processes are necessary? Which assumptions are the most risky and need to be tested? How can "fail fast, fail often" be lived without destroying market opportunity? How is success defined, measured, and communicated to stakeholders? |

## Key Concepts

- **Progression curve (research/trends/foresight).** Grasps microtrends, macrotrends, and megatrends at three levels: **basic technologies**, **innovations accelerators**, and **future scenarios**. Helps put events, life cycles, and other developments in context; later helps validate a vision prototype or a future target image; sharpens the vision and future customer needs; supports filtering important topics across the whole design cycle. Procedure: cluster related topics on the curve; combine strategic foresight, identified trends, and design thinking to build a theme map (the book's example: "The future of mobility"); bring trends to different levels to discuss impact and relevance; base visualization on reports from future observatories and trend researchers.
- **Problem statement / HMW question.** These are "the starting point and the end of the problem space." The problem statement formulates the problem succinctly in one sentence, in the form: **"How might we redesign... (what?) (for whom)... so that... (his/her need)... is satisfied?"** It is developed iteratively from WH questions (Why is it a problem? Who has a need? When does it occur? How is it solved today?). The HMW question results from the collected findings and is based on a point of view — a substantiation of the problem statement.
- **Interview for empathy.** Looks at the outlined problem from the user's point of view; in early phases the interviews help understand the *context* in which a potential user acts. Especially helpful for a business ecosystem design team to break out of previous thought patterns and shift from a product-centered or company-centered to a customer-centered attitude. Uses a **question map** (WHY / HOW / WHO / WHEN around the subject) plus **journey stages** (always / often / sporadically) to recognize patterns early. Findings sought: where a user gathered information beforehand, what information was valuable, when the purchase decision was made, how easy payment was. The book's definition: "Empathy is about listening to the echo of another person in yourself and drawing conclusions about their needs."
- **Extreme users / lead users.** Lead users often act as innovators, satisfying requirements not yet known to the mass market. Extreme users exceed the normal use limits of a product, service, or system. Both give knowledge about the leading edge of unaddressed market areas. Findings can be documented as video presentation or illustrated storytelling and shared with internal and external teams.
- **Vision prototype.** The first concept attempting to solve all identified needs and problems, with a rather distant time horizon reachable through a series of products/services. Designed to help the team overcome the "**groan zone**" — the transition from the divergent phase of problem exploration to the convergent phase of problem solution.
- **Final prototype.** Concludes the phase of problem solving; should be elegant and simple at the same time. "One principle for the final prototype is: The simpler the functionality, the better." It must contain elements from the critical items diagram. "Ultimately, only reality will produce the truth. The customer will either love the solution or else lose interest fast."
- **Lean canvas over business model canvas.** "The lean canvas is far more suitable for the transition toward the minimum viable product... because it is relatively less inside-oriented than the business model canvas." Early focus is on fields I–V (problem, customer segments, value proposition, solution, existing alternatives) to review problem/solution fit; iterate in those five until a stable image emerges, then complete the rest in any order.

## Mental Models

- **Prototype resolution follows purpose.** "Low-resolution prototypes focus on the insights with respect to needs, practicality, and functionality and are mostly used in the divergent phase. High-resolution prototypes concentrate on feasibility and profitability."
- **Prototyping is experiment design.** "The procedure is primarily about designing an experiment for the user to learn something from the interaction for improving the prototype." Before prototyping, ask what type of insights you want and why you want to do an experiment; formulate the assumptions to be tested and decide how the experiment is carried out.
- **Creativity is a team sport.** "In a session of free association of thoughts and the brainstorming of many people, a random thought may lead to the best solution ideas."
- **No fishing expeditions.** "Successful business for growth initiators do not simply create new products in a fishing expedition. They think, very specifically, about which value proposition will trigger a great response from customers."
- **Elegance is emergent.** "When implementing the final prototype, elegance will show up on its own — in thought and in its realization."

## Anti-patterns

- Thinking in solutions before developing awareness of the customer's problem ("Too often, people think in solutions").
- Letting expertise and industry knowledge dominate lenses #1 and #2.
- Overfulfillment of needs — one of the things the final prototype exists to avoid.
- Validating individual features and calling it an MVP (see Ch 12).
- Losing the core team between lenses: "The core team should accompany the next phase so no losses in knowledge transfer are incurred."

**Brainstorming rules** (the book's own set): quantity before quality · creative confidence · use gestures · visual ideas · building on the ideas of others · always, only one person speaks at a time · no prejudices · continue with the brainstorming · fail often and early on.
Special forms named: **super heroes** (How would Steve Jobs solve the problem?) and **analogies/benchmarks** (How would Amazon solve the problem?).

**Transition from design thinking to lean start-up — final prototype vs MVP:**

| Dimension | #1 Final prototype | #2 Minimum viable product |
|---|---|---|
| **Purpose** | Mainly testing feasibility and desirability of the prototype of a solution to be integrated in an ecosystem | Validation at all levels of the design lenses (feasibility, desirability, viability) with as little effort as possible and a maximum of learning |
| **Focus** | Presentation to decision-making bodies and stakeholders, e.g. for funding the next phase | Good enough for market launch and interaction with real customers |
| **Features** | Various; among other things rejected via MVP as part of validation | Basic functions, functional elements, and experiences |
| **Target group** | Small audience of stakeholders and decision makers | Larger group of initial (test) customers and potential actors in the ecosystem |
| **Legacy** | Discarded after the final presentation and definition of MVP requirements | First version of a marketable solution |
| **Type of feedback** | Feedback on the concept, idea, and experience for the customer | Feedback on individual functions and experiences or a chain of experiences from different MVPs |
| **Design** | Mock-ups, wireframes | Product functionality, features, and experiences |
| **Customer benefit** | Shows a *potential* value proposition | Delivers a *true/validated* value proposition |
| **Creation time / risk** | Business case and product untested; low funds; future risks unknown; current risk low | Business case and product/features validated; sufficient financing; moderate risks |
| **Tests** | Customer and market needs | Test of the solution and relevant functions; checking willingness to spend money |
| **Revenue** | Normally no revenue from the prototype | Possible initial income from interaction with early adopters as (test) customers |

## Worked Example

**Building the persona / user profile canvas and the critical items diagram** — the two tools whose outputs the ecosystem work depends on most directly.

**Persona / user profile canvas — eight areas, in the book's order:**

1. **Describe the persona as exactly as possible** — name, age, gender, and basic demographic data, plus profession and hobbies. "A persona should be described as accurately as possible."
2. **Explain the persona's task to be performed** (jobs to be done / customer tasks).
3. **Describe use cases in the context of the problem statement.** Typical questions: Where? What? How?
4. **Description of pains** — challenges or existing problems (problems/frustration/pains).
5. **Description of gains** — opportunities or benefits that exist, e.g. due to life situations or social status (pleasure/gains).
6. **Creation of a sketch or picture of the persona** (mood board or sketch of the persona).
7. **Exploring the persona's surroundings.** Typical question: Who influences the persona? (influencers)
8. **Which trends exist that might affect the persona?** (applications, trends)

What it achieves: create a fictional character who is a potential customer; create a picture of the potential customer shared by everybody on the team; visualize the goals, desires, and needs of a typical customer; come to a consistent understanding of a target group. It "helps to get away from traditional customer segmentation and explore the real needs of and tasks to be performed for potential customers."

**Critical items diagram — the five-step procedure with its exact counts:**

1. At the beginning, ponder the question: **"What is crucial for a successful solution to the problem?"** — based on the findings from the understand and observe phases.
2. Sketch a critical items diagram on the whiteboard or a large sheet of paper and discuss on the team **which experiences the user must have / which functions are crucial for the user**.
3. **Each team member writes the eight elements that are crucial for them on Post-its.**
4. **Each member names four experiences and four functions, one of which focuses on completely new or future expectations.**
5. **Consolidate the results and agree on the team about eight crucial elements.** On this basis, define "How might we..." questions interesting enough to launch the ideate phase successfully.

What the diagram is for: appraise the results from the understand and observe phases and filter out the crucial elements; prepare the ideate and prototype phases and later the realize-MVP phase; figure out what is essential to the project and agree on what might be important to success. Its elements can describe either **the experience a user expects the solution to provide** or **an expected function**, and they should be questioned after each iteration — though "some will necessarily have relevance to a critical experience or critical function all the way to the final prototype/MVP."

Its downstream role — the reason it matters beyond lens #1: "The contents from the critical items diagram are highly relevant to the creation of prototypes, MVPs all the way to the realization of MVEs." It is also "especially useful for defining the functions and experiences for an MVP" and "serves as a backlog in the lean start-up phase for further features that expand the MVPs or the entire value proposition for the offer in the ecosystem."

**Requirements for the MVP — how the three inputs combine.** The book's routing: the **critical items diagram** (p. 196), the **lean canvas** (p. 202), and the **final prototype** (p. 201) all feed **requirements for MVP**, which then feeds MVP → MMF → backlog:
- **MVP**: test value and benefits to the customer.
- **MMF**: test marketable offering.
- **Backlog**: list of future features and experiences.

The requirements list should bring all relevant information for the validations into topical and chronological order; alongside features, functions, and experiences it should include elements dealing with **the integration of third-party systems**, and begin initial deliberations about how the future enterprise architecture and system architecture might look, including relevant components, products, and interfaces in a future business ecosystem. Requirements can be itemized on lists, described, or laid down in initial sketches and wireframes — "It is crucial that the teams will have a clear picture in the next phase of WHAT is to be implemented."

## Key Takeaways

- Lens #1's job: customer alone, no industry expertise, prototypes of rising resolution, ending in a final prototype and MVP requirements.
- The critical items diagram is the single most load-bearing tool in this chapter for ecosystem work — 8 elements, 4 experiences + 4 functions per person, one of them future-facing; it becomes the MVP definition and later the MVE backlog.
- Problem statement template: "How might we redesign (what) (for whom) so that (need) is satisfied?"
- Lean canvas beats business model canvas here because it is less inside-oriented; iterate fields I–V first.
- The final prototype is *discarded* after MVP requirements are defined — it is a decision artifact, not a product.
