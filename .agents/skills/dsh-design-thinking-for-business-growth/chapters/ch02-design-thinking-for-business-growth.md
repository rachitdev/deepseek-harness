# Chapter 2 — Design Thinking for Business Growth

*Pages 32–56 ("Introduction: Design Thinking for Business Growth — Why? How? What?")*

## Core Idea

Design thinking for business growth "defines how a single company or all companies in a business ecosystem **create, deliver, and capture** market values in common." It is the next logical step in the evolution of design in a business context: design thinking and systems thinking (from ~1960 and ~2000, aimed at organizations, systems, platforms) extend into **business ecosystem design** (from ~2020, aimed at cross-industry systems, value streams, and multidimensional business models). The expansion is primarily about the multidimensional nature of business models and the design of complete ecosystems, and it changes both the scope of development and the strategic focus.

## Frameworks Introduced

**The four key questions (WHAT / HOW / WHO / HOW, plus a retrospective WHY).**
- WHAT are the customer needs?
- HOW to build and test the experiences and features for a core value proposition?
- WHO are the appropriate ecosystem actors to jointly offer the proposition?
- HOW to design, implement, and leverage business ecosystems for growth?
- (retrospectively) WHY do some ecosystems thrive and others wither over time?

**Business model design question set** — five questions arranged around the model:
- **WHO** — Who is the customer?
- **WHAT** — What is offered to the customer? (value proposition)
- **WHERE** — Where is the customer problem / ecosystem topic area?
- **HOW** — How is the system configured? How are channels and ecosystem provisioned?
- **WHY** — Why is the system profitable? (value streams)

These map directly onto the "where to play" and "how to win and configure" framework (Ch 6–7).

**Three approaches to business models, compared:**

| Example | Methodology / mindset | Objective |
|---|---|---|
| Business model development, value proposition design | Design thinking | Optimizing existing business models with a strong focus on the **HOW** |
| Lean canvas | Design thinking + lean | Creating new business models with a strong focus on the **WHY** |
| Multidimensional creation of business models | Design thinking + systems thinking + ecosystem design | Disrupting entire industries and creating multiple business models for all participating actors, focused on **WHERE TO PLAY** and **HOW TO WIN AND CONFIGURE** |

**The three faces of design thinking** the book keeps distinct: design thinking as a **mindset**, as a **toolbox**, and as a **culture**. For growth, the toolbox is expanded with lean start-up methods plus new tools from minimum viable ecosystem landscapes through to MVEs; the mindset is expanded by three additional design lenses; and culture remains "the greatest challenge," since it brings design teams closer to the market while turning the actors into co-innovators.

## Key Concepts

- **The Sweet Spot** — the intersection where potential for new business models, value propositions, and ecosystems sits. COVID-19 is cited as the proof that customer needs can change without notice.
- **The Hype about business ecosystems.** Two design/implementation questions face every company: initialize a suitable ecosystem alone, or become part of an existing one? Either way you participate in value creation. The ecosystem approach also lets you *test different market opportunities* by participating in several ecosystems — impossible under a centralized approach with finite resources.
- **Prerequisites for participation**: high trust in other actors; common values, ideas, and ambitions; financial resources from basic idea through scaling (technologies and interfaces, but mainly setup and coordination costs). The big challenge, though, is shaping the corporate culture toward a participative mindset and saying goodbye to one-sided ownership pretensions: "The customer does not belong to a specific company but moves freely in the ecosystem."
- **720-degree view of the customer.** The book uses 720° (not 360°) for the holistic, cross-actor view of the customer that ecosystems integrate from data provided by individual actors. Distributed databases, ledgers, and blockchain are named as the technologies that will do this decentrally with a new view of data sovereignty.
- **Ecosystem journey.** The radically newly designed customer experience chain that integrates the services of several companies.
- **Opti-channel.** Large, powerful ecosystems serve customers through the channel most suitable to them (see Ch 14 for the multi-/omni-/opti-channel evolution).
- **Invincible companies** (Osterwalder) — organizations that constantly reinvent themselves, exploring the future (EXPLORE) while exploiting market position (EXPLOIT) with a balanced culture of innovative strength and implementation power.
- **What a business ecosystem is *not*.** Not every collaboration, partnership, or sourcing of partial services is an ecosystem. Central instances controlled by one initiator, and the countless platforms and marketplaces of the last two decades, are frequently mislabeled. Next-generation ecosystems must open up to the outside, be transparent about skills and values, and accept new market roles.

## Mental Models

- **Two paradigm shifts, precisely stated.**
  - *Business models*: from "What is the business model of the company to be successful on the market by means of differentiation?" (differentiation, one revenue model, individual capabilities, possession of the customer interface) to "How can every single actor who contributes his skills to the creation of a unique value proposition profit in multiple ways?" (contribution to a joint proposition, various possibilities of participation, shared capabilities, participation with the right skills).
  - *Growth*: from "How can a business model be expanded with an ecosystem play?" (linear view: business model → optimization lever → partner and business network → limited value creation) to "How can a business ecosystem benefit the growth of one company?" (business ecosystem → network multiplier → value proposition, multidimensional business model → potential for exponential growth).
- **The 20/70 capability rule of thumb.** About 20% of a company's core capabilities contribute around 70% of the value added; conversely, up to 20% of a company's capabilities need to be adapted to exploit the new 70% of first-time value creation in an ecosystem play.
- **Advanced orchestrators aim to become invisible** to customers, focusing on emotional and powerful propositions (e.g. nudging customers into better financial habits) rather than selling financial products unrelated to real needs.

## Anti-patterns

- Calling any two-company collaboration or sourcing arrangement an "ecosystem" because it sounds up-to-date and innovative.
- Everyone wanting to be initiator and orchestrator: studies show a majority of companies searching for new growth paths would like that role — clearly visible in insurers and banks, nearly all of which initialize housing (renting, buying, furnishing) or wealth-and-security ecosystems in order not to lose the customer interface.
- Augmenting existing linear business models with well-known partners under sourcing-strategy logic rather than a portfolio-based business model view.
- Top management unwilling to change, so existing models are protected and short-term financial optimization beats viable systems.
- Assuming the black ocean position is permanent — Uber went from over 80% market share to around 50% by 2020 in its main segment.

## Reference Tables

**EXPLOIT vs EXPLORE** (after A. Osterwalder et al., *The Invincible Company*, 2020):

| Dimension | EXPLOIT | EXPLORE |
|---|---|---|
| Focus | Efficiency and growth | Exploration of new things and radicalness |
| Uncertainty | Low | High |
| View of finances | Safe haven with steady returns and dividends | Risk-happy venture capital style with exponential earnings expectations |
| Culture and processes | Linear, projectable and predictable; low risk of failure | Fast and iterative, with fast learning cycles and adaptations arising from failures |
| Leadership, workflow, skills | Management focused on organization and planning, efficient processes to save time and resources | Management that focuses on curiosity, sees uncertainty as opportunity, discerns patterns, navigates between overall picture and details |

**Red / Blue / Black Ocean:**

| | Red ocean | Blue ocean | Black ocean |
|---|---|---|---|
| Market | Competition in the existing market | Creating new markets | Creating completely new market areas |
| Stance | Beating competitors | Avoiding competitors | Realizing a unique value proposition together with other actors (including competitors) |
| Demand | Exploiting existing demand | Tapping new demand | Solving previously unaddressed customer problems and needs |
| Benefit/cost | Direct link between benefits and costs | Undoing the direct link | Establishing new value streams, business models, and growth opportunities |
| System alignment | To the strategic decision for differentiation *or* low cost | To differentiation *and* low cost | To co-creation, coevolution, and win-win for all actors |

## Worked Example

**Uber and Didi as black ocean players** — the chapter's own worked case (pp. 52–53).

Uber's black ocean position and its erosion:
- Uber at times held **over 80%** of the market in its area; by **2020** it held roughly **50%** of its main market segment.
- The book's reading: this shows how crucial it is to orchestrate continuous advancement of the value proposition over time and set the right course as part of business ecosystem leadership.
- Uber's response was to introduce new subsegments aimed at exploiting new customer needs: **UberX, UberPool, UberEats, UberMoto**, all added over roughly ten years, plus large-scale investment in technologies (e.g. self-driving cars) some of which would completely disrupt the current business model.
- The mechanism is co-creation: innovations and ideas coming from actors in the system, and close cooperation with vehicle manufacturers and other technology platforms.

Didi's contrasting incentive design:
- The Chinese competitor Didi, as a mobility service provider, offers its actors in the system **many car-related services** — ranging from **discounts on gas to discounts on new vehicles**.
- The book's point: a black ocean strategy needs well-thought-out systems that give the various actors *multiple incentives* to cooperate with the ecosystem. Incentives, not just a proposition, hold the actor side together.

Named black ocean pairs by domain (companies with no more than three perceptible competitors):
- E-commerce: **Amazon and Alibaba**
- Mobility: **Uber and Didi**
- Mobile operating systems: **Google and Apple**
- Payments: **Alipay and WeChat Pay** (detailed on pp. 300–305; see Ch 16)

## Key Takeaways

- Design thinking for business growth is defined by *create, deliver, and capture value in common* — the interaction of the three and the timing of focus on each drives value growth speed and later scaling options.
- The two paradigm shifts (multidimensional business models; ecosystem-driven growth) are the mechanics behind mindshifts #8 and #9.
- Black ocean is an ambition and a configuration, not a permanent position; Uber's decline from >80% to ~50% is the book's own cautionary number.
- The 20/70 rule sizes the capability adaptation an ecosystem play demands.
- Refuse the label "ecosystem" for centrally-controlled platforms and sourcing partnerships; the test is opening up, transparency about skills and values, and acceptance of new market roles.
