# Chapter 9 — New Ways of Working

*Pages 163–171 ("New Ways of Working")*

## Core Idea

Participating in a business ecosystem changes how work is done, not only what is worked on. Ecosystems provide access to talent and expertise regardless of whether it sits at a technology supplier, research institution, start-up, or perceived competitor — because "the resources of companies are limited, and not all (new) capabilities can be developed within the company." The chapter's central claim about the future: "The future way of working combines traditional roles and functions with technologies to exploit significant gains in productivity and efficiency" — human and technology as a *team*, with each side's strengths exploited.

## Frameworks Introduced

**Future of work — three levels of transformation:**

| Level | Content |
|---|---|
| **Work** | The work actually carried out, supported by contemporary technology to achieve the desired goals and create value for the ecosystem initiative |
| **Workforce** | A combination of different skills and talents — not only permanent employees but freelancers, gig workers, crowd sourcing teams, and interdisciplinary teams working together, e.g. in co-creation on the relevant ecosystem initiatives |
| **Workplace** | Where the work is done and teams collaborate — both the physical location and the technologies used for digital collaboration; location and mode of collaboration characterize the culture of "future of work" |

Key questions about future of work: Who can do the work? Which work steps can be automated? Which technologies help in the collaboration of human beings and machines as a team? Where is the work conducted? (with virtual collaboration explicitly named).

**View of VUCA worlds** — a side-by-side of the typical vertically integrated company and the typical business ecosystem player, mediated by the four VUCA dimensions:

| VUCA dimension | Vertically integrated company | | Business ecosystem player |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Volatility** (changeable, erratic) | **Profit** — profit maximization, employees as a cost factor | → | **Purpose** — meaning in vision and value proposition, ecosystem orientation |
| **Uncertainty** (lack of clarity) | **Hierarchies** — traditional authoritarian and direct management relationships | → | **Networks** — team of teams, human centered, digitized |
| **Complexity** (interactions) | **Control** — bureaucratization, standardization, centralization | → | **Ability** — strengthening of customer-facing employees (decentralization) |
| **Complexity** (interactions) | **Planning** — efficiency optimization, budgeting, strategic costing | → | **Experiments** — agile methods, design of MVPs, MVEs |
| **Ambiguity** (misunderstandings) | **Confidentiality** — information as an imbalance in power, company secrets | → | **Transparency** — open workflow and knowledge sharing |

**Four types of company, by when and how they open up** — arranged on two axes (unipolar/bipolar ↔ multipolar; autocratic-hierarchical ↔ evolutionary-cooperative):

| Type | Description | + | – |
|---|---|---|---|
| **The cooperatives** | Companies that either have an idea to be discussed with other actors at an early stage or rely on collaboration with others right from the onset to define a shared system | Strong focus on co-creation and development of radically new ecosystems | Collaboration and co-creation are more complex and time consuming |
| **The exploratives** | Companies that not only have a strategy but experiment with various ecosystem approaches and look for opportunities to play an active part in other ecosystems | Market opportunities can be exploited ad hoc | High level of effort and expense for coordinating multiple talks with many actors |
| **The seekers** | Companies with a clear idea of where market opportunities lie for them; actively seek out other actors to build ecosystems on their own or contribute capabilities in specific constellations | Clear vision of the target image and the implementation | Little room for new ideas and changed value propositions at a later stage |
| **The mysterious** | Companies that play their cards close to the chest; strategy discussed at top management level, prototypes and MVPs developed behind locked doors; information shared with potential actors only once the ecosystem has been implemented | Low risk of being copied by other actors | Late value proposition validation |

**Five co-creation approaches** — forms of collaboration, each with its goal and the behavior it requires:

| Form of collaboration | Goal | Behavior |
|---|---|---|
| **Conduct research** | Learn and understand | Be curious, observe, and understand the needs of customers; explore facts and dig deeper by asking WH questions (Who? What? When? Where? Why? How?) |
| **Open** | Develop and grow | Discard assumptions and prejudices; consciously search for other experiences and new perspectives; high willingness to change one's opinion due to new insights |
| **Integrative** | Be appreciative and act in a solution-oriented way | Avoid any unbalanced compromises when searching for win-win situations for all actors; creative reframing and use of different worldviews as an advantage for dynamics of change |
| **Collaborative** | Team work and team of teams | Listen actively and build on the ideas of others; collaboration that aims at strengthening social cohesion |
| **Reflective** | Be prudent and develop spirally | Reflect on actions and effectiveness; view challenges and problems in a wider context; mediate in situations where different views must be reconciled |

The five run along an **analytical procedure** axis, from research at one end to reflection at the other.

## Key Concepts

- **Impact on workforce skills.** A company's decision to enter an ecosystem play has direct impact on necessary workforce skills. Most important is the team's ability to **adapt quickly**, to see changes as an opportunity, and to accept the complexity inherent in designing and participating in ecosystems. The transformation expands tasks not only for the ecosystem leadership team but for all employees involved.
- **The human-technology pairing.** "Human beings and technologies work best together when the strengths of both are exploited. And this mutual relationship is needed to ascertain new customer needs with empathy, to use human curiosity and imagination and the technology to boost efficiency, and realize exponential growth. The challenge consists of designing the work in such a way that ensures the success of people and machines."
- **Co-creation timing varies.** Starting points for ecosystem considerations vary: some companies rely on cooperation and openness from the very onset, others discuss the topic internally and draft first prototypes and MVPs before opening.
- **Co-creation spans the whole cycle.** It is a key element in solving design challenges, constructing first prototypes and MVPs, designing business ecosystems, and scaling — because the actors in the system are seen as **innovators who are close to the respective customer needs**.
- **Learning organization.** In a world of disruption, change, and transformation — VUCA — every organization searches for new ways of learning and becomes a learning organization. The respective design lenses help get a grip on uncertainty, make complexity tangible and mappable, and test assumptions iteratively and with agility.
- **Automation reshapes sequences.** Various automation technologies work hand in hand with the teams, completing tasks faster. "The new way human beings and technology work together as a team allows work sequences to be redefined."
- **Employees remain decisive.** "Employees will remain a decisive factor in work in the future since they take on ever more complex tasks and leave repetitive, mundane tasks to machines and automation."

## Mental Models

- **Why a business ecosystem is a talent strategy.** Through participation, new ways of working policies can be implemented that generate new knowledge in a cooperative way of working, obtain problem-solving skills, and make use of skills in technology — regardless of where in the ecosystem those skills sit.
- **The VUCA read-across.** Each VUCA symptom has a paired ecosystem-native answer: volatility answered by purpose, uncertainty by networks, complexity by decentralized ability and by experiments, ambiguity by transparency.
- **Dynamic, not static, situational awareness.** VUCA is a vital element in this context because the system is rarely static: it is dynamic and changes very quickly and irregularly in particular phases. Since ecosystems are complex and networked, "a complete and static view is not possible" — and because the situation is described and evaluated differently depending on the actor or person, ambiguity is structurally guaranteed.

## Anti-patterns

- Traditionally managed companies refusing new collaborative ways of working while still expecting contextualization in conventional strategy tools — the chapter names this as *the* challenge such companies face.
- Deferring all opening-up (the "mysterious" pattern) and thereby paying with late value proposition validation.
- Over-committing to a fixed target image early (the "seekers" pattern) and leaving little room for new ideas and changed value propositions later.
- Treating complexity as something to be removed rather than accepted: the required team ability is explicitly "to accept the complexity that lies in the design and participation in business ecosystems."

## Worked Example

**Choosing a co-creation posture and form for an ecosystem initiative** — the chapter's frameworks applied as the book presents them for use.

*Step 1 — Locate your company among the four opening-up types.* The book's stated observation is that "four approaches to opening up can be observed. They all have advantages and disadvantages," and it positions them on two axes so that the diagonal opposites are the real trade-off pairs:
- **The mysterious** (autocratic/hierarchical, unipolar-bipolar) versus **the cooperatives** (evolutionary/cooperative, multipolar) — the maximum-secrecy versus maximum-openness diagonal.
- **The seekers** (unipolar-bipolar, evolutionary-cooperative) versus **the exploratives** (autocratic-hierarchical, multipolar) — the focused-target-image versus portfolio-of-bets diagonal.

*Step 2 — Recognize that the choice sets your validation timing.* Each cell's minus sign is a validation cost:
- Mysterious → "Late value proposition validation."
- Seekers → "Little room for new ideas and changed value propositions at a later stage."
- Exploratives → "High level of effort and expense for the coordination of multiple talks with many actors."
- Cooperatives → "Collaboration and co-creation are more complex and time consuming."

*Step 3 — Select the form of collaboration for the phase you are in.* The five co-creation approaches are ordered along an analytical procedure. In practice this maps to the design lenses: **conduct research** during design thinking (be curious, observe, dig with WH questions); **open** when moving between lenses (discard assumptions and prejudices); **integrative** during ecosystem design, where the win-win must hold for every actor ("avoid any unbalanced compromises when searching for win-win situations for all actors"); **collaborative** during MVE realization (listen actively, build on others' ideas, strengthen social cohesion); **reflective** at each transition (reflect on actions and effectiveness, view problems in a wider context, mediate reconciliations).

*Step 4 — Set the VUCA-response targets.* For each of the five contrasts in the VUCA table, name where the initiative currently sits and where it must be: profit→purpose, hierarchies→networks, control→ability, planning→experiments, confidentiality→transparency. The book's framing is that a business ecosystem player is defined by holding the right-hand column, and that these are what the design lenses deliver — "the respective design lenses help to get a grip on uncertainty, make complexity tangible and mappable and test assumptions iteratively and with agility."

The chapter's summary of what all four steps are for: "For the design of and participation in business ecosystems, certain values, mindsets, and ideas as well as open and transparent collaboration with other actors are vital."

## Key Takeaways

- Future of work has three levels — work, workforce, workplace — and the workforce level explicitly includes freelancers, gig workers, and crowd-sourced and co-creation teams.
- VUCA is not rhetoric here; each dimension is paired with a specific ecosystem-native answer.
- Four opening-up postures (cooperatives, exploratives, seekers, mysterious), each with a named advantage and a named cost — the cost is always about validation timing or coordination load.
- Five co-creation forms — research, open, integrative, collaborative, reflective — each with its own required behavior.
- Ecosystem participation is a way to reach talent and capability you cannot build in-house; that is a stated reason to join one.
