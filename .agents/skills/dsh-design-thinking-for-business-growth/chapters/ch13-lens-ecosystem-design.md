# Chapter 13 — Lens 3: Ecosystem Design

*Pages 228–269 ("Lens #3: Ecosystem Design" — Procedural Model Intro; Ecosystem Design tools)*

## Core Idea

This is the book's own discipline. "Business ecosystem design has become a discipline in itself," first presented in *The Design Thinking Playbook* (2018) and elaborated here into a **four-loop, ten-step procedural model** whose output is the **minimum viable ecosystem (MVE)**.

The governing principle: **maximum learning with the smallest number of actors per role in the system**. "Viable" means there is an actor for each role so the system can be tested. Since even simple ecosystems can be very complex, the advice is to break the overall system into subsystems and map the relationships between actors, so a shared value proposition can be created, tested, measured, and improved iteratively with a minimum number of necessary actors.

## Frameworks Introduced

**The four loops and ten steps.** Design takes place on three levels — **customers, business, and technology**.

| Loop | Steps | Content |
|---|---|---|
| **Virtuous design loop** | 1–7 | 1. Formulation of the key value proposition · 2. Brainstorming to select and describe the actors · 3. Allocation of the actors on the ecosystem map · 4. Definition of the value streams and links between actors · 5. Elaboration of the benefits and disadvantages for each individual actor · 6. Multidimensional view of the business models · 7. (Re)design of the business ecosystem |
| **Validation loop** | 8 | Looking at the collaboration between the actors; view of the decision makers and teams per actor |
| **Realization loop** | 9 | Formation of a team to implement the MVE |
| **Experiment loop** | 10 | Implementation of the final MVE; testing the ecosystem on the basis of one or several MVPs |

Starting point: **validated customer needs and MVP**. "It is not meant to be a blueprint for a procedure. Instead, it is a suggestion to map the most important elements for achieving an MVE."

**Ecosystem design canvas** — the documentation instrument for the loops:

| Field | Prompts |
|---|---|
| **Determine the needs of the users/customers** | Who is the customer or user? Describe the customer/user profile (pains, gains, jobs to be done, use case). What problem is to be solved? |
| **Core value proposition** | What is the core value proposition for the user/customer? |
| **Definition of the value streams** | What are the current and future (positive and negative) value streams? Which product/service streams, money/credit streams, data and information flow? What are the digital and digitizing value streams/assets? |
| **Describe the actors** | Who are the actors in the business ecosystem? What is their function and role in the system? How high is their motivation to participate? |
| **Design / redesign** | *Design*: Which actors are pivotal for providing the core value proposition? (For placement, go from the inside to the outside.) Also place actors with advanced and complementary offerings, enabling functions, and others directly or indirectly part of the system. *Redesign*: Do various scenarios exist with different actors? Which actors can be eliminated? Are there actors who scale multidimensional value streams or better value streams? Is the ecosystem robust and able to survive in the new scenario? |
| **Prototype, test, and improve the business ecosystem** | What are the MMFs/MVPs the ecosystem starts with? How can the first MVE be tested? What interactions/testing and measurement methods help us improve the value streams, business models, and the role of actors iteratively? What is the minimum version of the first MVE? |
| **Analysis of the advantages and disadvantages of each actor** | What are the advantages and disadvantages for each actor? What are their strengths/weaknesses and opportunities/risks in the system? |
| **Multidimensional view of the business models** | What does the resultant business model and value proposition for each actor look like? How does the respective business model contribute to the core value proposition? Is the defined core value proposition the result of the sum of the value propositions of all actors? |

**Key questions about ecosystem design**, by criterion:

| Adaptability | Value enhanceability | Feasibility |
|---|---|---|
| How can it be ensured that the other actors support the solution? How can lasting value be created (e.g. first-mover, lock-in, exclusiveness)? How can other actors develop innovations and integrate them? How are the actors selected, and what are the requirements in terms of skills, quality, technology, and handling the customer interface? How is data on customers and offerings shared with the other actors? | What is one's own business model and that of the other actors? Which downstream value streams exist? How can values be created together in the ecosystem? What does the ecosystem economy look like? How can compliance with provisions and rules be ensured? How can network and ecosystem effects be used for exponential growth? | Which additional technologies are needed for integration with and interaction between actors? Which system architecture will support digital interaction? Which partnerships and suppliers should be built out and exploited? Are the necessary skills and assets available or can they be provided by other actors/suppliers, e.g. to develop, launch, and scale a technology platform? |

**Customer journey → ecosystem journey.** The shift the lens forces:

| Dimension | Customer journey | Ecosystem customer journey |
|---|---|---|
| **Touch points** | Customer touch points with a company | Mapping all connections and interactions in an ecosystem |
| **Purpose** | Improve existing interactions via different touch points — inform, order, pay, deliver, install, handle warranty, dispose | Design a holistic experience for all actors in the system who together provide a unique value proposition |
| **Touch points (mode)** | Interaction of customer with a company and its offer via different channels | Interaction of customer with the ecosystem via different channels and in different configurations of the customer interfaces across the ecosystem |
| **Application** | Optimization of existing interactions via data analytics, observation, A/B testing | Radically new approaches, usually with high automation and mass customization for recurring interaction; easy integration of offerings from the actors |
| **Roles** | Focus on specific customer groups and segments and their needs in the customer experience chain | Focus on customer groups and segments **as well as actors, suppliers, and other players** active in the ecosystem |

## Key Concepts

- **Core value proposition definition.** "A core value proposition is a concise explanation of the functional and emotional benefits offered by the ecosystem as a whole. The focus is clearly on solving a customer problem or satisfying a customer need. It is **not** a positioning in the form of self-presentation and underscoring distinguishing features." It "is like a contract between the customer and all actors in the business ecosystem."
- **The messaging hierarchy.** Tagline = the big idea → value proposition = what supports the big idea → **brand messaging pillars** = the stories told to the various actors in the system to win them over so they participate and support the value proposition.
- **Value streams, in detail.** Simple ecosystems in traditional businesses manage with physical product/service flows, money/credit flows, and information. For digital and digitized value streams, **intangible values** are highly relevant: knowledge, software, data, design, music, media, addresses, virtual environments, cryptocurrencies, tokens, or access and transfer of ownership and possession. These are increasingly decentralized and exchanged directly between actors. **Negative value streams exist** — e.g. through a transfer of risk.
- **Ecosystem capital, restated.** "The combination of different value streams in a business ecosystem allows for mapping value creation as a dynamic and networked exchange in which, theoretically, every actor maintains a connection with one another... **This wealth of relationships is the ecosystem capital.**" Compare: incremental value creation on cost-plus to cover ROI, versus dynamic and networked value creation generating ROI, ecosystem capital, and intellectual capital from the value streams.
- **Governance passes from initiator to orchestrator.** "While the initiator moves more and more to the background of the activities, responsibility is increasingly passed on to the orchestrator."
- **Inter-specific relationships.** In the validation loop, the "so-called inter-specific relationship between participating individuals and teams ensures the existence of a business ecosystem." This involves understanding the personal interests, needs, and motivations of those involved. "Alongside the rational decision to be part of the ecosystem, **personal motivation (e.g. of a decision maker) is at least as relevant.**"
- **Two entry approaches to redesign.** Design radically new ecosystems (certain actors can be eliminated as early as in the run-up), or first create the ecosystem predominant today and optimize it in an iteration. "Especially if existing business ecosystems are to be radically restructured, the second approach makes sense since processes, procedures, information, and value streams can be redefined."

## Mental Models

- **A simple rule for actor incentives.** "Without an advantage or a corresponding financial incentive, actors will not be enthused about the system!" Named benefits an actor may seek: benefiting from innovation · heightened efficiency · better analytics and data quality · quicker scaling · part of a new value proposition · utilization of new technologies · access to data and intellectual property (IP) · better access to resources and talent · new touch points and new customer access · access to existing key customers.
- **The more options, the more attractive.** "The more options there are for all actors and the better thought-out the options are for extracting added value from the business system through positive value streams, the more attractive the ecosystem is for all those involved."
- **Redesign generates ideas, not just improvements.** "Dealing with different constellations in the virtuous loop brings new ideas and business model approaches that help to amplify the uniqueness or find new pathways for expanding the value proposition." New findings can be tested as MVP within an initial MVE or parked in the backlog.
- **Robustness through iteration.** "Most of all, it is important to prove the robustness of the scenarios by means of iterations and experiments."

## Anti-patterns

**The most common design errors in the initialization of business ecosystems:**
- Insufficient focus on new or changed customer needs
- Weak core value proposition
- Wrong configuration of the business ecosystem
- Lack of governance structures and skills in orchestrating business ecosystems
- Wrong business model for the objectives of the business ecosystem
- Too little commitment in the multidimensional view of business models
- Sluggish implementation of the ecosystem initiative and wrong leadership approaches in the scale-up phase

**The headline statistic:** "According to a survey conducted by the Henderson Institute (2020), the main cause for the failure of business ecosystems (**85%**) is a weak business ecosystem design; only **15%** of the initiatives examined came to naught due to implementation errors."

**How to avoid the most frequent design errors:** start with design thinking and a customer problem; realize initial MVPs and create compelling value propositions; design the business ecosystem through various loops up to the realization of an MVE; pay attention to a form of governance that fits the system and to adequate ecosystem leadership approaches.

Also named: multidimensional business modelling "is complex and asks too much of many business ecosystem design teams" — but the knowledge is "highly relevant to later considerations and the interaction with potential actors."

**Role playing procedure** (tool 3): each team member takes on the role of one of the defined actors (**assign a maximum of 12 roles in the first phase**). The value proposition is written down in the middle of the room. Actors are grouped by importance or by their direct/indirect relation to other actors and to the value proposition. Each "actor" explains their role, advantages, disadvantages, and why they took that position; positions can then change, new actors be added, or distances altered. Findings are integrated into the actor descriptions.

**Ecosystem map procedure** (tool 4): (1) cover a large table with paper so all team members can see the map; (2) divide the space by closeness to the core value proposition — circles drawn on the paper; (3) place the individual actors; (4) discuss with the team or in co-creation. **Number of circles depends on system complexity; for initial considerations two or three circles suffice.** Map variants used: current system (ACTUAL state) · greenfield approach · (re)design of ecosystems, with prototype 1 / prototype 2 / prototype 3 each carrying PRO and CON.

**VALUE STREAMS MAPPING template** (tool 5): *Value stream types* — what types are relevant (services, money, information, data, innovation/IP, intangible assets)? *Manifestation* — in what way do the value streams flow? *Typical value stream* — what does a typical value stream of a specific kind look like, between the actors, with the orchestrator, or directly with a customer? Then visualize individual value streams for subsystems or the entire ecosystem map. For complex projects the book names digital tooling (tr3dent.com) for mapping large numbers of actors and value streams.

**Revenue models for ecosystems** (tool 6), grouped:

| Cost-plus approaches (established) | Mixed models | Digital models (newer) |
|---|---|---|
| Make and sell directly; make and distribute; auction retail; franchised retail; cost-plus; time and material; cash conversion cycle; spectrum retail; brokerage; advertising | Transaction facilitator; affiliate; peer-to-peer; dynamic pricing; e-commerce/marketplace; pay as you go; freemium; subscription; multi-sided platform; product as a service; low-touch; bait and hook; pay within payment | Disintermediation; ecosystem initiators; customer data; reputation builders; dropshipping; hidden revenues; on-demand; one for one; crowd funding |

Properties of the newer models: lower and more flexible cost base · utilization of automation to attain superior operational excellence · competitive edge through connection of offers and undercutting prices via cross-subsidization · rapid learning in a trial-and-error culture · focus on customer experiences and a unique value proposition · dynamic and networked collaboration among actors · proactive elimination of intermediaries · provision of value-added digital elements of the ecosystem.

Named examples: Uber, Lyft, Airbnb → on-demand; Salesforce, Netflix → subscription; Facebook, Google → advertising and ad-supported; Alibaba, Amazon → moved from 100% e-commerce to new digital models in evolutionary steps; **Tencent** → hybrid, combining a transaction facilitator in payments (WeChat Pay), online-to-offline (O2O) business with physical and digital services, and integrated online shopping with partners (JD.com), monetizing via technology fees, premium service fees, third-party access fees, data fees, and established revenue models.

**Governance structure options for the MVE** — five paths, from earliest to most independent:
1. Ecosystem unit
2. Vertical / company branch
3. Subsidiary / affiliate
4. New venture
5. Participation of several actors in the new venture

The governance chain: *Ecosystem strategy* (right to play; digital requirements; design lenses) → *Operating model* (ecosystem governance; ecosystem configuration; team of teams; backlog functions; orchestrating the actors; skills and mindset) → *Result* (ecosystem performance KPIs; speed of growth; evolution of the ecosystem; expansion of the value proposition; growing ecosystem capital; opening up to the outside and transparency).

**Five success factors in dealing with business ecosystems:**
1. **Ecosystem awareness** — each company should see itself as part of the ecosystem and develop the ability to recognize its roles and behaviors through the eyes of other actors and from different angles.
2. **Understanding systemic possibilities** — consciously reflect on ecosystems and imagine what productive behaviors are possible for themselves and the whole ecosystem, so as to change the value streams in a targeted way.
3. **Governance of ecosystems** — the ability to work on and in the system, integrate partners (co-creation), and generate benefits for all actors.
4. **Sustainable ecosystem intelligence** — establish the ability to promote and improve long-term systems thinking and design thinking, and develop strategies and techniques for agile advancement of the ecosystem.
5. **Leadership with business ecosystem design** — build the ability to integrate systems design in the culture of the organization and to break out of existing rules consciously (black ocean).

**Challenges by dimension** (initiator/orchestrator vs whole ecosystem):

| Dimension | Challenges for the initiator and orchestrator | Challenges for the entire ecosystem |
|---|---|---|
| **First MVE** | Find the right actors for the roles; create transparency and trust; define degrees of leeway and appropriate rules | Establishing a shared culture of collaboration; recognize the strengths and weaknesses of the system |
| **Implementation of MVE** | Mastering complexity; managing uncertainty; establishing governance | Continuous improvement and optimization; establishing high entry barriers for competitors; allowing co-competition where it makes sense |
| **Leadership** | Communicate the vision clearly; respond dynamically to change; expansion and adaptation of the value proposition | Harmonization of one's own vision with the target image of the ecosystem; constant reflection on success, new requirements, and dynamics |
| **Scale** | Collaboration with the other actors; driving innovation and growth together; expand the value proposition and retain market share | Exploitation of lock-in effects; scaling through network effects; keep focus on exponential growth |

## Worked Example

**The 8 Golden Rules for an MVE** (p. 262) plus the prototyping/testing procedure — the book's own operating checklist for the experiment loop.

> **The 8 Golden Rules for an MVE**
> 1. Combine design thinking and systems thinking.
> 2. Think not only of transformation but also how the system evolves.
> 3. Find inspiration in the working of biological ecosystems.
> 4. Think at all levels: customer, relationships among actors, and technology.
> 5. Begin with considerations about the benefits for each individual actor, and use the value streams to show the actor's viability.
> 6. Accept the complexity of multiple business model considerations and assume the perspective of the other actors.
> 7. Start with the implementation of the ecosystem on the basis of the virtuous loop, validation loop, realization, and experiment loop.
> 8. Adapt the system if it doesn't work in its minimal version.

**What the MVE actually is.** "The MVE unites initial technology components with the value proposition and the designed value streams. It is far more complex than a product or service provided to the customers. Several actors are needed for the MVE, who together deliver the added value to the customers. **'Viable' means that there is an actor for each role in the system so the system can be tested.** The validation shows that it works technically and that the actors and the customers obtain the promised benefits. The MVE is proof that the system is viable, it can correspondingly grow, and in the best-case scenario, can be scaled over time."

**What the MVE lets the team do:** validate the core value proposition of the ecosystem and individual value propositions for specific actors/roles; test whether the defined value streams will generate the desired offer; test assumptions regarding skills, benefits, and willingness to cooperate on the part of the participating roles/actors; test multiple scenarios and manifestations of MVPs or combinations of MVPs to create an initial holistic ecosystem offering; test, measure, and improve the business model of the ecosystem and the assumptions on the multidimensional view of business models.

**Procedure for realizing the MVE:** it requires the relevant MVPs/MMFs — usually a selection of necessary individual components for an initial overall offer. **At least one actor and a defined number of customers should participate for each role in the system.** The goals of the MVE and the underlying assumptions must be communicated transparently. Actors are encouraged to contribute improvements and ideas at an early stage. Customers should be closely accompanied and involved in the respective improvement loops.

The build–measure–learn discipline carries across: "For MVE considerations, elements and procedures from lean start-up can be adapted to reflect on the ecosystem and improve it. Applying the same principles of build, measure, and learn as in the realization of an MVP and to complete preparations for the MVE before realization has proven to be a good thing."

**The final MVE.** "The final MVE is much more than a prototype or an experiment. It is actually the minimal implementation of a system, which then will be gradually expanded and scaled. This blueprint is proof that business ecosystem design has yielded a relevant system that is viable and generates value."

Its three-step launch procedure:
1. **Launch the MVE on the market.** Make sure the selected features and experiences have a **WOW! effect** on the customer from the very onset. Adjust the collaboration of the actors for delivery of the offer in an early phase.
2. **Utilize the momentum on the market** to continue an alternative development path in the gradual expansion of the ecosystem. Make use of the methods and tools for scaling.
3. **Exploit the full potential of all actors** for further innovation and network effects and to realize exponential growth. Adjust and expand the offer constantly and respond quickly and specifically to new or emerging customer needs.

**The named case the chapter carries.** The book's illustrated MVE example is the **"Renting" MVE for a target ecosystem of "Renting, Buying, Housing"** — a target ecosystem map on which the minimal set of actors and roles is marked, the possible orchestrator/initiator is identified, and the MVE subset is outlined against the fuller target ecosystem. The caption states the design intent exactly: "The business ecosystem designed receives the validation of the **system/actors fit** through the realization of an MVE." The same housing ecosystem returns in Ch 14 as the worked example of value proposition *expansion*.

**Multidimensional business modelling, two steps** (tool 7):
1. Think about the business model with which an actor can make money **on the basis of the defined value streams**. The lean canvas with corresponding expansions helps with the definition.
2. **Assume the view of the respective actor/role.** Ask how the actor can make *additional* money on the basis of services from the ecosystem — e.g. particular skills and capabilities, customer access, regional presence, physical locations, or know-how in certain key technologies.

The book's illustration is **Amazon B2B cloud services**: the business model of Actor 1 in the ecosystem stacks into reselling of cloud services (IaaS) · system integration services · E2E services · connectivity services · managed services — "multiple possibilities for further business models (**cumulative levers with a factor of 5 to 10**, depending on the business model)."

**Data ecosystems as a subsystem** — the expanded view (p. 257). In many ecosystems data is "the lubricant to ensure fast and agile innovation," and subsystems in the form of data ecosystems arise, comprising value streams with the ecosystem and its actors and allowing further monetization. The loop: customer data (from provision of offers · from consumption/context · from customer experience/behavior) → business ecosystem orchestrator applies **deep learning**, **refine and enrich**, interpretation, automated evaluation → **market, customer, and process insights** and usable data → adaptive and new offers, data-driven innovation → **data-based expansion of the value proposition** → data-driven ecosystem expansion → **opportunity for more business models / monetize ecosystem**. Governance constraints named on the diagram: **rules**, **Chinese walls**, **privacy and security**.

## Key Takeaways

- Four loops (virtuous design, validation, realization, experiment), ten steps, one output: the MVE.
- "Viable" is a precise threshold: one actor per role, so the system can actually run.
- 85% of ecosystem failures are design failures, not implementation failures — the strongest argument in the book for this lens.
- Actor incentives are not optional; the actor description template forces motivation, compatibility, market dominance, and current business model to be stated per actor.
- Multidimensional business modelling has two moves: what the actor earns from the defined value streams, and what *additional* models the actor can stack — the Amazon cloud example puts that at 5–10x.
- Value streams include intangibles and can be negative; map them per subsystem, not only whole-system.
- Governance can start as an ecosystem unit and evolve to a jointly owned new venture; the initiator recedes as the orchestrator takes over.
