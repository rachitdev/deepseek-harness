# Chapter 12 — Lens 2: Lean Start-Up

*Pages 208–227 ("Lens #2: Lean Start-Up")*

## Core Idea

The lean start-up lens exists to gain initial market experience with a product or service and to draw conclusions from customer feedback that advance the MVP. It iteratively tests the most key experiences and functions, allows initial pricing tests, and repeats until the MVP covers the customer needs. The book follows "the conceptual ideas of Eric Ries, Steve Blank, and Ash Maurya."

Its ecosystem-specific twist: **several MVPs are needed in preparation for the MVE**, and often various options are implemented simultaneously so appropriate conclusions can be drawn. The validated value proposition is the starting point for considerations on a functioning business ecosystem, and the MVP doubles as the artifact used to persuade potential actors.

The lens replaces "the decades-old formula in the definition of growth topics" — a linear approach of competitor analyses, addressable markets, and market segments culminating in a business plan sold to decision makers — with an approach where **agile development works hand in hand with customer development** to prove benefit and value to the customer/user from the beginning.

## Frameworks Introduced

**Lean start-up MVP canvas** — three columns: initial situation, plan, results.

| Column | Field | Prompts |
|---|---|---|
| *Initial situation* | **Persona** | For whom is this MVP? Who will test the MVP? |
| *Initial situation* | **Top 3 problems / challenges** | What is the focus of this MVP? What problems or challenges are addressed by it? |
| *Initial situation* | **Customer journey and use cases** | Which step in the customer journey / ecosystem journey, or what use cases, are improved by it? |
| *Plan* | **Vision and road map** | What is the product vision? What does the road map look like? How will we expand the width and depth of functions step by step? |
| *Plan* | **Top 3 features** | What are the top features tested in this MVP? |
| *Plan* | **MVP** | How does the MVP contribute to the vision? |
| *Plan* | **Build** | How can these features be built? |
| *Plan* | **Costs and schedule** | What are the costs, and what does the schedule look like? |
| *Results* | **Conclusion / next steps** | What are the most important insights? Does this vision or strategy have to be adapted (**pivot**)? |
| *Results* | **Learn** | What do we want to learn in the next step? What have we learned? |
| *Results* | **Measure** | How can we measure the results and validate our assumptions? |

| Viability | Desirability | Feasibility |
|---|---|---|
| Which problem is to be solved with the business model? Where do fixed and variable costs arise? What value streams yield income? **Build – Measure – Learn.** An iterative feedback loop applied until the product or service has reached the desired market maturity.
1. **Build** — after the MVP has been built with real added value for the customer (value proposition) and made available to potential customers, measure the effects.
2. **Measure** — based on two key questions: *Do potential customers accept the MVP? What improvements are desired?* From these insights a learning process is set in motion.
3. **Learn** — iterative feedback loops enable learning based on customer feedback; the main advantage is fast response to market changes.

Measurement activities named: evaluation and analysis of feedback; prioritizing the accumulated feedback; validation of assumptions.

**Innovation accounting.** Measuring innovation success is an integral part of lean start-up; it lets the team prove objectively that the right conclusions are drawn from MVP experiments. "The aim of innovation accounting is to verify the most risky business model assumptions by way of empirical testing with customers." Early simulations of sales figures can be performed with **driver trees**. The five-question set (the AARRR-style funnel):

| # | Stage | Question |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Acquisition | How do customers find us? |
| 2 | Activation | Do the customers have a first positive experience? |
| 3 | Retention | Are the customers coming back? |
| 4 | Revenue | How does the offer or function make money? |
| 5 | Referral | Do the customers recommend the offer to other people? |

**User stories / acceptance criteria.** Three elements: role/persona (for whom, in the system where the persona has the application); wish/goal (which needs are to be satisfied); measurable result (benefits). Template:

> As **&lt;role/persona&gt;** (those parts of a system/offer with specific tasks to be met), I would like to **&lt;have goal/wish/need satisfied&gt;** so that **&lt;benefit/added value, measurable output&gt;**.
> It is done if... To implement it, I., II., and III. must be done.

Acceptance criteria canvas fields: title, priority, cost estimate; WHO (as... role), WHAT (would like... goal/wish), WHY (to... benefit); then *Specified* (how things begin) → *Is carried out* (action taken) → *Then the result is* (result of action).

Every user story should have at least one acceptance criterion that: is identified before implementation; is testable; has a clear result (e.g. passed/failed); contains functional and nonfunctional criteria. Members of the business ecosystem should be able to describe all criteria, and all criteria are normally verified by the product owner.

## Key Concepts

- **What "minimum viable" means.** All processes and development steps kept as lean as possible — a pragmatic procedure to risk minimization, now used for all radical changes in existing companies, from digital transformation to exploration of new growth fields.
- **"Product" is loose.** "The word 'product' in MVP is in many cases a combination of a product or a service with a function and/or experience."
- **The MVP is more high-resolution than the final prototype.** In this procedural model, the MVP sits above the prototypes realized in design thinking.
- **Prioritization matrix for MVP scope.** Elements are entered into a matrix reflecting **impact on added value for the customer** against **urgency for testing**. The four quadrants: high impact + urgent → *to be integrated in the MVP*; high impact + not urgent → *discuss*; low impact + urgent → *check again*; low impact + not urgent → *not to be integrated in the MVP*.
- **Develop several MVPs in tandem**, test them all, and learn from it.
- **Usability is about understanding, not technology.** "Usability is about the question of how the customer/user understands, perceives, and uses something; it is not about the technology used for it."
- **MMF (minimum marketable feature).** To move from MVP to ecosystem design, "something like an MMF is needed that, in the prototype of a business ecosystem, is used to establish the MVE, test it, and gradually improve it." Choose a feature and part of a value proposition/offer with the greatest customer benefit — "a sort of **killer function or experience** that brings the ecosystem to life, engenders enthusiasm in the customer, and convinces potential actors of the overall system." Depending on complexity, an MVP can also be used directly for developing and testing the MVE.

## Mental Models

- **Maximum learning, minimum effort.** "This certainly doesn't mean that a limited version of a future solution is to be realized. Instead, the aim is achieving a maximum gain in knowledge with the least effort."
- **A good MVP aims at value, not features.** "A good MVP aims at the value and benefit to the customer. The validation of individual features alone usually does not make a good MVP." The four framing questions: Which function can be built? Which function is capable of offering a benefit to the customer? Which version of an MVP can yield the greatest measurable added value? How can the value proposition be validated?
- **Pivot timing is judgment.** "The right time for a radical change is decisive. Frequently, it's the gut feeling that decides when this change of direction is due." And: "Minor adaptations and features of customer needs or of the functions/features are not pivots but part of the MVP process."
- **Customers cannot price directly.** "In most cases, the potential customers are not able to articulate the value they place on a feature directly; what they can do is compare two alternatives in an A/B test and express their preference."

## Anti-patterns

- Relying on pure, unvalidated assumptions "that usually turn out to be false."
- Calling a stripped-down version of the future solution an MVP.
- Treating minor feature adaptations as pivots.
- Offering a single feature and expecting market success: "Offering a single feature is not enough to be successful on the market."
- Innovative customer development dismissed as risky: "In the mindset of integrated and rather traditionally managed companies, 'innovative customer development' is seen as risky because there is often little room for the exploitation of existing know-how or for achieving economies of scale."

**Usability feedback four-quadrant matrix** — customer behavior vs customer attitude, qualitative (direct) vs quantitative (indirect):
1. What does the customer **do**? (behavior, qualitative)
2. What does the customer **say**? (attitude, qualitative)
3. **Why?** How and how can it be fixed? (behavior, quantitative)
4. **How often / how much?** (quantitative)

Procedure: (1) write down what potential customers do; (2) document key statements from test customers; (3) find out why customers behave in a certain way and what workarounds they chose; (4) record everything countable and measurable — e.g. how many clicks were needed, when a process was aborted, and how much customers would have paid.

**Willingness-to-pay analysis.** Pricing models named: cost plus pricing, comparison pricing, and WTP analysis. Methods: **discrete choice analysis** (based on actual purchase data or asking customers for preference among alternatives with different attribute sets), and **conjoint analysis** (given a sufficiently large number of votes, an implicit evaluation of each feature by the customers). The value ladder is **cost → price → WTP**, with **value added** as the gap.
Procedure: (1) open question in interaction with the potential customer and the MVP — *What is the attribute/feature worth to you?*; (2) or start from an existing price indication; (3) as part of A/B tests, ask about various pricing aspects; (4) the analysis may relate to the overall offer, individual features, or specific services (e.g. neutral consultancy).
"It is unlikely that each customer has the same willingness to pay for a particular product or service, and it is recommended to create a **market demand curve** that shows how many customers will buy at a given price; from this, the **market price elasticity** can be calculated."

**MVP portfolio planning.** Built on an **Ansoff matrix** adapted to MVPs in potential ecosystems — original dimensions being market penetration, product development, market development, and diversification. "Products in the form of existing offers and existing markets normally don't require lean start-up considerations. The focus in design thinking for business growth is on **innovative customer development (new markets / new offers = potential black ocean offer)**."
Portfolio grid procedure: enter individual projects, reflect on progress **every four weeks or at shorter intervals**; bubble size per initiative indicates value added (NPV, IRR, revenue, or other figures); four phases show implementation status, roughly classified as **discovery** (prototype/develop) and **delivery** (MVP); arrows indicate shifts to the next phase or a return to discovery — e.g. because the first usability test yielded new insights.

## Worked Example

**The transition from MVP to MVE — the five questions** (p. 223), the book's own gate for leaving lens #2.

*What the transition requires.* "In many ways, the MVPs delivered validation of the relevant functions, experiences, and features, so the whole attains true added value for the customer." To move to ecosystem design, an **MMF** is needed — a feature and part of a value proposition chosen for greatest customer benefit, a killer function or experience that brings the ecosystem to life, engenders enthusiasm in the customer, and convinces potential actors of the overall system.

*The five questions related to the transition to the MVE:*

1. **Is a business ecosystem needed for the realization of the overriding value proposition, or does a normal partnership suffice?**
2. **What is the experience, function, or feature (MMF) to start the minimum viable variant of the ecosystem?**
3. **What skills are needed by the other actors? What does your own company bring to the table?**
4. **For which persona/target group is the first function and experience?**
5. **Have potential business ecosystem partners been involved in the preliminary deliberations? Should they also be a part of the co-creation for the business ecosystem? Are there any other actors who should be a part of the process of finding a solution as early as in the design phase?**

*What the questions are for.* They help the team reflect on whether this is actually the right way to implement the project; find out which killer function and experience the focus should be on so as to make the ecosystem intriguing for other actors; and perform a **gap analysis** with regard to skills existing versus skills needed to realize the system or parts of it.

*Question 1 is the real gate.* It is the book's own restatement of "what a business ecosystem is not" (Ch 2), placed at exactly the moment the team is most tempted to escalate a partnership into an ecosystem.

**Transition from lean start-up to ecosystem design — MVP vs MVE:**

| Dimension | #2 Minimum viable product | #3 Minimum viable ecosystem |
|---|---|---|
| **Purpose** | Validation at all levels of the design lenses (feasibility, desirability, viability). Principle: achieving maximum learning with as little effort as possible | Validation at all levels of the design lenses (feasibility, **adaptability**, and **value enhanceability**). Principle: achieving maximum learning with the **smallest number of actors per role** in the system |
| **Focus** | Good enough for market launch and interaction with real (test) customers | Use of the MVP as the basis for testing a functioning business ecosystem with the involvement of all actors and (test) customers |
| **Features** | Basic functions, functional elements, and experiences | All necessary experience and functions for **at least one key experience** for the customer in terms of the defined value proposition |
| **Target group** | Larger group of initial (test) customers and basis for talks with potential actors | Interaction and co-creation with actors in the system for building and validating the ecosystem |
| **Legacy** | First version of a marketable solution | First validation of a functioning business ecosystem |
| **Type of feedback** | Feedback on individual functions and experiences, or a chain of experiences from different MVPs | Feedback on value streams, customer interfaces, data, algorithms within the scope of the MVE |
| **Design** | Product functionality, features, and experiences | Interaction of components for the design of the value proposition or parts of it, in the form of experiences or functions |
| **Customer benefit** | Delivers a validated value proposition within the scope of one or more MVPs | Delivers a validated value proposition from actors in an MVE |
| **Creation time / risk** | Business case and product/features validated; sufficient financing; moderate risks | Interplay of actors and value streams validated; multidimensional view of business models carried out; business model of the ecosystem is known; financing by the initiator or other actors; **increased risk** |
| **Tests** | Test of the solution and relevant functions; willingness to spend money | Test of the value proposition and associated services, value streams, and interactions of the actors; checking the customer interfaces |
| **Revenue** | Possible initial income from early adopters as (test) customers | Possible initial income from a functioning ecosystem with (test) customers |

## Key Takeaways

- Several MVPs, often in parallel, prepare a single MVE; the MVP also serves as the persuasion artifact for potential actors.
- MVP scope decision: impact on customer added value × urgency for testing; only high-impact-and-urgent goes in.
- Nine named pivots, tracked as customer/problem/solution hypotheses over successive experiments; minor feature changes are not pivots.
- WTP is measured comparatively (A/B, conjoint), not asked directly; build a demand curve and derive price elasticity.
- Ansoff-based MVP portfolio, reviewed every four weeks, with new markets × new offers as the black ocean quadrant.
- The MMF — one killer function or experience — is what carries the MVP into the MVE.
