# Chapter 3 — Ecosystems as Growth Lever

*Pages 57–81 ("Definition of Ecosystems as Growth Lever")*

## Core Idea

"An artful business ecosystem makes use of the resources of every accessible and suitable system as well as of all relevant actors to generate added value for the customer and ecosystem partners." Business ecosystem design sits at the **interface** between three other ecosystem types — innovation, knowledge and information, and transaction and data ecosystems — and exploits them as design building blocks. This chapter supplies the definitional apparatus the rest of the book runs on: types, roles, value propositions, value streams, complexity levels, and the biological analogy (with its limits).

## Frameworks Introduced

**Three types of ecosystems that business ecosystem design draws on:**

| Type | Purpose | Ambition | Organization / procedure | Output |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Business ecosystems (as an integrated discipline)** | Value proposition aimed at a specific target group and their needs; dynamic, agile to change | Exponential growth | Collaborative and decentralized; *initiate and orchestrate* | The value proposition itself |
| **Transaction and data ecosystems** | Combine customer data into centralized or decentralized systems for a synchronized 720° view; coordinate offerings, interfaces, technologies | Network effects and scaling | Value driven and data driven; *build and monetize* | Automation and standardization enabling scaling and data collection for new products, services, propositions |
| **Innovation ecosystems** | Develop new products, services, or processes through co-creation between all actors; often cluster (FinTech Singapore, IT security Tel Aviv, Crypto Valley Switzerland, Silicon Valley) | Differentiation and technology leadership | Open innovation and business model innovation; *invent and scale* | Business model innovations or technology-driven innovation, clustering around key technologies |
| **Knowledge and information ecosystems** | Exploit decentralized knowledge and information resources, creating synergies by swapping values (research institutions, universities, information service providers) | Intellectual property (IP) and negotiation power | Collaboration with qualified organizations and research institutions; *fund and execute* | New knowledge from research or validated information of trusted entities |

**The roles grid.** Roles are placed on two axes — direct vs indirect benefit, and part of the value proposition vs enabler technologies — around the CUSTOMER at the center.

| Role | Responsibilities |
|---|---|
| **Customer** | Procures service; interacts with the ecosystem on a recurring basis; shares data about behavior and transactions; gets direct/indirect added value |
| **Initiator** | Recognizes customer needs and market opportunity; starts initialization; provides budget for initial prototypes and MVP; switches to orchestrator or becomes an actor |
| **Orchestrator** | Coordinates activities; defines principles and rules; defines framework conditions for growth; realizes sustainable governance |
| **Actor** | Has skills or offerings that are integrated; renders service or partial services of the value proposition; innovates within the principles and rules; connects parts of its activities over the project |
| **Supplier** | Delivers technology or technology components; provides software or integration layers; delivers infrastructure and connectivity; **no customer relationship** |
| **Other players** | Often recipients of information; serve as an *oracle* for the validation of data in automated processes; usually no direct customer interface |

Rule of thumb: **five to six roles are often sufficient** to define a business ecosystem.

**The value proposition anatomy** — four interlocking elements:
- **Value proposition**: products, services, offerings, and experiences.
- **Value streams**: between actors, in the form of money, information, data, innovation/IP, or intangible assets.
- **Performance delivery**: activities, resources, and skills required for fulfillment and delivery.
- **Risks**: taken by the whole ecosystem or by an actor responsible for a specific proposition — operational, performance-related, financial, risks from other actors' incentives, or systemic/dynamic risks. Examples: performance guarantee, outsourcing, regulatory compliance.

**Three levels of business system complexity:**

| | Focused partner network | Centralized business network | Decentralized business ecosystem |
|---|---|---|---|
| Thinking in | Value chains | Platforms | Black oceans |
| Era / driver | Second and third industrial revolution; outsourcing, IaaS/SaaS | Age of the Internet; platform economy | Present transition |
| Basis of collaboration | SLAs; prices set by power relations of sourcing partners | Network effects, scaling, APIs; existing power relations, familiar business models | Orchestrator, changed customer needs, multidimensional business models |
| Complexity | Low | Medium | High |
| Examples | — | Amazon, Apple, Uber, Spotify | — |

The focused partner network belongs to the category of partnerships and value chains — it does not have the character of a real business ecosystem.

## Key Concepts

- **The stability spectrum.** Very stable systems = vertically integrated companies with static customer/supplier chains. Very agile systems = open, capital-economy markets without government regulation. Business ecosystems lie between the two.
- **Integrated company vs ecosystem view.** Ecosystems operate beyond existing industry and company boundaries (external focus on value creation); align to network effects and overarching network structures rather than 100% linear value creation; are more exposed to market dynamics and technology change; have declining predictability of target states, heightened uncertainty, and permanent adjustment as a vital capability; and have increasingly decentralized governance and control.
- **Ecosystem capital.** The wealth of relationships between actors. Combining value streams lets value creation be mapped as a dynamic and networked exchange in which theoretically every actor connects to every other — so well-thought-out ecosystems generate more value than the sum of independent actors.
- **Strategic fields of action for an ecosystem play**: leverage of existing capabilities (EXPLOIT) through to forays into new, previously uncovered offers (EXPLORE); compilation of a unique but diverse or complex range of propositions requiring different actors; tapping new marketplaces and marketing channels; disruption of one's own industry, value chains, or individual intermediaries.
- **When to bother.** Vertically integrated companies well positioned with current models may legitimately not care. The picture differs for companies sensing disruption from new market participants, moving in a dynamic environment with new technologies, or for whom growth and diversification can only be achieved by spending large amounts of resources. Ecosystems also give smaller companies access to resources, experience, and skills normally available only to large conglomerates.

## Mental Models

- **Biology as blueprint, with a caveat.** Biological ecosystems have one or several equilibria, operate in cooperation *and* competition, and require species to adapt to remain viable. Business ecosystems differ in one decisive way: **participation requires incentives** — market participants only participate if they get something out of it, are sponsored, or have no choice because of disruption. Design follows the positive principle of co-creation, value generation for all actors, and a proposition supported by all.
- **The island analogy.** Integrated companies with rigid supply chains that see no threat resemble species on an island — birds that no longer fly, because there are no predators to flee. The question for both is the same: how quickly can a transformation be carried out, and is there enough time to survive? Large, slowly growing species with long lifespans need longer to adapt.
- **Co-creation as intertidal-zone fertilization.** Joint exploitation of resources and mutual fertilization of ideas in the "intertidal zones" — the shared work of different actors on the system is the foundation for unique value propositions and innovation.
- **Only recurring value survives.** "Only those business ecosystems that create recurring or sustainable values for the actors survive and grow." This value design is part of defining an MVE and one of the most complex tasks in ecosystem design.

## Anti-patterns

- Applying old centralized, hierarchical patterns to initial ideas about participating in or building an ecosystem.
- Integrating only actors who contribute to fulfilling the underlying customer need, and forgetting technology suppliers needed to deliver requirements smoothly across channels and interfaces.
- Assuming all ecosystems succeed because the best-known examples (WeChat, Amazon, Alibaba) did — this overlooks the far greater number of initiators, orchestrators, and actors who did not.

## Worked Example

**Coevolution of Apple and Goldman Sachs** — the chapter's named example (pp. 71–73).

The setup: two companies that "at first glance are not related but complement one another and develop together." Goldman Sachs is an investment banking and securities trading firm headquartered in New York, serving major companies, governments, and high net worth individuals (HNWI) with M&A consulting, asset management, and brokerage; since 2018 its **Marcus Bank** has offered fee-less private loans, interest-bearing online savings accounts, and certificates of deposit. Apple is a technology group developing computers, smartphones, and consumer electronics plus proprietary operating systems and application software, and handles music, movies, and other digital content.

The symbiosis: Goldman Sachs was a big Wall Street name but until a few years ago not active in the credit card business. Goldman's trust plus Apple's technology-lifestyle approach made it possible to conquer new market areas at low risk. Goldman handles processing in the background while new customer contacts emerge for Marcus Bank. Apple customers gain benefits such as reimbursement of **up to 3%** of purchases in the Apple Store for digital and physical goods. According to Apple, the data has so far not been analyzed and used for advertising purposes. David Solomon, CEO of Goldman Sachs, called the Apple Card launch **"the most successful credit card launch ever."**

The convergence path (as reproduced in the book's two-column timeline; several year labels in the source rendering are partially corrupted, so only the reliably legible ones are given):

| Apple | Goldman Sachs |
|---|---|
| **Apple Pay (2014)** — mobile contactless payment system and digital wallet; NFC payment at POS, in iOS apps, or on the Internet | **Marcus (2016)** — online platform for open credits and savings accounts for private customers; fee-less fixed-income private loans, high-yield online savings, certificates of deposit; a building block of an integral digital wealth product being integrated into investment management |
| **Apple Card** — physical credit cards and cards integrated in the Wallet app; no user fees; 1–3% daily cash-back to the Apple Cash Card; real-time interest estimate; real-time fraud protection; colour-coded expense overviews | **U.S. asset management for the "mass affluent" market** — acquisition of United Capital Financial Advisors LLC; development of an investment platform realizing customer interactions via Marcus |
| **Apple Sign-In** — secure identification for web services; disposable e-mail addresses preventing third parties from reading the Apple ID; protection of user data; anonymous surfing | **Stake in Elinvar** — German start-up founded by former Deutsche Bank bankers; digital platform helping traditional lenders offer services online; Goldman holds **13.9%** |
| **Apple CryptoKit** — Swift API, cryptographic developer tool; hashing, public/private key generation and exchange, encryption integration in iOS applications | **Stake in Nutmeg** |

The strategic reading: the affluent target group has financial assets between **$250,000 and $1 million** depending on country and bank. The collaboration could constitute the basis for a larger ecosystem servicing the well-to-do and other target groups — a unique customer experience for wealthy banking customers emerging over time from individual elements of both companies. Digital content is one of the important future value streams in the Apple business ecosystem; step by step Apple has built out these services, anchoring customers in them so that leaving becomes harder over time. The Apple Card, integrating Apple Pay, is another such anchor.

## Key Takeaways

- Business ecosystem design is positioned *between* innovation, knowledge/information, and transaction/data ecosystems and deliberately consumes them as building blocks.
- Six roles cover most systems; the distinguishing test for a supplier is that it has no customer relationship.
- Value streams carry money, information, data, innovation/IP, and intangible assets — and can be negative (e.g. transfer of risk).
- The biological analogy breaks at incentives: nature does not require a business case, ecosystems do.
- Complexity climbs from focused partner network (not a real ecosystem) through centralized business network to decentralized ecosystem — with capability requirements climbing alongside.
