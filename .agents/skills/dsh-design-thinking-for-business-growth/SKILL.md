## Core Frameworks & Mental Models

**Problem to Growth and Scale framework** — the book's spine. Five fits in sequence, each with an artifact:
`Customer needs → problem/solution fit (final prototype) → product/market fit (MVP) → system/actors fit (MVE) → ecosystem/coevolution fit (black ocean)`.
Design thinking + research feed the first two; lean start-up validates the MVP; ecosystem design produces the MVE; scale produces exponential growth.

**The four design lenses** — the operating model. Each has key questions, a canvas, and a tool set. Transitions between them are fluid; steps can be skipped when prototype resolution allows.
1. **Design thinking** — customer needs to final prototype and MVP requirements.
2. **Lean start-up** — MVP, build–measure–learn, willingness to pay, MMF.
3. **Ecosystem design** — actors, value streams, multidimensional business models, MVE.
4. **Scale** — network effects, opti-channel, ecosystem capital, exponential growth.

**Solution maturity criteria change per lens.** Feasibility runs through all four. Then:
lens 1 = desirability + viability; lens 2 = desirability + viability (validated); lens 3 = **adaptability + value enhanceability**; lens 4 = **captivateability + rhythmicability**.

**Ten mindshifts** (Ch 1) — the paradigm test before any ecosystem work: product/company-centered→customer-centered; linear→iterative; analysis→symbiosis; silos→coevolution; elements→unique value proposition; decoupling→networking; isolation→relationships/value streams; individual→multidimensional business models; red/blue ocean→**black ocean**; command and control→**initiate and orchestrate**.

**Black ocean strategy** — the ambition of a well-designed ecosystem: a configuration with no more than three perceptible competitors, where a unique value proposition is delivered *together with other actors (including competitors)*, solving previously unaddressed customer problems. Red ocean = beat competitors; blue ocean = avoid them; black ocean = make competing impossible.

**Multidimensional view of business models** — the load-bearing design rule. The initiator/orchestrator must design not only the ecosystem's own business model but a way for *every* actor to make money, plus the additional models each actor can stack on top (Amazon B2B cloud example: cumulative levers of factor 5–10). Without an advantage or financial incentive, actors will not join.

**Roles grid** — Customer at the center; Initiator (spots the need, funds prototypes/MVP); Orchestrator (coordinates, sets principles and rules, governs growth); Actor (contributes capability, innovates within the rules); Supplier (technology, no customer relationship); Other players (information recipients, validation oracles). Five to six roles usually suffice.

**Ecosystem complexity ladder** — focused partner network (value chains, SLAs) → centralized business network (platform economy, network effects, APIs) → decentralized business ecosystem (orchestrator, multidimensional models, exponential growth potential).

**Ten-step (re)design of a business ecosystem** across four loops (Ch 13): virtuous design loop (steps 1–7), validation loop (8), realization + experiment loop (9–10). Output: the **MVE** — maximum learning with the *smallest number of actors per role*, one actor per role minimum.

**Where to play / how to win / how to configure** — the three-column ecosystem strategy canvas that documents everything the four lenses produce, with EXPLOIT (existing partnerships, technologies, known opportunities) and EXPLORE (new ones) as the bottom band.

**Decision rules a practitioner uses here**
- Do not start with ecosystem design. Go through design thinking and lean start-up first, or the team will treat customers as one actor among many rather than the center. Teams that already have validated needs plus an MVP may start at lens 3 directly.
- If a collaboration is two companies sourcing partial services, it is not an ecosystem — do not call it one.
- 85% of ecosystem failures trace to weak *design*, only 15% to implementation errors (Henderson Institute, 2020). Spend accordingly.
- EXPLOIT-only ecosystem plays cap growth at roughly a factor of 2; ecosystem capital plays are where >20% ROI, >40% margins and 10x price-sales ratios sit.
- Test the assumption in the top-right of the critical-assumptions matrix (critical + unknown) first.
- If OKRs are 100% achieved, the objectives were not ambitious enough; 80% attainment is the target for outstanding performance.

## Scope & Limits

- **Edition**: first English edition, Wiley 2022, translated from the German original (Verlag Vahlen). Chapter numbering here is imposed by this skill; the book itself is organised into named sections (Mindshifts, Ecosystem Strategy, Design Lenses Toolbox, Reflection, Examples) rather than numbered chapters. Page ranges above are the book's printed page numbers.
- **What is covered**: business ecosystem design as a discipline, ecosystem strategy, the four design lenses with their canvases and tools, ecosystem leadership and governance, scaling, reflection, and 13 company examples.
- **What is not covered**: this is not a design thinking primer. The book explicitly defers tool depth to *The Design Thinking Playbook* (2018) and *The Design Thinking Toolbox* (2019); the design thinking and lean start-up lenses here are summaries of tools treated at length elsewhere. No implementation code, no financial modelling detail, no legal/regulatory guidance beyond the PESTLE prompts.
- **Dating**: market data, capital-market multiples, and company snapshots are as of 2019–2021 (Tencent 1.2bn WeChat users, Ant's ~€150bn pre-IPO valuation, COVID-19-era WeChat mini-program figures, YOUR NOW as a Daimler/BMW joint venture, WeWork/The We Company). The BCG Henderson Institute studies cited are from 2019 and 2020. The "by 2030, >30% of global revenue in business ecosystems" projection and the "15 to 20 dominant business ecosystems worldwide" estimate are the author's forward-looking claims, not measurements.
- **Extraction quality**: the text was extracted from a heavily illustrated, hand-lettered layout. Display headings frequently render `D` as `P` (e.g. "PESIGN", "MOPEL"), some diagram labels and figure-only pages carry little recoverable text, and several full-page infographics (the subway-line trajectory diagram p. 174–175, the driver tree p. 215, several capital-configuration charts in Ch 16) survive only as fragments. Where a figure could not be read reliably, this skill describes what the surrounding prose states and does not reconstruct the missing labels.
- Template download URLs in the book point to `en.business-ecosystem-design.com` and `dt-toolbook.com/skop`; availability is not verified here.
