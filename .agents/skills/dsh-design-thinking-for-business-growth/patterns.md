# Patterns — Design Thinking for Business Growth (Lewrick, 2022)

The book's reusable moves, each as **When / How / Trade-offs**, grouped by its own parts.

---

## Part 1 — Mindset and paradigm

### Run the ten mindshifts as an entry test
**When** — Before any ecosystem strategy work, and again whenever a design team's deliberations start pivoting on its own products.
**How** — Walk the ten shifts (customer-centered, iterative, symbiosis, coevolution, unique value propositions, networking, value streams, multidimensional models, black ocean, initiate and orchestrate). For each, name the observable failure mode in the team's current behaviour. The book's canonical tell: "the customer is no more than one actor out of many in the system."
**Trade-offs** — Cheap and fast, but it is a diagnosis, not a cure. Companies that have already been through digital transformation start further along and may over-credit themselves; practice shows teams reverting anyway.

### Agree design principles before the lenses start
**When** — Early in the initiative, ideally with project sponsors and cross-company partners present.
**How** — Everyone writes principles on Post-its into a "basket." Sort onto a pyramid in three groups — more project-specific higher up, general ecosystem principles at the base. Vote with glue dots down to max three per section, max nine total. Post them where the team meets them daily.
**Trade-offs** — Fast decisions later at the cost of an early workshop; principles set too generically give no guidance, set too specifically they date as the ecosystem evolves.

### Refuse the label "ecosystem" for partnerships
**When** — Whenever a two-company collaboration, sourcing arrangement, marketplace, or centrally controlled platform is being described as an ecosystem.
**How** — Apply three tests: does it open up to the outside? Is it transparent about skills and values? Does it accept new market roles in existing or new systems? Ask the lens #2 transition question: is an ecosystem needed for the overriding value proposition, or does a normal partnership suffice?
**Trade-offs** — Unpopular internally, since "ecosystem" attracts sponsorship. But mislabelling propagates: a partnership designed as an ecosystem inherits ecosystem governance cost with none of the network effects.

## Part 2 — Ecosystem strategy

### Two inventories, then a what-if
**When** — At the start of "where to play," especially where the company has never thought in ecosystems.
**How** — (1) Initiatives/industry matrix: write down every initiative company-wide, plot isolated-to-networked against own-industry-to-cross-industry; the interesting cells are networked and cross-industry. (2) Cooperation/industry matrix: same for partnerships, cooperations, suppliers, on focus-to-diversification. (3) Then break the inventory's gravity with the what-if sequence: what are the current needs? what if? where might a WOW! be created? what works?
**Trade-offs** — The inventories build shared reality and often surface forgotten access to data, customers, or technology. They also anchor thinking on what exists — which is exactly why the what-if step is not optional.

### Adapt PESTLE rather than run it
**When** — Contextualizing an ecosystem strategy for decision makers and management bodies.
**How** — Re-question each of the six dimensions for the ecosystem specifically (e.g. legal: "Are the laws and regulations understood in **all the dimensions in which the business ecosystem will operate**?"). Share results with every team designing the ecosystem, not just strategy.
**Trade-offs** — Makes the option legible to boards. But analysis without iterative exploration of customer needs "will not lead to success" — treat it as complementary, never as a substitute for lens #1.

### Document the strategy canvas continuously
**When** — Throughout the four lenses, not once at the start.
**How** — Route each canvas's output to its strategy canvas fields: design thinking canvas → topic area, customer needs, customer interface; lean start-up canvas → value proposition, capabilities, MVP/MMF/backlog; ecosystem design canvas → vision, roles, business models; growth and scale canvas → growth and scale, EXPLORE. Keep the EXPLOIT/EXPLORE band populated on every pass.
**Trade-offs** — Living documentation costs discipline but prevents the recurring failure of losing the larger target image once realization starts.

### Configure the mobile, not the parts
**When** — Setting or revising an ecosystem's configuration.
**How** — Select a manifestation for each of the eight dimensions (strategic focus, customer interaction, customer relationships, capabilities, product/service portfolio, organizational design, competitive strategy, roles). Then check coupling: black ocean ambition pulls greenfield capability build, transversal teams, digital-first interaction, and the orchestrator role along with it.
**Trade-offs** — Coupling makes the configuration coherent and makes half-measures visible; it also means one strategic ambition can commit large amounts of organizational change you did not price in.

### Pick the EXPLOIT/EXPLORE mix deliberately
**When** — Deciding what growth ceiling you are designing for.
**How** — EXPLOIT contributions (existing skills, core business, existing models) into an ecosystem give linear growth, factor ~2 at most. EXPLORE with new configurations of business models including partners' gives black ocean potential, usually via a greenfield approach. Most companies run a mixture; successful ecosystem actors run a *balanced portfolio*.
**Trade-offs** — EXPLOIT is fundable, predictable, and low-risk but caps the outcome; EXPLORE requires greenfield distance from corporate structures and mindsets and carries the risk profile to match.

## Part 3 — Leadership and ways of working

### Place innovation teams by knowledge complexity
**When** — Deciding where an ecosystem or innovation team physically and organizationally sits.
**How** — Use the innovation team matrix. High market complexity + low technology knowledge → move technology information to where the market and customer knowledge are. Low market complexity + high technology knowledge → move market and customer information to the technology. Both high → rotating workforce and temporary co-allocation. Both low → higher-level information exchange suffices.
**Trade-offs** — Rotation delivers cross-silo dialogue and mindful transformation at the cost of continuity; some teams lose depth while gaining breadth.

### Cascade OKRs across ecosystem partners
**When** — Coordinating a team-of-teams working across company boundaries.
**How** — Vision → initiatives → goals → strategies → tactics → action plans. Max five objectives per quarter × four key results, cycles of two to four months, individual objectives 1–2 long-term plus 2–4 near-term, everything transparent to everyone. Stretch the OKRs across business units *and* ecosystem partners. Treat 80% attainment as excellent.
**Trade-offs** — Makes growth initiatives visible at all levels and speeds teams up; but OKRs across company boundaries expose internal targets to partners, and stretch goals demand a "bowl of trust" that many organizations do not yet have.

### Choose an opening-up posture consciously
**When** — Deciding when to bring potential actors into the initiative.
**How** — Locate yourself among cooperatives / exploratives / seekers / mysterious, and accept the named cost: complexity and time (cooperatives), coordination load (exploratives), reduced later reframing (seekers), late value proposition validation (mysterious). In actual company practice, opening is mostly done *after* the MVP, because maturity gives decision makers more certainty.
**Trade-offs** — Each posture buys one thing with another; the only wrong answer is not choosing, and thereby defaulting to the mysterious posture by inertia.

### Match the co-creation form to the phase
**When** — Every collaborative session across the lenses.
**How** — Conduct research (design thinking) → open (lens transitions) → integrative (ecosystem design, where win-win must hold for every actor) → collaborative (MVE realization) → reflective (after each lens). Each form has a stated required behaviour.
**Trade-offs** — Naming the form sets expectations and avoids a research session drifting into premature solutioning; it also adds a facilitation overhead teams may resist.

## Part 4 — The four design lenses

### Consolidate eight critical items before prototyping
**When** — After the understand and observe phases, before ideation.
**How** — Ask "What is crucial for a successful solution?" Each member writes eight elements — four experiences, four functions — one of them focused on completely new or future expectations. Consolidate to eight team-agreed elements. Derive HMW questions from them.
**Trade-offs** — This single artifact carries all the way through: it defines the MVP's functions and experiences, becomes the lean start-up backlog, and later seeds the MVE. Forcing consolidation to eight loses nuance but produces something actionable; re-question the items each iteration.

### Prototype as experiment, resolution by purpose
**When** — Any prototyping cycle in lens #1.
**How** — Before building, state what insights you want and why. Formulate the assumptions to be tested and decide how the experiment runs. Low-resolution prototypes for needs, practicality, functionality in the divergent phase; high-resolution for feasibility and profitability. Record everything on the exploration map (experience × function axes, expected vs actual).
**Trade-offs** — Explicit experiment design slows the first prototype and speeds every one after; the exploration map is only useful if maintained, and its value appears retrospectively.

### Test the critical-and-unknown assumption first
**When** — Continuously, in every lens.
**How** — Plot assumptions on critical ↔ not critical against known ↔ unknown, tagged by criterion (desirability, viability, feasibility, and later adaptability, value enhanceability, captivateability, rhythmicability). Examine the top-right quadrant. Re-plot after each lens, because new assumptions about actors and value streams arrive as the system takes shape.
**Trade-offs** — Prevents effort going into comfortable validation, but requires the team to admit which assumptions it does not know — a social cost, not a methodological one.

### Build several MVPs in parallel, then choose one MMF
**When** — Preparing for the transition into ecosystem design.
**How** — Prioritize elements by impact on customer added value × urgency for testing; only high-impact-and-urgent goes into an MVP. Develop several MVPs in tandem and learn across them. Then select the **one killer function or experience** (the MMF) that brings the ecosystem to life and convinces potential actors. Answer the five transition questions before proceeding.
**Trade-offs** — Parallel MVPs cost more and buy comparative learning plus fallback options. The MMF choice is a bet: pick wrong and the actors you approach see no reason to join.

### Measure willingness to pay comparatively
**When** — Validating pricing on an MVP.
**How** — Start with the open question ("What is the attribute/feature worth to you?"), then move to A/B and conjoint comparisons, since customers cannot articulate value directly. Build a market demand curve; derive price elasticity. Sanity-check that costs sit in a healthy relationship with validated WTP.
**Trade-offs** — Comparative methods need volume of responses to be meaningful; the open question is cheap but unreliable on its own.

### Design the ecosystem in four loops, ten steps
**When** — Once customer needs are validated and at least one MVP exists.
**How** — Virtuous design loop (core value proposition → actors → ecosystem map → value streams → advantages/disadvantages per actor → multidimensional business models → redesign) → validation loop (collaboration between actors, decision makers and teams per actor) → realization loop (team to implement the MVE) → experiment loop (implement and test the final MVE on one or several MVPs). Design at three levels: customers, business, technology. Break complex systems into subsystems.
**Trade-offs** — 12–16 weeks or more, and "one of the most demanding" phases. But 85% of ecosystem failures are design failures — this is where the money is.

### Design the ACTUAL system before the TARGET
**When** — Especially when restructuring an existing ecosystem radically.
**How** — Draw today's system with its actors and flows, highlight problem areas, then redesign. The alternative — designing radically new and eliminating actors up front — is valid for greenfield. For restructuring, the ACTUAL-first path lets processes, procedures, information, and value streams be redefined without losing sight of customer needs.
**Trade-offs** — ACTUAL-first anchors the team in existing structures (the thing the mindshifts warn against) but produces defensible arguments for what to eliminate.

### Make every actor's business model explicit
**When** — Step 6 of the virtuous design loop, and again before approaching each actor.
**How** — Two moves. (1) What model lets this actor make money **on the basis of the defined value streams** (use the lean canvas with expansions)? (2) Take the actor's own view: what *additional* money can it make from ecosystem services — skills, customer access, regional presence, physical locations, key-technology know-how? Fill the actor description template: function, examples, motivation, compatibility with the value proposition (strong/neutral/weak), time horizon (MVP/MVE/expansion stage), market dominance, advantages and disadvantages, value contribution, current business model.
**Trade-offs** — Complex and "asks too much of many business ecosystem design teams" — but without it, actors have no reason to join, and the book's simple rule applies: "Without an advantage or a corresponding financial incentive, actors will not be enthused about the system!"

### Role-play the actors around the value proposition
**When** — After actor descriptions exist, before placing them on the map.
**How** — Write the value proposition in the middle of the room. Each team member takes an actor (max 12 roles in the first phase). Group by importance and by direct/indirect relation to others and to the proposition. Each explains their role, advantages, disadvantages, and position. Positions change, actors are added, distances shift. Integrate findings back into the descriptions.
**Trade-offs** — Surfaces missing skills, missing actors, and poor fit fast, and builds shared understanding. It runs on assumptions about the actors until those are checked with the actors themselves.

### Draw several ecosystem map variants with PRO and CON
**When** — Steps 3 and 7 of the virtuous design loop.
**How** — Cover a table with paper. Draw two or three concentric circles by closeness to the core value proposition. Place actors. Produce variants: current system (ACTUAL), greenfield approach, and redesign prototypes 1/2/3, each annotated PRO and CON. For each variant, determine the impact on individual actors and value streams. Prove robustness by iterations and experiments.
**Trade-offs** — Variant generation is where new business model approaches appear; it also multiplies the work of value-stream mapping, which is why complex initiatives need digital tooling.

### Ship an MVE with one actor per role
**When** — Closing lens #3.
**How** — Assemble the relevant MVPs/MMFs. Put at least one actor and a defined number of customers behind each role. Communicate goals and underlying assumptions transparently. Encourage actors to contribute improvements early. Apply build–measure–learn. Follow the 8 Golden Rules, including "Adapt the system if it doesn't work in its minimal version." Then launch with a WOW! effect and use the market momentum.
**Trade-offs** — Increased risk relative to an MVP, financed by the initiator or other actors — but this is the only way to validate value streams, actor interplay, and the multidimensional business models before full implementation.

### Expand by leverage, innovate, expand
**When** — After the final MVE is on the market.
**How** — (1) Leverage the first proposition. (2) Innovate by exploring. (3) Expand the proposition — into new topic areas, with more actors holding the same skills/roles, with new actors and roles, and by identifying further customer needs. Give actors the freedom to become active "even at the limits of the rules," and test recurring new behaviours quickly and iteratively. The cycle returns to design thinking, lean start-up, and ecosystem (re)design.
**Trade-offs** — Expansion without actor freedom stalls innovation; expansion with too much freedom loses coherence of the core value proposition. The orchestrator holds that balance.

### Move to opti-channel, not omni-channel
**When** — Scaling customer interaction.
**How** — Multi-channel → omni-channel (seamless integration, journey optimization) → opti-channel (best possible interaction as part of the ecosystem journey; chat and voice bots; personalized interaction; emotional AI later). Gather physical-world information into digital records; give the customer the best channel rather than the choice; let machines exchange information for real-time customization; apply algorithms and automation to decisions in both worlds.
**Trade-offs** — Opti-channel needs the data omni-channel produces, so the stages are sequential; choosing for the customer improves experience but requires trust in your inference.

### Preserve positive costs while cutting negative ones
**When** — Any cost programme during scaling.
**How** — Classify costs as "positive" (facilitating future exponential growth) or "negative" (reducible by strategic cost-cutting). Keep the standing constraint: cost of user acquisition below the lifetime value generated. Look for expanded value streams — separate big data ecosystems, anonymized data on marketplaces, mass customization, third-party data provision within the law.
**Trade-offs** — The classification is a judgment call and will be contested by finance; getting it wrong in either direction either strangles growth or funds waste.

## Part 5 — Reflection

### Reflect at three levels after every lens
**When** — After each micro cycle, each sprint, each design lens, and at every lens transition.
**How** — Initiative level (what went well, what methods, what to do differently, key learnings). Meta level (what is ecosystem design, pros and cons, challenges, difference from and combination with other approaches, use cases and introduction in one's own environment). Personal level (which paradigm shift is easy, where the mental hurdles are, whether you and the partners fall back into old patterns). Use the reflection canvas to capture design lenses, digital fluency, ecosystem leadership, market opportunities, capabilities, mindset, principles, technologies, data maturity, capital, and governance.
**Trade-offs** — Regular reflection costs cycle time; skipping it means you cannot know whether the team is actually acting in concert.

### Run feedback as a ritual with one hard rule
**When** — Every iteration, on prototypes, stories, business models, and whole ecosystems.
**How** — "I like" (a specific decision, never the person), "I wish" (a concrete addition, never a criticism of what exists), "I wonder" (the strategic question, without accusation). Write it down, express thanks, use it. **The recipient does not start a discussion.**
**Trade-offs** — Preserves mood and partnership, which matters most in cross-company and sensitive work; it deliberately suppresses immediate debate, so genuine misunderstandings surface later rather than at once.

### Separate what the team controls from what it does not
**When** — Retrospectives after a lens, workshop, co-creation session, or actor interviews.
**How** — Sailboat: island (shared vision and motivation), wind (accelerating factors), anchor (inhibitory factors), cliffs (hazards against which the team has no power — market, laws, new technologies, competitors). Collect on Post-its, read aloud, cluster to causes not symptoms, define precise measures for the next iteration, close with feedback on the retro itself.
**Trade-offs** — The cliffs category prevents the team from burning energy on the uncontrollable; it can also become a place to file problems that were in fact addressable.
