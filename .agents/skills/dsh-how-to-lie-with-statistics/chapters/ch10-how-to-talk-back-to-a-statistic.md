# Chapter 10 — How to Talk Back to a Statistic

## Core Idea

The concluding chapter drops the pirate act. "So far, I have been addressing you rather as if you were a pirate with a yen for instruction in the finer points of cutlass work. In this concluding chapter I'll drop that literary device. I'll face up to the serious purpose that I like to think lurks just beneath the surface of this book: **explaining how to look a phoney statistic in the eye and face it down; and no less important, how to recognize sound and usable data in that wilderness of fraud to which the previous chapters have been largely devoted.**"

The method is deliberately non-technical: "Not all the statistical information that you may come upon can be tested with the sureness of chemical analysis or of what goes on in an assayer's laboratory. But you can **prod the stuff with five simple questions**, and by finding the answers avoid learning a remarkable lot that isn't so."

## Frameworks Introduced

## THE FIVE QUESTIONS

### 1. Who Says So?

"About the first thing to look for is **bias** — the laboratory with something to prove for the sake of a theory, a reputation, or a fee; the newspaper whose aim is a good story; labour or management with a wage level at stake."

**Conscious bias.** "The method may be direct misstatement or it may be ambiguous statement that serves as well and cannot be convicted. It may be **selection of favourable data and suppression of unfavourable**. Units of measurement may be shifted, as with the practice of using one year for one comparison and sliding over to a more favourable year for another. An improper measure may be used: a mean where a median would be more informative (perhaps all too informative), with the trickery covered by the unqualified word 'average'."

**Unconscious bias.** "Look sharply for unconscious bias. **It is often more dangerous.** In the charts and predictions of many statisticians and economists in 1928 it operated to produce remarkable things. The cracks in the economic structure were joyously overlooked, and all sorts of evidence were adduced and statistically supported to show that we had no more than entered the stream of prosperity."

**The O.K. name.** "The *who* may be hidden by what Stephen Potter, the Lifemanship man, would probably call the 'O.K. name'. Anything smacking of the medical profession is an O.K. name. Scientific laboratories have O.K. names. So do universities, more especially ones eminent in technical work." The Cornell marriage article "made good use of the O.K. name of Cornell. Please note that while the data came from Cornell, the conclusions were entirely the writer's own. But the O.K. name helps you carry away a misimpression of 'Cornell University says...'"

> **Rule: "When an O.K. name is cited, make sure that the authority stands behind the information, not merely somewhere alongside it."**

Also on the watch-list: the phrase **"the survey shows"** — "look sharp whenever you meet those words!"

### 2. How Does He Know?

"Watch out for evidence of a **biased sample**, one that has been selected improperly or — as with this one — **has selected itself**. Ask the question we dealt with in an early chapter: **Is the sample large enough to permit any reliable conclusion?** Similarly with a reported correlation: Is it big enough to mean anything? Are there enough cases to add up to any significance?"

The layman's licence: "You cannot, as a casual reader, apply tests of significance or come to exact conclusions as to the adequacy of a sample. On a good many of the things you see reported, however, you will be able to tell at a glance — **a good long glance, perhaps** — that there just weren't enough cases to convince any reasoning person of anything."

### 3. What's Missing?

"You won't always be told how many cases. **The absence of such a figure, particularly when the source is an interested one, is enough to throw suspicion on the whole thing.** Similarly a correlation given without a measure of reliability (probable error, standard error) is not to be taken very seriously. Watch out for an average, variety unspecified, in any matter where mean and median might be expected to differ substantially."

Sub-checks Huff enumerates:
- **A missing comparison.** *Look* magazine on Mongolism: "one study shows that in 2,800 cases, over half of the mothers were 36 or over." "Getting any meaning from this depends upon your knowing something about the ages at which women in general produce babies. Few of us know things like that."
- **A missing normal range.** The *New Yorker*'s "Letter from London" on the great fog: the death rate for Greater London "jumped by twenty-eight hundred." "But how lethal was the visitation? Was it exceptional for the death rate to be that much higher than usual in a week? All such things do vary. And what about ensuing weeks? Did the death rate drop below average, indicating that if the fog killed people they were largely those who would have died shortly anyway?"
- **Percentages given, raw figures withheld** (see Worked Example).
- **A missing base for an index.** "A national labour organization once showed that indexes of profits and production had risen much more rapidly after the depression than an index of wages had. As an argument for wage increases this demonstration lost its potency when someone dug out the missing figures. It could be seen then that profits had been almost bound to rise more rapidly in percentage than wages simply because **profits had reached a lower point, giving a smaller base**."
- **A missing causal factor.** Figures showing business on the upgrade because April retail sales beat the previous year's — "What was missing was the fact that Easter had come in March in the earlier year and in April in the later year."
- **Missing changes in measurement.** A great rise in cancer deaths over a quarter-century is misleading unless you know how much comes from: cancer now being listed where "causes unknown" was formerly used; more frequent autopsies giving surer diagnoses; more complete reporting and compiling; and more people reaching the susceptible ages. "And if you are looking at total deaths rather than the death rate, don't neglect the fact that there are more people now than there used to be."

### 4. Did Somebody Change the Subject?

"When assaying a statistic, watch out for **a switch somewhere between the raw figure and the conclusion. One thing is all too often reported as another.**"

Huff's list of standard switches:
- **Reported cases ≠ cases.** California encephalitis reports tripled and residents shipped their children away — but deaths from sleeping sickness did not rise. "State and federal health people had come in in great numbers to tackle a long-time problem; as a result of their efforts a great many low-grade cases were recorded that in other years would have been overlooked."
- **Newspaper coverage ≠ incidence.** Lincoln Steffens and Jacob A. Riis "created a crime wave" in New York by competing to dig up burglaries. Theodore Roosevelt, then president of the reform Police Board, "put an end to the crime wave simply by asking Steffens and Riis to lay off. **The official police record showed no increase at all.**"
- **Saying ≠ doing.** A Ministry of Works hot-water survey of "6,000 representative British homes" produced the *San Francisco Chronicle* headline **BRITISH HE'S BATHE MORE THAN SHE'S** (men 1.7 baths a week in winter, 2.1 in summer; women 1.5 and 2.0). "What the Ministry really found out is **how often these people said they bathed, not how often they did so**... all that can safely be concluded is that they say they do." (Huff notes the figures "would be more informative if there were some indication of whether they are means or medians.")
- **A definition changed between counts.** A census showed half a million more US farms than five years earlier — "a back-to-the-farm movement." "The definition of farm used by the Bureau of the Census had been changed; it took in at least 300,000 farms that would not have been so listed under the earlier definition."
- **Reported facts distorted by incentive or habit.** Census reports show more people aged thirty-five than thirty-four or thirty-six — one family member reports the others' ages and rounds to a multiple of five. "One way to get around this: ask for birth dates instead." The population of a large area in China was 28 million; five years later, 105 million — "The first census was for tax and military purposes, the second for famine relief." A US decennial census found more people aged 65–70 than there had been aged 55–60 ten years earlier, not explicable by immigration: "Most of it could be a product of large-scale falsifying of ages by people eager to collect social security. Also possible is that some of the earlier ages were understated out of vanity."
- **Total cost vs one component.** Senator William Langer's cry that "we could take a prisoner from Alcatraz and board him at the Waldorf-Astoria cheaper" — comparing $8-a-day *total maintenance* at Alcatraz with hotel *room rent* alone.
- **Post hoc as subject-change.** "The post hoc variety of pretentious nonsense is another way of changing the subject without seeming to. **The change of something with something else is presented as because of.**" *Electrical World*'s composite chart in "What Electricity Means to America" showed electrical horsepower in factories climbing, average wages per hour climbing, and average hours per week dropping. "All these things are long-time trends, of course, and there is no evidence at all that any one of them has produced any other."
- **Firsters.** "Almost anybody can claim to be first in something if he is not too particular what it is." At the end of 1952 two New York papers each claimed first rank in grocery advertising, and both were right: the *World-Telegram* was first in full-run advertising (the only kind it ran); the *Journal-American* was first in total linage. "This is the kind of reaching for a superlative that leads the weather reporter on the radio to label a quite normal day 'the hottest June second since 1967'."
- **Interest rates.** "Six per cent sounds like six per cent — but it may not be at all." Borrow £100 from a bank at six per cent and repay in equal monthly instalments over a year and you pay about **£3**. "But another six per cent loan, on the basis sometimes called £6 on the £100, will cost you twice as much. That's the way most automobile loans are figured... The point is that you don't have the £100 for a year. By the end of six months you have paid back half of it. If you are charged at £6 on the £100... you really pay interest at nearly **twelve per cent**." Worse: freezer-food plans quoted six to twelve per cent that was "an on-the-dollar figure and, worst of all, the time was often **six months** rather than a year. Now £12 on the £100 for money to be paid back regularly over half a year works out to something like **forty-eight per cent** real interest. It is no wonder that so many customers defaulted and so many food plans blew up."
- **The semantic approach.** Accountants deciding "surplus" is a nasty word, replacing it with "retained earnings" or "appreciation of fixed assets"; Standard Oil directors weighing a stock split so "the profits per share do not look so large."

### 5. Does It Make Sense?

"'Does it make sense?' will often cut a statistic down to size when the whole rigmarole is **based on an unproved assumption**."

- **Unproved assumption.** The Rudolf Flesch readability formula "purports to measure how easy a piece of prose is to read, by such simple and objective items as length of words and sentences. Like all devices for reducing the imponderable to a number and substituting arithmetic for judgement, it is an appealing idea." The assumption "that such things as word length determine readability... remains to be proved." Robert A. Dufour tested it: it rated "The Legend of Sleepy Hollow" half again as hard to read as Plato's *Republic*, and Sinclair Lewis's *Cass Timberlane* more difficult than Jacques Maritain's essay "The Spiritual Value of Art." **"A likely story."**
- **False on its face.** "Many a statistic is false on its face. It gets by only because the magic of numbers brings about a suspension of common sense." Leonard Engel's medical examples in *Harper's*: a well-known urologist's calculation of **eight million** US cases of prostate cancer — "enough to provide 1.1 carcinomatous prostate glands for every male in the susceptible age group"; a prominent neurologist's estimate that one American in twelve suffers migraine, which, since migraine causes a third of chronic headache cases, "would mean that a quarter of us must suffer from disabling headaches"; the oft-quoted **250,000** multiple-sclerosis cases when "death data indicate that there can be, happily, no more than thirty to forty thousand."
- **The life-expectancy fallacy.** Social Security hearings were "haunted" by the argument that since life expectancy is only about sixty-three years, a retirement age of sixty-five is a sham "because virtually everybody dies before that." Rebuttals: look around at people you know; and "the basic fallacy... is that the figure refers to **expectancy at birth**, and so about half the babies born can expect to live longer than that." The figure also "came from the 1939–41 period and remained in use long after it was out of date. Presumably the current figure, calculated a generation later, of **69.7** will produce a new and equally silly argument to the effect that practically everybody now lives to be sixty-five."
- **Common sense beating the chart.** A big electrical-appliance company planned small-capacity products — apartment-size refrigerators — on a declining birth rate "taken for granted for a long time. Then one of the planners had an attack of common sense: He came out of his graphs and charts long enough to notice that he and his co-workers and his friends and his neighbours and his former classmates with few exceptions either had three or four children or planned to. This led to some open-minded investigating and charting — and the company shortly turned its emphasis most profitably to big-family models."
- **Impressive precision contradicts common sense.** A study announced that a working woman living with her family needed **$40.13** a week for adequate support. "Anyone who has not suspended all logical processes while reading his paper will realize that the cost of keeping body and soul together cannot be calculated to the last cent. But there is a dreadful temptation; '$40.13' sounds so much more knowing than 'about $40'." Likewise the American Petroleum Industries Committee's average yearly automobile tax bill of **$51.13**.
- **Extrapolation.** "Extrapolations are useful, particularly in that form of soothsaying called forecasting trends. But in looking at the figures or the charts made from them, it is necessary to remember one thing constantly: **The trend-to-now may be a fact, but the future trend represents no more than an educated guess.** Implicit in it is 'everything else being equal' and 'present trends continuing'. And somehow everything else refuses to remain equal, else life would be dull indeed."

## Key Concepts

- **The five questions are a sequence, not a menu.** Source → method → omissions → substitution → plausibility.
- **The Gallup 1948 forecast** was called by government research man Morris Hansen "the most publicized statistical error in human history" — "and it was a paragon of accuracy, however, compared to some of our most widely used estimates of future population."
- **Population forecasting failures.** "As late as 1938 a Presidential commission loaded with experts doubted that the population of the United States would ever reach 140 million; it was 12 million more than that just twelve years later. Yet college textbooks published so recently that they were still in use at that time predicted a peak population of not more than 150 million and figured it would take until about 1980 to reach it." The opposite error, a century earlier: Abraham Lincoln, in his second message to Congress, "predicted the US population would reach 251,689,914 in 1930."

## Mental Models

- **The authority stands behind, not alongside.** Data from a reputable institution do not make the writer's conclusions institutional.
- **Trend-to-now is fact; trend-onward is guess.**
- **Look up from the chart.** The appliance planner's cure was to notice his own neighbours.
- **Round the number back.** If "$40.13" would be absurd as "about $40," the precision is the only thing carrying the claim.

## Anti-patterns

- Accepting a percentage without asking for the raw count behind it.
- Treating a rise in reports, diagnoses, coverage, or definitions as a rise in the underlying phenomenon.
- Extending a curve past its data (the television sets; the Mississippi).
- Quoting an aggregate life expectancy as if it were a maximum age.
- Comparing an interest rate quoted on the original principal with one on the declining balance.
- Letting an O.K. name transfer credibility to conclusions it never endorsed.

## Reference Tables

**The five questions as a working checklist**

| # | Question | What you are hunting | Signature tells |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Who says so?** | conscious and unconscious bias; borrowed authority | interested party; "the survey shows"; an O.K. name standing alongside rather than behind; a favourable year chosen for one comparison and a different one for the next |
| 2 | **How does he know?** | sampling method and adequacy | self-selected sample; low response rate; no n; a correlation with too few cases |
| 3 | **What's missing?** | absent n, error term, range, base, comparison, causal factor | unqualified "average"; percentage with no raw figure; index with no base; a count with no normal-range context |
| 4 | **Did somebody change the subject?** | raw figure → conclusion substitution | reported cases for cases; saying for doing; a redefined unit; component cost for total cost; percentage of one base for another; post hoc |
| 5 | **Does it make sense?** | unproved assumptions and face-absurdity | impossible implied totals; spurious decimals; naive extrapolation; conclusions your own observation contradicts |

## Worked Example

**Two demonstrations Huff runs end to end.**

**A. The Chicago Journal of Commerce survey — questions 1 and 2 applied.**

The published claim: of 169 corporations that replied to a poll on price gouging and hoarding, **two-thirds** declared they were absorbing price increases produced by the police action in the Far East. "'The survey shows,' said the *Journal* (look sharp whenever you meet those words!), 'that corporations have done exactly the opposite of what the enemies of the American business system have charged.'"

*Question 1 — Who says so?* "This is an obvious place to ask, 'Who says so?' since the Journal of Commerce might be regarded as an interested party."

*Question 2 — How does he know?* "It turns out that the Journal had begun by sending its questionnaires to **1,200 large companies. Only fourteen per cent had replied.** Eighty-six per cent had not cared to say anything in public on whether they were hoarding or price gouging."

*Restating the finding honestly against the real denominator:*

| Of 1,200 companies polled | share |
|---|---|
| said they had **not** raised prices | **9%** |
| said they **had** | **5%** |
| **wouldn't say** | **86%** |

"The Journal had put a remarkably good face on things, but the fact remains that there was little to brag about... Those that had replied constituted a sample in which bias might be suspected."

**B. Percentages given, raw figures withheld — question 3 applied three times.**

| Claim | Raw figures | Effect of restoring them |
|---|---|---|
| "Thirty-three and one-third per cent of the women at Johns Hopkins had married faculty members!" — reported by someone "not particularly enamoured of co-education" when the university had just begun admitting women | "There were **three women** enrolled at the time, and **one** of them had married a faculty man." | the shocker evaporates |
| The Boston Chamber of Commerce's American Women of Achievement: of the sixteen also in *Who's Who*, they had "**sixty academic degrees and eighteen children**" | Among them were Dean Virginia Gildersleeve and Mrs Lillian M. Gilbreth. "Those two had a full third of the degrees between them. And Mrs Gilbreth, about whose offspring *Cheaper by the Dozen* was written, supplied two-thirds of the children." | the "informative picture of the group" is a picture of two people |
| A corporation announced its stock was held by **3,003 persons, averaging 660 shares each** — true | "of the two million shares of stock in the corporation **three men held three-quarters** and three thousand persons held the other one-fourth among them" | the mean conceals near-total concentration |

**C. Uncontrolled extrapolation — question 5 applied.**

Television sets in American homes increased "around **10,000 per cent** in one five-year period early in the game. Project this for the next five years and you'd have found that there were about to be a couple of thousand million of the things, Heaven forfend, or **forty sets to the family**. If you want to be sillier yet, begin with a base year even earlier in the telly scheme of things and you can just as well 'prove' that each family will soon have not forty but forty thousand sets."

And the closing quotation, Mark Twain in *Life on the Mississippi* (1874):

> "In the space of one hundred and seventy-six years the Lower Mississippi has shortened itself two hundred and forty-two miles. That is an average of a trifle over one mile and a third per year. Therefore, any calm person, who is not blind or idiotic, can see that in the Old Oölitic Silurian period, just a million years ago next November, the Lower Mississippi River was upward of one million three hundred thousand miles long, and stuck out over the Gulf of Mexico like a fishing-rod. And by the same token any person can see that seven hundred and forty-two years from now the Lower Mississippi will be only a mile and three-quarters long, and Cairo and New Orleans will have joined their streets together, and be plodding comfortably along under a single mayor and a mutual board of aldermen. **There is something fascinating about science. One gets such wholesale returns of conjecture out of such a trifling investment of fact.**"

## Key Takeaways

- Run all five questions, in order, on any statistic that matters: **Who says so? How does he know? What's missing? Did somebody change the subject? Does it make sense?**
- Unconscious bias is more dangerous than conscious bias because nobody is guarding against it — including its author.
- Authority attaches to data, not to the conclusions printed beside them.
- Restore the raw numbers behind every percentage; one-in-three can be one-of-three.
- The commonest single fault is substitution: reports for cases, words for deeds, a component for the whole, one base for another.
- Extrapolation carries two silent clauses — "everything else being equal" and "present trends continuing" — and both routinely fail.
- The goal is not to reject statistics but to "recognize sound and usable data in that wilderness of fraud."
