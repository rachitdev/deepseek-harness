# Chapter 7 — The Semi-attached Figure

## Core Idea

**"If you can't prove what you want to prove, demonstrate something else and pretend that they are the same thing. In the daze that follows the collision of statistics with the human mind, hardly anybody will notice the difference."**

The semi-attached figure is a number that is genuine, often precisely measured, and *attached to a different question from the one being answered*. "The general method is to pick two things that sound the same but are not." Unlike the biased sample or the truncated graph, nothing here is wrong with the measurement — the fault is in the **join** between measurement and claim.

Huff opens with the judge in India: "The government are very keen on amassing statistics — they collect them, add them, raise them to the nth power, take the cube root and prepare wonderful diagrams. But what you must never forget is that every one of those figures comes in the first instance from the chowty dar [village watchman], who just puts down what he damn pleases."

## Frameworks Introduced

**The construction kit for a semi-attached claim.**
1. Measure something adjacent that you *can* measure.
2. Report it in large type, with an impressive source. "Make sure that the laboratory is reputable or has an impressive name. Reproduce the report in full. Photograph a doctor-type model in white clothes and put his picture alongside."
3. Do not mention the gimmicks. "It is not up to you — is it? — to point out that an antiseptic that works well in a test tube may not perform in the human throat, especially after it has been diluted according to instructions to keep it from burning throat tissue."
4. Do not name the mechanism. "Don't confuse the issue by telling what kind of germ you killed. Who knows what germ causes colds, particularly since it probably isn't a germ at all?"

**"So what?" — the one-word test.** Of "27 per cent of a large sample of eminent physicians smoke Throaties — more than any other brand": "The figure itself may be phoney, of course, in any of several ways, but that really doesn't make any difference. **The only answer to a figure so irrelevant is 'So what?'**" Do doctors "have any inside information that permits them to choose the least harmful among cigarettes? Of course they don't."

**"More than what?"** The juice extractor was advertised as extracting "26 per cent more juice," "proved by laboratory test" and "vouched for by Good Housekeeping Institute." Pinned down, it meant only that it got that much more juice **than an old-fashioned hand reamer**. "It had absolutely nothing to do with the data you would want before purchasing; this juicer might be the poorest on the market. Besides being suspiciously precise, that twenty-six per cent figure is totally irrelevant."

**Counts vs rates — get the denominator.** "More people were killed by aeroplanes last year than in 1910. Therefore modern planes are more dangerous? Nonsense. There are hundreds of times more people flying now, that's all." The prescription: "Get the rate, by inquiring into the number of fatalities for each **million passenger miles**. That will come closest to telling you where your greatest risk lies."

**One fact, five presentations.** "There are often many ways of expressing any figure. You can, for instance, express exactly the same fact by calling it
- a one per cent return on sales,
- a fifteen per cent return on investment,
- a ten-million-dollar profit,
- an increase in profits of forty per cent (compared with 1965–9 average), or
- a decrease of sixty per cent from last year.
The method is to choose the one that sounds best for the purpose at hand and trust that few who read it will recognize how imperfectly it reflects the situation."

## Key Concepts

- **The inverting poll.** Princeton's Office of Public Opinion Research asked people whether black people had as good a chance as white people to get jobs, and separately measured prejudice. "People most strongly prejudiced were most likely to answer Yes... about two-thirds of those who were sympathetic towards Negroes did not think the Negro had as good a chance at a job as a white person did, and about two-thirds of those showing prejudice said that blacks were getting as good breaks as whites." Therefore, as prejudice rises, your trend line reports improving job opportunity. "**The worse things get, the better your poll makes them look.**"
- **Undifferentiated complaints relabelled.** A personnel manager surveys employees for any complaint against the union, then reports "a vast majority — 78 per cent — are opposed to the union." "What you have done is to add up a bunch of undifferentiated complaints and tiny gripes and then call them something else that sounds like the same thing." Fair enough, in a way: "The union can just as readily 'prove' that practically all the workers object to the way the plant is being run."
- **Non-comparable groups.** Navy death rate in the Spanish-American War: **9 per thousand**. New York City civilians, same period: **16 per thousand**. Recruiters used this to show it was safer to be in the Navy. "The Navy is made up mainly of young men in known good health. A civilian population includes infants, the old, and the ill, all of whom have a higher death rate wherever they are."
- **Reporting artefacts, not disease.** Three southern states accounted for about **80 per cent** of US influenza and pneumonia cases — because "these three states required reporting of the ailments after other states had stopped doing so." Southern malaria collapsed from hundreds of thousands of cases a year to a handful — because "cases are now recorded only when proved to be malaria, where formerly the word was used in much of the South as a colloquialism for a cold or chill."
- **The counter-intuitive corollary.** For polio, reported cases hit a record while deaths did not. More children at susceptible ages; more diagnosis of mild cases; more polio insurance and charitable aid creating financial incentive to report. "It is an interesting fact that **the death rate or number of deaths often is a better measure of the incidence of an ailment than direct incidence figures** — simply because the quality of reporting and record-keeping is so much higher on fatalities. In this instance, the obviously semi-attached figure is better than the one that on the face of it seems fully attached."
- **Hidden profits.** From the United Automobile Workers' magazine *Ammunition*: "The statement says, last year the company made $75 million in profits. Just one and a half cents out of every sales dollar. You feel sorry for the company. A bulb burns out in the latrine. To replace it, the company has to spend 20 cents. Just like that, there is the profit on 20 sales dollars. Makes a man want to go easy on the paper towels. But, of course, the truth is, what the company reports as profits is only a half or a third of the profits. The part that isn't reported is hidden in depreciation, and special depreciation, and in reserves for contingencies."
- **Not always deliberate.** "Not all semi-attached figures are products of intentional deception. Many statistics, including medical ones that are pretty important to everybody, are distorted by inconsistent reporting at the source."

## Mental Models

- **Test tube ≠ throat.** A germ count in glass says nothing about a cold in a person.
- **Exposure explains the count.** "More accidents occur in clear weather, because there is more clear weather than foggy weather. All the same, fog may be much more dangerous to drive in."
- **Before-and-after is a photograph, not a measurement.** A living room re-photographed after painting — but new furniture was added between exposures, "and sometimes the 'before' picture is a tiny one in poorly lighted black-and-white and the 'after' version is a big photograph in full colour." The hair-rinse pair: "most of the change, you note on careful inspection, has been wrought by persuading her to smile and throwing a back light on her hair. **More credit belongs to the photographer than to the rinse.**"
- **Choose your denominator, choose your verdict.** Same nine-month period, General Motors: **12.6 per cent** profit on sales (after taxes), **44.8 per cent** on investment — "which sounds a good deal worse — or better, depending on what kind of argument you are trying to win."

## Anti-patterns

- Substituting a laboratory proxy for the clinical endpoint.
- Reporting counts where the population at risk has changed.
- Comparing groups with different age and health composition.
- Reading a change in reporting rules as a change in the world.
- Choosing the profit denominator (sales vs investment) that wins the argument, without saying which.
- Before-and-after comparisons in which more than one thing changed.

## Reference Tables

**Attached or semi-attached?**

| Claim made | Figure actually measured | The gap |
|---|---|---|
| Nostrum cures colds | half an ounce killed **31,108 germs in a test tube in eleven seconds** | test tube ≠ throat; dilution; unknown germ; colds probably not bacterial |
| Blacks getting a fairer shake, trend improving | share of respondents answering yes | answers track *prejudice*, not employment |
| Throaties are the doctors' choice | **27%** of eminent physicians smoke them | doctors have no inside knowledge of tobacco harm |
| A better juicer | **26% more juice** than a hand reamer | wrong comparator entirely |
| Driving at 7 a.m. is four times safer than 7 p.m. | "Four times more fatalities occur on the highways at 7 p.m. than at 7 a.m." (approximately true) | more people are on the road in the evening; per-driver risk unknown |
| Rail travel is dangerous | **4,712** deaths chargeable to railroads in one year | nearly half were motorists at crossings, most of the rest were riding the rods; **only 132** were train passengers — and even that needs passenger-miles |
| The company barely profits | **1.1%** net earnings on sales (A&P, defended in *Harper's* letters: "a little over $10 for every $1,000 invested") | annual return on investment ≠ margin on sales. Rebuttal from a later issue: "If I purchase an article every morning for 99 cents and sell it each afternoon for one dollar, I will make only 1 per cent on total sales, but **365 per cent on invested money** during the year." |

## Worked Example

**The 1948 Republican campaign statement on Governor Dewey and teachers' pay.**

The text as issued in October 1948:

> "When Dewey was elected Governor in 1942, the minimum teacher's salary in some districts was as low as $900 a year. Today the school teachers in New York State enjoy the highest salaries in the world. Upon Governor Dewey's recommendation, based on the findings of a Committee he appointed, the Legislature in 1947 appropriated $32,000,000 out of a state surplus to provide an immediate increase in the salaries of school teachers. As a result the minimum salaries of teachers in New York City range from $2,500 to $5,325."

The audit:
1. **Identify the implied comparison.** "Before" = $900. "After" = $2,500 to $5,325. The reader concludes teachers' pay rose roughly threefold to sixfold under Dewey.
2. **Check that the two figures describe the same population.** They do not. "The small figure is the lowest salary in **any rural district of the state**, and the big one is the range in **New York City alone**."
3. **Check the unit.** One is a single floor value; the other is a range of minima.
4. **Check what else changed.** Six years, a war, and general wage inflation sit between the two numbers, unmentioned.
5. **Verdict.** "It is entirely possible that Mr Dewey has proved himself the teacher's friend, but these figures don't show it. It is the old before-and-after trick, with a number of unmentioned factors introduced and made to appear what they are not... There may have been an improvement under Governor Dewey, and there may not."

Huff notes the timing: "In America the semi-attached figure enjoys a big boom every fourth year. This indicates not that the figure is cyclical in nature, but only that campaign time has arrived."

## Key Takeaways

- Ask of every figure: *what exactly was counted, and is it the thing being claimed?*
- "So what?" is a complete refutation of an irrelevant number.
- Insist on the denominator: per passenger-mile, per person at risk, per unit of exposure.
- A change in the counting rules produces a change in the count. Rule out reporting artefacts before believing a trend.
- Where reporting quality is poor, a proxy with good record-keeping (deaths) can beat the "direct" measure.
- Where several legitimate expressions of one fact exist, expect the sponsor to pick the flattering one — so ask for the others.
