# Chapter 2 — The Well-Chosen Average

## Core Idea

"Average" is a word with a very loose meaning. It can denote the **mean**, the **median**, or the **mode** — three different numbers computed from the same data. Choosing among them lets you publish two wildly different figures about the same people, in the same year, and defend both: "Both those figures are legitimate averages, legally arrived at. Both represent the same data, the same people, the same incomes. All the same it is obvious that at least one of them must be so misleading as to rival an out-and-out lie."

The tell is the **unqualified word "average."** Where it appears in a context whose distribution is skewed — above all money — treat the figure as unusable until the kind of average is named.

## Frameworks Introduced

**The three averages.**

| Average | Definition (Huff's wording) | Behaviour on income data |
|---|---|---|
| **Mean** | "the arithmetic average... add up all the incomes and divide by the number there are" | pulled upward by a few large values; the big number |
| **Median** | "half the families in question have more than £2,000 a year and half have less" | the informative one for skewed data |
| **Mode** | "the most frequently met-with figure in a series" | where the crowd actually is |

**The normal-distribution test.** The three averages coincide only when the data "have the grace to fall close to what is called the normal distribution" — the bell curve, where "mean, median, and mode fall at the same point." Heights of men: any average will do. Incomes: they will not. So the decision rule is *first ask whether the underlying distribution is bell-shaped or skewed; only then decide whether the choice of average matters.*

**Skew.** With incomes, "more than ninety-five per cent... would be under £4,000, putting them way over towards the left-hand side of the curve. Instead of being symmetrical, like a bell, it would be skewed. Its shape would be a little like that of a child's slide, the ladder rising sharply to a peak, the working part sloping gradually down. The mean would be quite a distance from the median."

**The whipsaw device.** A company statement engineered so that "the worse the story, the better it looks" — by reclassifying who is inside the averaged group and switching which average is used. See the Worked Example.

**The two questions for any average-pay figure.** *"Average of what? Who's included?"*

## Key Concepts

- **"Nearly everybody is below average."** In the neighbourhood Huff sells you property in, most residents are small farmers, wage earners, or elderly pensioners; three millionaire week-enders "boost the total income, and therefore the arithmetical average, enormously... to a figure that practically everybody in the neighbourhood has a good deal less than." Sounds like a joke; is arithmetic.
- **Mixing average types across years.** "You can see what this would do to the validity of any comparison made between the 'average' (mean) of one year and the 'average' (median) of another."
- **Composition change masquerading as a rate change.** United States Steel once reported average weekly earnings up 107 per cent in less than a decade — true, but the earlier figure "includes a much larger number of partially employed people." "If you work half-time one year and full-time the next, your earnings will double, but that doesn't indicate anything at all about your wage rate."
- **A well-documented average is a usable average.** The Census Bureau's family-income figure comes with everything you need: that the average is a **median**; that "family" means "two or more persons related to each other and living together"; and that the sample size gives **nineteen chances out of twenty** that the estimate is correct within, say, ±$71. "The Census people have both skill enough and money enough... Presumably they have no particular axes to grind." Huff's point is not that averages are useless — it is that *unqualified* averages are.
- **Selective specification as a tell.** *Time* reported new subscribers' "median age is 34 years and their average family income is $7,270"; older readers' "median age was 41 years... Average income was $9,435." Why is the average named for age both times and carefully unspecified for income? "Could it be that the mean was used instead because it is bigger, thus seeming to dangle a richer readership before advertisers?"

## Mental Models

- **The estate agent and the ratepayer.** Same road, same year's data. Selling you the house: "the average income in this neighbourhood is some £10,000 a year" (mean). Petitioning to keep the rates down: "the average income in this neighbourhood is only £2,000 a year" (median). "Am I lying now, or was I lying last year? You can't pin it on me either time. That is the essential beauty of doing your lying with statistics."
- **Overalls for Africans.** Even a good average is not enough for some purposes: if you manufacture clothing you need ranges and deviations, not a central value. (Handed off to Chapter 3.)
- **The proprietor in the payroll.** "Average annual pay of £3,800" can conceal both £1,400 salaries and the owner's profits taken in the form of a whopping salary.

## Anti-patterns

- Publishing "average" with no qualifier where mean and median plainly diverge.
- Averaging owners with workers to make the pay gap disappear.
- Reclassifying profit as salary (bonuses) so it lands in the wage pool.
- Comparing an average across years without checking that the composition of the group and the type of average are unchanged.
- Quoting a mean for income "because it is bigger."

## Reference Tables

**Choosing/auditing an average**

| Situation | Use / demand | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Heights, most physical human characteristics | any — they coincide | approximately normal distribution |
| Incomes, wealth, firm sizes, house prices | **median** (and say so) | markedly skewed; mean is dragged by a long right tail |
| "What is the typical case?" | **mode** | most frequently met-with value |
| Any published "average" of money | ask *which average*, *of whom*, *how measured*, *how accurate* | the four questions Huff attaches to the $6,940 US family-income figure |

## Worked Example

**The three-partner manufacturing company — turning a bad-looking statement into a good one.**

The facts at year end:
- £99,000 paid out to **90 employees**.
- **3 partners** paid themselves **£5,500 each** in salary.
- **£21,000 profit** to be split equally among the three.

*Version 1 — honest and terrible.* Since all employees do similar work for similar pay, mean and median barely differ.

| | |
|---|---|
| Average wage of employees | **£1,100** |
| Average salary and profit of owners | **£12,500** |

"That looks terrible, doesn't it?"

*Version 2 — the whipsaw.* Take £15,000 of the profits and distribute it to the three partners as **bonuses**. Now average up the wages *including yourself and your partners*. And **be sure to use a mean**.

| | |
|---|---|
| Average wage or salary | **£1,403** |
| Average profit of owners | **£2,000** |

"Ah. That looks better." Less than six per cent of the money available for wages and profits has gone into profits — a figure "you can publish, post on a bulletin board, or use in bargaining." Nothing was falsified: the same money went to the same people. Two moves did the work — **redefining the averaged group** and **choosing the mean**.

"This is pretty crude because the example is simplified, but it is nothing to what has been done in the name of accounting. Given a complex corporation with hierarchies of employees ranging all the way from beginning typist to president with a several-hundred-thousand-dollar bonus, all sorts of things can be covered up in this manner."

Huff also prints a distribution chart for the skewed neighbourhood, marking the arithmetical average well above both the median (the one in the middle, twelve above and twelve below) and the mode (the value occurring most often) — the visual companion to "nearly everybody is below average."

## Key Takeaways

- "Average" unqualified is not data. Demand mean/median/mode by name.
- The gap between mean and median *is itself the information* about skew — and is exactly what the manipulator wants hidden.
- Two legitimate averages of one dataset can differ fivefold (£10,000 vs £2,000) with neither being a lie.
- Audit the *membership* of the averaged group as hard as the arithmetic: who was slipped in, who was left out.
- A trustworthy average arrives labelled with its type, its definition of the unit, and its margin of error.
