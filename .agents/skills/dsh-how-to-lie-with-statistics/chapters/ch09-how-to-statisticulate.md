# Chapter 9 — How to Statisticulate

## Core Idea

**"Misinforming people by the use of statistical material might be called statistical manipulation; in a word (though not a very good one), statisticulation."**

This is the catalogue chapter: maps, spurious decimals, percentages, shifting bases, bogus addition, percentage points, percentiles, and index numbers. Its governing insight is that most statisticulation is committed downstream of the statistician. "What comes full of virtue from the statistician's desk may find itself twisted, exaggerated, over-simplified, and distorted-through-selection by salesman, public-relations expert, journalist, or advertising copywriter."

**The one-sidedness test for intent.** Huff was once told by the president of a chapter of the American Statistical Association that the cause is not chicanery much of the time but incompetence. His reply: "False charts in magazines and newspapers frequently sensationalize by exaggeration, **rarely minimize anything**... When has a union employed a statistical worker so incompetent that he made labour's case out weaker than it was? **As long as the errors remain one-sided, it is not easy to attribute them to bungling or accident.**"

**The closing verdict on the discipline.** "The fact is that, despite its mathematical base, **statistics is as much an art as it is a science**. A great many manipulations and even distortions are possible within the bounds of propriety. Often the statistician must choose among methods, a subjective process, and find the one that he will use to represent the facts. In commercial practice he is about as unlikely to select an unfavourable method as a copywriter is to call his sponsor's product flimsy and cheap when he might as well say light and economical. Even the man in academic work may have a bias (possibly unconscious) to favour, a point to prove, an axe to grind."

And the balancing warning: "arbitrarily rejecting statistical methods makes no sense either. That is like refusing to read because writers sometimes use words to hide facts and relationships rather than to reveal them."

## Frameworks Introduced

**The map as a variable-smuggler.** "A map introduces a fine bag of variables in which facts can be concealed and relationships distorted." See the Worked Example.

**The spurious decimal.** "For a spurious air of precision that will lend all kinds of weight to the most disreputable statistic, consider the decimal." Ask a hundred citizens how many hours they slept last night; total 783.1; announce that people sleep an average of **7.831 hours** a night. "You will sound as if you knew precisely what you were talking about. If you had been so foolish as to declare only that people sleep 7.8 (or 'almost 8') hours a night, there would have been nothing striking about it. It would have sounded like what it was, a poor approximation and no more instructive than almost anybody's guess." (Meanwhile "most people will miss their guess by fifteen minutes or more, and there is no assurance that the errors will balance out.")

**Percentage of a tiny n.** The US Department of Labor's *Monthly Labor Review* stated that **4.9 per cent** of offers of part-time household employment with car fare, in Washington D.C. in a specified month, were at $18 a week. "This percentage, it turned out, was based on precisely **two cases**, there having been only **forty-one** offers altogether. Any percentage figure based on a small number of cases is likely to be misleading. **It is more informative to give the figure itself.** And when the percentage is carried out to decimal places you begin to run the scale from the silly to the fraudulent."

**The Battle of the Shifting Base.** A percentage is meaningless until the base is named, and swapping bases mid-argument produces free arithmetic.

**Bogus addition.** Two forms: adding up items that do not belong together, and adding percentages as if they were quantities.

**Percentage vs percentage points.** "If your profits should climb from three per cent on investment one year to six per cent the next, you can make it sound quite modest by calling it a rise of **three percentage points**. With equal validity, you can describe it as a **one hundred per cent increase**. For loose handling of this confusing pair watch particularly the public-opinion pollers."

**Percentiles.** "It means his rank in each one hundred students. In a class of three hundred, for instance, the top three will be at the 99 percentile, the next three at the 98, and so on. The odd thing about percentiles is that **a student with a 99-percentile rating is probably quite a bit superior to one standing at 90, while those at the 40 and 60 percentiles may be of nearly equal achievement.** This comes from the habit that so many characteristics have of clustering about their own average, forming the 'normal' bell curve."

**Index numbers, and the choice of base and averaging method.** "Index numbers are vital matters to millions of people now that wage rates are often tied to them. It is perhaps worth noting what can be done to make them dance to any man's music." See the Worked Example.

## Key Concepts

- **The phoney family.** The Census reported the average family income as **$3,100** (a median, "as of course it should be"); the Russell Sage Foundation's philanthropic-giving story said **$5,004**. Their method: divide total personal income by 149,000,000 to get **$1,251** per person, "which becomes $5,004 in a family of four." Two exaggerations at once: a mean instead of the "smaller and more informative median," and the assumption "that the income of a family is in direct proportion to its size. Now I have four children, and I wish things were disposed in that way, but they are not. Families of four are by no means commonly twice as wealthy as families of two." Huff adds, in fairness, that the Sage statisticians "may be presumed innocent of desire to deceive" — the family figure was a by-product of a study of *giving* — "but it spread its deception no less effectively for that."
- **Marx's decimals.** In figuring the rate of surplus value in a mill, Marx began with "a splendid collection of assumptions, guesses, and round numbers": waste assumed at 6 per cent, raw material "in round numbers £342," spindles "we will assume, £1 per spindle," wear and tear "we put at 10 per cent," rent "we suppose to be £300" — the data "which may be relied upon, were given me by a Manchester spinner." From these he calculates a rate of surplus value of 153 11/33 per cent and, for a ten-hour day, "necessary labour = 3 33/43 hours and surplus-labour = 6..." Huff: "There's a nice feeling of exactness to that two thirty-thirds of an hour, but **it's all bluff**."
- **Adding percentage increases.** From *The New York Times Book Review*: plant and manufacturing expenses "have risen as much as 10 to 12 per cent over the last decade, materials are up 6 to 9 per cent, selling and advertising expenses have climbed upwards of 10 per cent. Combined boosts add up to a minimum of 33 per cent (for one company) and to nearly 40 per cent for some of the smaller houses." Huff: "Actually, if each item making up the cost of publishing this book has risen around ten per cent, **the total cost must have climbed by about that proportion also.** The logic that permits adding those percentage rises together could lead to all sorts of flights of fancy. Buy twenty things today and find that each has gone up five per cent over last year. That 'adds up' to one hundred per cent, and the cost of living has doubled. Nonsense."
- **The rabbit sandwich.** "'Well,' he said, 'I have to put in some horse meat too. But I mix 'em fifty-fifty: one horse, one rabbit.'"
- **Averaging incommensurables.** A union cartoon showed the boss adding one regular hour at £1.50, one overtime hour at £2.25, and one double-time hour at £3 "for an average hourly wage of £2.25. It would be hard to find an instance of an average with less meaning."
- **The school-days proof.** From 365 days subtract 122 for a third of the time in bed, 45 for three hours a day eating; from the remaining 198 take 90 for summer vacation and 21 for Christmas and Easter. "The days that remain are not even enough to provide for Saturdays and Sundays." Too obvious for serious business? The UAW's *Ammunition* says not: "Every time there is a strike, the Chamber of Commerce advertises that the strike is costing so many millions of dollars a day. They get the figure by adding up all the cars that would have been made if the strikers had worked full time. They add in losses to suppliers in the same way. Everything possible is added in, including street car fares and the loss to merchants in sales."
- **A battle of the statisticians is a gift.** "Honest men get a break when statisticulators fall out." The Steel Industry Board caught both sides: the union compared productivity with **1939**, "a year of especially low volume"; the companies, "not to be outdone in the deception derby," compared *money received by employees* rather than average hourly earnings, since "so many workers had been on part time in the earlier year that their incomes were bound to have grown even if wage rates had not risen at all."
- **The honest chart.** Huff reproduces a *Time* chart "as an example of a non-lying chart." Facing two equally valid methods — one favouring management, one labour — "Time simply used both," superimposed, on the same data. In billions of dollars, wages rose about twice as fast as profits and involved perhaps six times as many dollars: inflation looks wage-driven. As percentages of increase, the wage line is flat and the profit line shoots up: inflation looks profit-driven. "You could take your choice of conclusions. Or, perhaps better, you could easily see that neither element could properly be singled out as the guilty one. **It is sometimes a substantial service simply to point out that a subject in controversy is not as open-and-shut as it has been made to seem.**"
- **Semantic escapes.** Accountants deciding "surplus" is a nasty word and proposing "retained earnings" or "appreciation of fixed assets"; Standard Oil directors considering a stock split "if the profits per share do not look so large." And the language jokes Huff closes on: a Florida candidate accusing his opponent of "practising celibacy"; a New York exhibitor quoting the *Times* calling *Quo Vadis* "historical pretentiousness"; Crazy Water Crystals advertising "quick, ephemeral relief."

## Mental Models

- **Percentages do not add; they compound and they need a common base.**
- **To reverse a 50 per cent cut you need a 100 per cent rise.** "All this illustrates why to offset a pay cut of fifty per cent you must get a raise of one hundred per cent."
- **Name the base before you believe the ratio.** In percentage-of-profit calculations "you have a choice of methods (**and you are obligated to indicate which you are using**)."
- **Precision beyond the input's precision is a bluff.** Whenever the output has more significant figures than the inputs could support, that is manipulation.

## Reference Tables

**Percentage traps and their corrections**

| Claim as published | What is wrong | Correct figure |
|---|---|---|
| "Buy your Christmas presents now and **save 100 per cent**" | confusion of base — the saving is 100% of the *new* price | a **50 per cent** reduction |
| Flower growers' president: "flowers are **100 per cent cheaper** than four months ago" | same | not free |
| Ida M. Tarbell, *History of the Standard Oil Company*: price cutting "ranged from **14 to 220 per cent**" | over 100% cut is impossible | "That would call for seller paying buyer a considerable sum to haul the oily stuff away." |
| *Columbus Dispatch*: product sold at a profit of **3,800 per cent**, on cost $1.75 and selling price $40 | method not stated and not either standard one | **2,185 per cent** on cost, or **95.6 per cent** on selling price |
| AP/*New York Times*, Indianapolis: a 5 per cent rise "gave back to the men **one-fourth** of the 20 per cent cut" | increase computed on the post-cut base | $1.00 → 80c; 5% of 80c = 4c = **one-fifth** of the 20c cut |
| *New York Times*: air mail lost through fire 4,863 lb of 7,715,741 lb, "a percentage of but **0.00063**" | arithmetic slip of two orders of magnitude | **0.063 per cent** — "one hundred times as great as the newspaper had it. An insurance company basing its rates in that way could get into a pack of trouble." |
| Hardware jobber: "**50 per cent and 20 per cent off list**" | the 20% is figured on the reduced base | a **60 per cent** discount, not 70 |
| Profit rising from 3% to 6% on investment | percentage vs percentage points | "a rise of three percentage points" **or** "a one hundred per cent increase" — both valid, pick honestly |

## Worked Example

**Two full demonstrations.**

**A. "The Darkening Shadow" — lying with a map.**

Distributed by the First National Bank of Boston and "reproduced very widely — by so-called taxpayers groups, newspapers, and *Newsweek* magazine."

- *What it shows.* The portion of American income taken and spent by the federal government, "by shading the areas of the states west of the Mississippi (excepting only Louisiana, Arkansas, and part of Missouri) to indicate that federal spending has become equal to the total incomes of the people of those states."
- *The deception.* "Choosing states having large areas but, because of sparse population, relatively small incomes." Area is doing the visual work; income is the actual variable.
- *The proof that it is deliberate.* "With equal honesty (and equal dishonesty) the map maker might have started shading in New York or New England and come out with a vastly smaller and less impressive shadow. Using the same data he would have produced quite a different impression... No one would have bothered to distribute that one, though. At least, I do not know of any powerful group that is interested in making public spending appear to be smaller than it is." Huff prints both versions — "Western style" and "Eastern style" — noting of the second, "To show we aren't cheating, we added Md., Del., and R.I. for good measure."
- *The honest alternative.* "He could have chosen a group of in-between states whose total area bears the same relation to the area of the country that their total income does to the national income."
- *Aggravating factor.* It is a chestnut. The same bank published versions for 1929 and 1937, which "shortly cropped up in a standard book, *Graphic Presentation*, by Willard Cope Brinton, as horrible examples. This method 'distorts the facts', said Brinton plainly. But the First National goes right on drawing its maps, and *Newsweek* and other people who should know better — and possibly do — go right on reproducing them with neither warning nor apology."

**B. Making one index prove three different things.**

The data: last year, **milk 10p a quart, bread 10p a loaf**. This year, **milk 5p, bread 20p**. "Now what would you like to prove? Cost of living up? Cost of living down? Or no change?"

| Method | Computation | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **Arithmetic mean, last year = base 100** | milk now 50% of base, bread now 200% of base; (50 + 200) / 2 = **125** | prices **up 25 per cent** |
| **Arithmetic mean, this year = base 100** | milk used to cost 200% of now, bread 50% of now; average = **125** | prices used to be **25 per cent higher** than they are now |
| **Geometric mean, either base** | √(50 × 200) = √10,000 = **100** | **no change at all** |

On the geometric average: "This is a little different from the arithmetic average, or mean, that we have been using but it is a perfectly legitimate kind of figure and in some cases the most useful and revealing. To get the geometric average of three numbers you multiply them together and derive the cube root. For four items, the fourth root; for two, the square root."

Three answers, three legitimate methods, one dataset. Which is why the base period and the averaging method must always be stated.

## Key Takeaways

- Statisticulation is usually committed by the communicator, not the statistician — but the one-sidedness of the errors betrays intent.
- Never accept a percentage without its base; never accept a change without knowing which base the change was computed on.
- Percentages of different bases cannot be added; percentage increases across cost components do not sum.
- Precision beyond the inputs (7.831 hours, £40.13, Marx's thirty-thirds) is a bluff.
- With a small n, give the raw figure; 4.9 per cent of forty-one is two.
- Index numbers dance to the chosen base period and the chosen average — arithmetic mean either way says ±25 per cent; the geometric mean says zero.
- Maps encode area, not the variable of interest.
- Presenting both defensible methods side by side (the *Time* chart) is the honest move.
