# Chapter 4 — Much Ado about Practically Nothing

## Core Idea

Every figure derived from a sample carries an error term. Ignore it and you will treat tiny gaps as real distinctions — the sin of making "much ado about practically nothing." There are two distinct failures, and Huff attacks both:

1. **The difference is not demonstrably real** — it sits inside the probable error, so the ranking is arbitrary (Peter's and Linda's IQs; 35% vs 40% readership).
2. **The difference is real but trivial** — mathematically demonstrable and utterly without consequence (Old Gold cigarettes). "A difference is a difference only if it makes a difference."

The chapter's operating instruction: **think in ranges, not points.** "You must always keep that plus-or-minus in mind, even (or especially) when it is not stated."

## Frameworks Introduced

**Probable error and standard error.** Huff builds the concept physically. Suppose you measure fields by pacing fence lines. First calibrate: pace off what you take to be a hundred yards, many times. Say on the average you are off by three yards — "you came within three yards of hitting the exact one hundred in half your trials, and in the other half of them you missed by more than three yards." Your **probable error** is three yards in one hundred, or three per cent. From then on, a fence line measuring 100 yards by pacing is recorded as **100 ± 3 yards**.

| Measure | Coverage | Note |
|---|---|---|
| **Probable error** | exactly half the cases | the one still used with the Stanford-Binet |
| **Standard error** | about two-thirds of the cases | "Most statisticians now prefer" it; "considerably handier in a mathematical way" |

**The maize-field sampling analogy.** Testing intelligence is "something like what you might do in estimating the quality of the maize in a field by going about and pulling off an ear here and an ear there at random. By the time you had stripped down and looked at a hundred ears, say, you would have gained a pretty good idea of what the whole field was like. Your information would be exact enough for use in comparing this field with another field — **provided the two fields were not very similar.** If they were, you might have to look at many more ears."

**Bands, not points.** "The only way to think about IQs and many other sampling results is in ranges. 'Normal' is not 100, but the range of 90 to 110, say, and there would be some point in comparing a child in this range with a child in a lower or higher range. But comparisons between figures with small differences are meaningless."

**"A difference is a difference only if it makes a difference."** The screening test for a real-but-tiny gap.

## Key Concepts

- **The damned little dots.** Sir Josiah Stamp's story: Lord Randolph, examining a revenue report, was gratified that customs revenue was up 34 per cent on the previous year. His private secretary corrected him — it was **.34 per cent**. "What difference does that make?" asked Randolph. When told one figure was a hundred times the other: "I have often seen those damned little dots before, but I never knew until now what they meant."
- **What an IQ test is not.** "Whatever an intelligence test measures it is not quite the same thing as we usually mean by intelligence. It neglects such important things as leadership and creative imagination. It takes no account of social judgement or musical or artistic or other aptitudes, to say nothing of such personality matters as diligence and emotional balance." The common school tests are "the quick-and-cheap group kind that depend a good deal upon reading facility; bright or not, the poor reader hasn't a chance." Huff's working concession: treat the IQ "simply as a measure of some vaguely defined capacity to handle canned abstractions."
- **The IQ as a sample.** "Now what an IQ test purports to be is a sampling of the intellect. Like any other product of the sampling method, the IQ is a figure with a statistical error, which expresses the precision or reliability of that figure." The Revised Stanford-Binet — individually administered, no reading requirement, "generally regarded as the best of the tests" — has a probable error of **three per cent**. Note carefully what this measures: "This has nothing to do with how good the test is basically, only with how consistently it measures whatever it measures." (Reliability, not validity.)
- **Sample attrition inside a survey.** Readership samples run to a few hundred, "particularly after those who do not read the magazine at all have been eliminated. For a magazine that appeals primarily to women the number of men in the sample may be very small. By the time these have been divided among those who say they 'read all', 'read most', 'read some', or 'didn't read' the article in question, the thirty-five per cent conclusion may be based on only a handful." Editors "to whom readership surveys are gospel, mainly because they do not understand them," demand more articles like the 40% one over the 35% one — "grasping at a thin straw."

## Mental Models

- **Overlapping error bands nullify a ranking.** If two bands overlap substantially, the observed order may simply be reversed in truth.
- **Bottom of a list of near-identical values.** In any list of essentially equal numbers, *something* has to be lowest. That position is a fact about arithmetic, not about quality.
- **Test scores are bands with a ± you were not told.** The plus-or-minus is missing precisely when it would spoil the story.

## Anti-patterns

- Declaring one child brighter, one article more popular, or one product better on a difference smaller than the error term.
- Quoting a point estimate from a sample with no ± attached.
- Confusing **reliability** (consistency of measurement) with **validity** (measuring the thing you care about).
- Reporting a real difference while "excluding all figures and any hint that the difference was negligible" — the Old Gold move.
- Reading "above average" and "below average" as meaningful categories for individuals near the centre.

## Reference Tables

**Peter and Linda — the arithmetic of the probable error**

| | Reported IQ | Probable error (Stanford-Binet, 3%) | Range covering half the trials | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Peter | 98 | ±3 | 95 to 101 | "no more than an even chance" the true value is in this band |
| Linda | 101 | ±3 | 98 to 104 | same |

Consequences Huff draws out: "there is one chance in four that Peter's IQ is really above 101 and a similar chance that Linda's is below 98. Then Peter is not inferior but superior, and by a margin of anywhere from three points up."

## Worked Example

**Old Gold: how to build a national advertising campaign on a negligible difference.**

1. **The origin.** The editor of *Reader's Digest*, "who smokes cigarettes but takes a dim view of them all the same," had a battery of laboratory people analyse the smoke of several brands.
2. **The finding.** The magazine published the nicotine and other content by brand. "The conclusion stated by the magazine and borne out in its detailed figures was that all the brands were virtually identical and that it didn't make any difference which one you smoked."
3. **The exploit.** "In the lists of almost identical amounts of poisons, one cigarette had to be at the bottom, and the one was Old Gold."
4. **The execution.** "Out went the telegrams, and big advertisements appeared in newspapers at once in the biggest type at hand. The headlines and the copy simply said that of all cigarettes tested by this great national magazine Old Gold had the least of these undesirable things in its smoke. **Excluded were all figures and any hint that the difference was negligible.**"
5. **The outcome.** "In the end, the Old Gold people were ordered to 'cease and desist' from such misleading advertising. That didn't make any difference; the good had been milked from the idea long before."

Note the structure: no false statement anywhere. The lie is entirely in the **suppression of the magnitudes** and in the implicature that a rank order implies a meaningful gap. Compare the readership case, where the fault is the opposite — a difference (35% vs 40%) that "can be of importance to a magazine" but "may not be a real one."

## Key Takeaways

- Attach a ± to every sample-derived number, supplied or not.
- Two questions, always in this order: *Is the difference real?* then *Is the difference big enough to matter?*
- Probable error covers half the cases; standard error about two-thirds. Neither is the whole story, and neither is usually printed.
- Reliability ≠ validity. A test can measure something with great consistency and still not measure what its name claims.
- A rank order among statistically indistinguishable items is a free advertising slogan.
- Watch the decimal point. ".34 per cent" and "34 per cent" differ by a hundredfold and by one damned little dot.
