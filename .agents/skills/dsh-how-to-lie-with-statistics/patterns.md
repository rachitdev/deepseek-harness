# Patterns — How to Lie with Statistics

Huff's reusable moves, stated as **When / How / Trade-offs**. Read them in both directions: as the manipulator's playbook (the book's literary device) and as the reader's detection kit (its serious purpose). "The crooks already know these tricks; honest men must learn them in self-defence."

---

## Part I — Patterns of the Sample

### P1. Interrogate the universe before the number
**When** any figure describes a large group — a nation, a class, a market, a profession.
**How** Name the universe the figure claims to describe. Name the frame actually sampled. List who is in the first but could not be in the second (unreachable, unlisted, unwilling). Apply the random-sample test: *does every name or thing in the whole group have an equal chance to be in the sample?*
**Trade-offs** True random samples are "so difficult and expensive to obtain for many uses that sheer cost eliminates it." You will almost never find one; the point is to know what compromise you are reading, not to reject everything.

### P2. Assume non-responders differ, and guess the direction
**When** a response rate is disclosed — or conspicuously is not.
**How** Ask what would make a person *not* answer this particular question. Re-project the result on the assumption that the silent share resembles the reason they were silent. (Huff's re-projection of the Poling conversion survey: assume the 90% who did not reply had no conversions to report; 4,144,366 becomes ~370,000.)
**Trade-offs** Your counter-projection is also a guess. Its value is in showing the published figure's fragility, not in replacing it.

### P3. Separate what people say from what people do
**When** the variable is self-reported: income, reading, tooth-brushing, bathing, sexual behaviour, age.
**How** Restate the finding using the verb the method supports: not "British men bathe more often than women" but "British men *say* they do." Where possible, find a behavioural trace instead — counting old magazines in a house rather than asking which are read. Ask for birth dates, not ages.
**Trade-offs** Even behavioural traces are proxies: counting magazines "does not tell you what people read, only what they have been exposed to."

### P4. Read the direction of poll bias
**When** any street, doorstep, or quota-sampled survey.
**How** Assume drift toward "the person with more money, more education, more information and alertness, better appearance, more conventional behaviour, and more settled habits." Also assume respondents give the pleasing answer, and that the interviewer's identity shifts the result.
**Trade-offs** This is a prior, not a correction factor. It tells you which way to be sceptical, not by how much.

## Part II — Patterns of the Summary Number

### P5. Demand the average by name
**When** you meet "average" unqualified.
**How** Ask: mean, median, or mode? Then ask whether the distribution is bell-shaped or skewed. If skewed, the choice matters and the omission is the message. Ask also *average of what, and who's included?*
**Trade-offs** For genuinely normal data (heights of a tribe) the question is pedantry. Spend the scepticism where the distribution has a tail.

### P6. Reconstruct the range
**When** a single central value is used to plan, compare, or reassure.
**How** Ask for the minimum, maximum, and spread. Test whether decisions built on the average survive at the edges. (61°F mean: San Nicolas 47–87, the desert 15–104.)
**Trade-offs** Ranges are driven by outliers, so pair them with a percentile picture where you can get one.

### P7. Attach a plus-or-minus, supplied or not
**When** comparing two sample-derived figures.
**How** Find or estimate the error term, form bands, and check for overlap. Only then rank. Then apply the second filter: *is the difference big enough to matter?*
**Trade-offs** Probable error covers half the cases, standard error about two-thirds — neither is a guarantee, and a lay reader "cannot apply tests of significance." A good long glance at n often settles it anyway.

### P8. Restore the raw numbers behind a percentage
**When** a percentage is quoted without an n, especially a startling one.
**How** Multiply back. One-third of the women at Johns Hopkins is one of three. 4.9% of household job offers is two of forty-one. Sixty degrees among sixteen women is two women holding a third of them.
**Trade-offs** None to speak of — Huff's own rule is that with small numbers "it is more informative to give the figure itself."

## Part III — Patterns of the Picture

### P9. Audit the axis
**When** any line or bar chart of levels.
**How** Find the vertical axis; check that it reaches zero or that a break is clearly marked. Read top and bottom labels. Compute the true percentage change. Compare it with the slope you perceived; the ratio is the exaggeration. Redraw with a zero baseline — the *Dun's Review* rebuttal.
**Trade-offs** Truncation is legitimate for rates, deviations, and changes, and sometimes necessary for resolution. The offence is an *unmarked* break on a chart of levels.

### P10. Apply the cube law to any picture-chart
**When** quantities are drawn as objects — moneybags, people, furnaces, animals, coins.
**How** Compute the stated ratio. Measure the drawn height ratio. Square it for page area, cube it for the implied solid. Check for independent horizontal stretching, which multiplies again.
**Trade-offs** Pictographs genuinely help lay readers. The honest form exists: repeat an identical icon and vary the count.

### P11. Ask what the map is actually encoding
**When** a quantity is shaded onto geography.
**How** Identify the visual variable (usually area) and the data variable (usually people or money). Ask whether the selected regions were chosen to maximise the mismatch. Construct the opposite-leaning selection from the same data and see how different it looks.
**Trade-offs** Some geographic questions really are about area. Most are not.

## Part IV — Patterns of the Join

### P12. Test the attachment ("So what?")
**When** an impressive number is offered in support of a claim.
**How** State precisely what was counted. State precisely what is being claimed. Ask whether the first entails the second. If it plainly does not, "the only answer to a figure so irrelevant is 'So what?'"
**Trade-offs** Proxies are sometimes all that exists; a semi-attached figure honestly labelled as a proxy is legitimate evidence (Huff's own point that deaths can beat reported cases).

### P13. Ask "more than what?"
**When** a comparative percentage appears with no comparator named.
**How** Demand the baseline. "26 per cent more juice" — than a hand reamer. "Triples the hardness of steel" — than what steel?
**Trade-offs** None; the question always has an answer, and refusal to give it is itself the finding.

### P14. Get the denominator
**When** counts of accidents, deaths, cases, or sales are compared across time or place.
**How** Convert to a rate over the population at risk or exposure: per passenger-mile, per person, per hour driven. Ask whether the compared groups are demographically comparable (Navy recruits vs a whole civilian population).
**Trade-offs** Rates require exposure data that is often unavailable — which is exactly why counts get published.

### P15. Rule out reporting artefacts before believing a trend
**When** a measured quantity jumps or collapses.
**How** Ask whether the definition, the diagnostic practice, the legal reporting requirement, the incentive to report, or the enumerating body changed. Cross-check with a measure whose recording quality is stable (deaths, official police records).
**Trade-offs** Sometimes both are true — a real change and a measurement change — and separating them needs work the report never did.

### P16. Name the base of every percentage, and never add them
**When** discounts, cuts and raises, profit margins, or cost-component increases are combined.
**How** Write down each base explicitly. Compound rather than sum. Remember a 20% cut needs a 25% rise to reverse it; a 50% cut needs 100%. State whether profit is on cost or on selling price — "you are obligated to indicate which you are using."
**Trade-offs** None. This is arithmetic hygiene.

## Part V — Patterns of Causation

### P17. Classify the correlation before speaking of cause
**When** a relationship is reported.
**How** Assign it to one of Huff's three types: (1) chance, (2) real with unknown direction, (3) real with neither variable acting on the other. Then run four rival explanations: chance, reverse causation, a third factor, and the trend of the times. Only if all four are disposed of may causal language be used.
**Trade-offs** You will often end with "cannot tell." That *is* the finding — "it is sometimes a substantial service simply to point out that a subject in controversy is not as open-and-shut as it has been made to seem."

### P18. Check the trend of the times first
**When** an interested party makes a fuss about a correlation.
**How** Ask whether both series were rising (or falling) anyway, along with everything else in the period. Deflate, detrend, or find a period where the two moved differently.
**Trade-offs** Detrending can remove real long-run effects along with the artefact.

### P19. Distinguish cohort from ageing, cross-section from longitudinal
**When** a difference between age groups is read as a change over a lifetime.
**How** Ask whether the same individuals — "or possibly equivalent groups" — were followed over time. If not, look for what was different about when each group grew up.
**Trade-offs** Longitudinal study is slow and expensive; it is also the only thing that settles the question.

### P20. Watch for selection into the treatment
**When** an outcome is credited to an intervention people chose.
**How** Ask who takes the treatment. Colleges "get a disproportionate number of two groups of kids: the bright and the rich." Both would have out-earned the average anyway.
**Trade-offs** Adjusting for selection needs data the study rarely collected; be explicit that you are flagging, not correcting.

### P21. Keep the correlation inside its range, and out of individual cases
**When** a fitted relationship is projected further, or applied to one person.
**How** Note the observed range and refuse to extend past it (rain and corn reverse). Note that "a correlation may be real and based on real cause and effect — and still be almost worthless in determining action in any single case."
**Trade-offs** Refusing extrapolation entirely forfeits forecasting, which is useful. State the assumptions instead: "everything else being equal" and "present trends continuing."

## Part VI — Patterns of Defence

### P22. Run the five questions in order
**When** any statistic that will inform a decision.
**How** *Who says so? How does he know? What's missing? Did somebody change the subject? Does it make sense?* Source, then method, then omissions, then substitution, then plausibility.
**Trade-offs** It is a screen, not an analysis: "Not all the statistical information that you may come upon can be tested with the sureness of chemical analysis."

### P23. Check that the authority stands behind, not alongside
**When** a reputable institution is named near a conclusion.
**How** Separate the data from the interpretation and ask which party supplied each. In the Cornell marriage piece, "while the statistics are Cornell's the conclusions are not."
**Trade-offs** Institutions also publish bad conclusions; the O.K. name test is necessary, not sufficient.

### P24. Round the number back and see if it still impresses
**When** a figure carries improbable precision — $40.13, $51.13, 7.831 hours, 153 11/33 per cent, $25,111.
**How** Restate it at the precision the inputs justify ("about $40"). If the claim loses all its force, the precision was the claim.
**Trade-offs** Some quantities genuinely are known exactly. Ask how they could have been measured that finely.

### P25. Look up from the chart
**When** a projection contradicts what you can observe directly.
**How** Test the number against your own experience and simple implied totals. Eight million prostate cancers implies 1.1 per susceptible male. A declining birth rate implied small refrigerators; the planner's neighbours all had three or four children.
**Trade-offs** Personal experience is itself a biased sample (Ch 1). Use it to raise a flag, then go get the data.

### P26. Publish both defensible methods
**When** you are the one presenting, and two legitimate methods favour opposite sides.
**How** Show both, on the same data, as *Time* did with wages and profits in dollars and in percentage change — Huff's "example of a non-lying chart."
**Trade-offs** It forfeits rhetorical force. It buys the reader the ability to judge, which is the whole point of the exercise.
