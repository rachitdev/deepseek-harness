# Glossary — How to Lie with Statistics (Darrell Huff)

Terms as Huff uses them, with the chapter where each is introduced or chiefly discussed.

**A & P fallacy** — Defending low profitability by quoting return on sales when return on investment is the relevant measure; 1.1% on sales can be 365% on invested money (Ch 7).

**Abscissa** — The horizontal axis of a graph. Changing its proportion to the ordinate is the second gee-whiz trick (Ch 5).

**Adding percentages** — Summing percentage increases across cost components as if they were quantities; if every item rises ~10%, the total rises ~10%, not the sum of the parts (Ch 9).

**Armchair correspondent** — A journalist who passes numbers along "uncritically re-writing government handouts" without asking what they mean (Ch 3).

**Available** — A weasel word: electric power "available to more than three-quarters of U.S. farms" may mean lines pass within a hundred miles (Ch 3).

**Average, unqualified** — The single most common concealment device; can denote mean, median or mode (Ch 2).

**Bar chart** — "The daddy of the pictorial chart"; honest when bars are of equal width and untruncated (Ch 6).

**Base (of a percentage)** — The quantity a percentage is computed against. Undeclared or shifted bases produce most percentage nonsense (Ch 9).

**Base period** — The reference period of an index number, set to 100. Choosing it decides the answer (Ch 9).

**Bell curve** — See Normal distribution (Ch 2).

**Before-and-after trick** — Comparing two figures or photographs that differ in more than the one factor being credited (Ch 7).

**Bias, built-in** — Bias produced by the selection method rather than by intent; it "can rig [a poll] automatically" (Ch 1).

**Bias, conscious** — Direct misstatement, ambiguous statement, selection of favourable data, suppression of unfavourable, shifted units of measurement (Ch 10).

**Bias, invisible** — Bias you cannot demonstrate but must still allow for: "There always is" (Ch 1).

**Bias, unconscious** — Bias in the analyst's own framing; "often more dangerous" — as in the economic charts of 1928 (Ch 10).

**Chowty dar** — The village watchman of the Indian judge's story, who "just puts down what he damn pleases" — the ultimate source of every collected figure (Ch 7).

**Cohort effect** — A difference between age groups caused by when they grew up, not by ageing; Helen M. Walker's toe-out illustration (Ch 8).

**Confusion of base** — "Save 100 per cent" for a 50% reduction; the saving is 100% of the *new* price (Ch 9).

**Control group** — The untreated comparison group; absent from the Doakes' toothpaste claim, present but useless in the undersized polio trial (Ch 3).

**Correlation** — A measured tendency of two variables to move together; "not proof that one has caused the other" (Ch 8).

**Correlation, chance** — A relationship produced by the operation of chance in a small sample and published because it came out right (Ch 8).

**Correlation, negative** — "As one variable increases the other tends to decrease" (Ch 8).

**Correlation, spurious (nonsense)** — A real statistical relationship with no causal link either way; Presbyterian ministers' salaries and the price of rum in Havana (Ch 8).

**Death rate as proxy** — Where diagnosis and reporting are unreliable, deaths may measure incidence better than reported cases (Ch 7).

**Decimal, spurious** — Extra decimal places lending "a spurious air of precision" to inexact data; 7.831 hours of sleep (Ch 9).

**Deviation** — Spread around an average; one of the "little figures that are not there" (Ch 3).

**Diminishing rhinoceros** — Huff's illustration of a shrinking population drawn as a shrinking animal (Ch 6).

**Discount stacking** — "50 per cent and 20 per cent off list" is 60% off, not 70%, because the second cut applies to the reduced base (Ch 9).

**Extrapolation** — Projecting a trend beyond the data; "the trend-to-now may be a fact, but the future trend represents no more than an educated guess" (Ch 10).

**Eye-appeal** — The visual attractiveness that motivates pictographs and licenses their distortion (Ch 6).

**Firsters** — Claimants to being "first" in some quietly chosen category (Ch 10).

**Gee-whiz graph** — A line graph with the baseline chopped off so a small change fills the frame (Ch 5).

**Geometric average** — The nth root of the product of n values; with milk at 50 and bread at 200, √10,000 = 100, "prices have not gone up or down" (Ch 9).

**Gesell's norms** — Developmental averages that, published without ranges, convince half of all parents their child is behind (Ch 3).

**Index number** — A figure expressing a level relative to a base period; manipulable through base choice and averaging method (Ch 9).

**IQ** — Treated by Huff as "a measure of some vaguely defined capacity to handle canned abstractions"; a sample of the intellect and therefore a figure with a statistical error (Ch 4).

**Law of averages** — "Only when there is a substantial number of trials involved is the law of averages a useful description or prediction" (Ch 3).

**Life expectancy at birth** — An average, not a ceiling; about half of babies born can expect to exceed it (Ch 10).

**Literary Digest error** — The 1936 poll of ten million telephone and magazine subscribers that elected Landon; shorthand for a sampling frame biased toward the affluent (Ch 1).

**Little figures that are not there** — Sample size, significance, probable error, range, base — the omissions that make a published figure unauditable (Ch 3).

**Mean (arithmetic average)** — Add all values, divide by the count; inflated by a skewed tail (Ch 2).

**Median** — The middle value: half above, half below; the informative average for skewed data (Ch 2).

**Mode** — "The most frequently met-with figure in a series" (Ch 2).

**Non-response** — Failure to reply to a survey; non-responders systematically differ from responders (Ch 1).

**Normal** — A statement of frequency, not of merit. "Confusing 'normal' with 'desirable' makes it all the worse" (Ch 3).

**Normal distribution** — The bell curve, where "mean, median, and mode fall at the same point" (Ch 2).

**O.K. name** — Stephen Potter's term, adopted by Huff: an authoritative-sounding institution cited alongside a claim it never made (Ch 10).

**One-dimensional picture** — A pictograph scaled in height to represent a quantity, thereby delivering the square on the page and the cube in the mind (Ch 6).

**Ordinate** — The vertical axis. Rescaling it is the trick "worth a dozen" of truncation (Ch 5).

**Passenger mile** — The exposure denominator that makes transport fatality figures comparable (Ch 7).

**Percentage points vs per cent** — 3% to 6% is a rise of three percentage points *or* a hundred per cent increase; both valid, one chosen (Ch 9).

**Percentile** — Rank per hundred. Gaps near the extremes mean far more than gaps near the middle, because of clustering about the average (Ch 9).

**Phoney family** — The Russell Sage Foundation's $5,004 "family income," made by multiplying a per-capita mean by four (Ch 9).

**Pictograph (pictorial chart)** — A chart using drawn objects as units; honest only when the icon is repeated, never resized (Ch 6).

**Post hoc ergo propter hoc** — "If B follows A, then A has caused B"; the chapter-8 fallacy (Ch 8).

**Probable error** — The error band covering exactly half the trials; the Stanford-Binet's is three per cent (Ch 4).

**Random sample** — A sample selected by pure chance from the universe; "the only kind that can be examined with entire confidence by means of statistical theory" (Ch 1).

**Random-sample test** — "Does every name or thing in the whole group have an equal chance to be in the sample?" (Ch 1).

**Range** — Lowest to highest observed value; Oklahoma City's 60.2° mean conceals 130 degrees of it (Ch 3).

**Rate vs count** — A count rises when exposure rises; the rate is what you want (Ch 7).

**Readability formula (Flesch)** — Huff's specimen of "reducing the imponderable to a number and substituting arithmetic for judgement," on an unproved assumption (Ch 10).

**Reliability vs validity** — The Stanford-Binet's probable error concerns "how consistently it measures whatever it measures," "nothing to do with how good the test is basically" (Ch 4).

**Reporting artefact** — An apparent change in the world produced by a change in who records what; malaria in the American South, influenza in three states (Ch 7).

**Respondents** — "As people are called when they answer such questions" (Ch 1).

**Sampling, three levels of** — The sample of the population, the sample of possible questions, and the sample of a person's attitudes given in an answer (Ch 1).

**Self-selection** — Sample members choosing themselves in or out; the metric-system coupon poll, the questionnaire about questionnaires (Ch 1, Intro).

**Semi-attached figure** — A genuine measurement attached to a different question from the one being answered (Ch 7).

**Shifting base, Battle of the** — Computing a decrease on one base and the offsetting increase on another; a 20% cut needs a 25% rise to undo, a 50% cut needs 100% (Ch 9).

**Significance, test of** — "A way of reporting how likely it is that a test figure represents a real result rather than something produced by chance" (Ch 3).

**Skewed distribution** — An asymmetric distribution "a little like that of a child's slide"; mean and median diverge (Ch 2).

**"So what?"** — The complete refutation of an irrelevant figure (Ch 7).

**Standard error** — The error band covering about two-thirds of cases; preferred by most statisticians and "considerably handier in a mathematical way" (Ch 4).

**Statisticulation** — Huff's coinage: "misinforming people by the use of statistical material" (Ch 9).

**Stratified random sampling** — Dividing the universe into groups in proportion to known prevalence and sampling within each; the economical substitute used in polling and market research (Ch 1).

**"The survey shows"** — A phrase to "look sharp" at whenever you meet it (Ch 10).

**Third factor** — The unmentioned cause of two correlated variables; storks and babies, big houses and chimney pots (Ch 8).

**Trend of the times** — The secular rise or fall that correlates everything with everything; the first alternative to check (Ch 8).

**Truncation** — Removing the lower portion of a chart's vertical axis (Ch 5).

**Universe** — "A word by which the statistician means the whole of which the sample is a part" (Ch 1).

**Whipsaw device** — A presentation in which "the worse the story, the better it looks," built by reclassifying group membership and choosing the flattering average (Ch 2).

**Yellow journalism (graphic)** — Huff's label for sensationalising facts through distorted pictures (Ch 6).
