# Patterns — Quick and Easy Music Theory (Lloyd Steiner, 2009)

The book's reusable moves, grouped by the part of the workflow they belong to. Each is
stated as **When / How / Trade-offs**.

---

## Part 1 — Spelling and Scale Construction

### Walk the formula
**When** you need any scale from any root.
**How** Write the W/H formula down. Put the root in slot 1. Move one interval at a
time on a piano diagram, filling slots 2 through 8. Slot 8 must be the same letter as
slot 1.
**Trade-offs** Slow but never wrong. Steiner recommends writing the formula out every
time until it is memorised; there is no shortcut offered.

### Run the five-rule checklist
**When** any scale is finished.
**How** Tick off: (1) every letter used, (2) letters in order, (3) no repeated letters
except the start/end note, (4) no flats if there are sharps, (5) no sharps if there are
flats.
**Trade-offs** Catches the two errors beginners actually make — skipped letters and
mixed accidentals. It cannot catch a miscounted interval (see the Pop Quiz answer "C",
which passes all five rules but is the wrong distance).

### Pre-commit to sharps or flats
**When** the root already carries an accidental, or you can see the scale will be
black-key heavy.
**How** Before building, decide from rules 4/5 which accidental type the scale will use,
then apply it to every black key you hit.
**Trade-offs** Turns a post-hoc check into a forward constraint and removes most
decisions. Fails if you misjudge the root; re-run the checklist to catch it.

### Order scales by accidental count
**When** you want to internalise key relationships.
**How** Learn the sequence C, G, D, A, E, B, F# (increasing sharps) and F, Bb, Eb, Ab,
Db, Gb (increasing flats).
**Trade-offs** Gives you the circle of fifths empirically without the book naming it.
Steiner leaves it as homework rather than teaching the theory behind it.

## Part 2 — Chord Construction

### Alphabet check before counting
**When** building any chord.
**How** Write the letters from the root, number them 1–5 (triads) or 1–7 (sevenths),
and read off the slots the chord uses. Those letters are now fixed.
**Trade-offs** Removes every enharmonic decision in advance. Cost: it forces you to
write E#, B#, Cb, Fb, and double accidentals. Steiner's position is that this cost is
non-negotiable.

### Count half steps interval by interval, not from the root
**When** filling in the notes after the alphabet check.
**How** Count root → third, then **from the third** → fifth, then from the fifth →
seventh. Each formula gives consecutive gaps, not distances from the root.
**Trade-offs** Easy to get wrong if you assume cumulative measurement. Nothing in the
formulas is measured from the root except the first interval.

### Re-spell the note, never the chord
**When** the counted pitch has an obvious name that is the wrong letter.
**How** Keep the pitch, change the written name: B → Cb, E → Fb, A → Bbb, G → Fx.
**Trade-offs** Produces spellings that look absurd on a piano ("Is it practical to
always refer to the letter E as Fb? Absolutely not!") but keeps the chord identifiable.
"A chord spelled as F, Ab, and B is no longer an F diminished chord."

### Build the triad, then add
**When** constructing sevenths, ninths, or 7sus4 chords.
**How** Recognise the triad in the first two interval numbers (4-3 major, 3-4 minor,
3-3 diminished, 4-4 augmented), build it, then take the last interval.
**Trade-offs** Much faster than counting four intervals from scratch, and makes the
compound names ("minor chord + major seventh") self-explanatory.

### Stack odd slots for extensions
**When** you need a 9th, 11th or 13th.
**How** Number the letters continuously past the octave and take slots 1, 3, 5, 7, 9,
11, 13.
**Trade-offs** Trivially simple, but the book stops there — it does not distinguish
dominant 9 from major 9 from minor 9, and defers the whole area to jazz study.

## Part 3 — Harmonising a Key

### Rotate the scale to find diatonic triads
**When** you want the legal chord set for any key.
**How** Write the seven scale notes. Renumber so each note in turn is slot 1. Read slots
3 and 5. Name the resulting triad and assign a Roman numeral by case.
**Trade-offs** No half-step counting needed — the scale has already fixed the spellings.
Only works if the scale itself is spelled correctly.

### Use the positional quality pattern
**When** you already trust the pattern and want speed.
**How** Apply the memorised pattern for the mode:
- Major: **I ii iii IV V vi viiº**
- Natural minor: **i iiº III iv v VI VII**
- Harmonic minor: **i iiº III iv V VI VII**
**Trade-offs** One-time memorisation replaces per-key derivation for all 13 keys. Risk:
applying the major pattern in a minor key.

### Raise the third of V only
**When** a minor-key progression needs a real tension-and-resolution ending.
**How** Take the natural minor triads, sharp the third of the v chord by one half step,
leave everything else alone.
**Trade-offs** The smallest possible change that fixes the weak dominant, and it keeps
the minor character. The rule is easy to over-apply: "Do not substitute the altered note
in any other chords."

### Filter every choice against the seven
**When** composing, soloing or singing in a key.
**How** "If the note isn't listed, it doesn't work in the key! If the chord isn't
listed, it doesn't work in the key!"
**Trade-offs** Guarantees things sound good; excludes borrowed chords, modal mixture and
chromaticism entirely. That is a deliberate beginner-scope decision, not an oversight.

## Part 4 — Communication

### Write it in figured bass
**When** you and the other player both know the scales and diatonic triads.
**How** State the key, then the Roman numerals in order: `E Major: I vi iii IV V I`.
**Trade-offs** Maximally compact and transposes for free — the numerals stay, only the
chord letters change. Useless without the key, and it requires real study from both
parties.

### Write it in chord abbreviations
**When** you want the widest audience with the least explanation.
**How** Capital letter for the root plus a suffix: none = major, `m` = minor,
`dim`/`º` = diminished, `aug`/`+` = augmented, `7` = dominant seventh,
`sus2`/`sus4`/`sus6` = suspended. Suffixes stack (`C#maj7sus4`).
**Trade-offs** "More popular nowadays than figured bass because they require less
information." No key needed. But the progression does not transpose — every letter must
be rewritten.

### Avoid the "Mommy, please hold my hand" method
**When** you catch yourself dictating chords note by note.
**How** Replace it with either of the two notations above.
**Trade-offs** Longhand is unambiguous to a complete beginner and hopeless at band
practice. "Unfortunately, Mommy can't come to band practice."

### Say letter and quality, always
**When** speaking to any other musician.
**How** "C major chord," "B minor chord," "G augmented chord," "E diminished chord."
**Trade-offs** None. Steiner is emphatic: "THERE IS NO SUCH THING AS A D CHORD, OR A B
CHORD, OR MINOR CHORD!" — the phrasing is a competence signal others read instantly.

## Part 5 — Arrangement and Composition

### Track voices in three rows
**When** arranging any progression.
**How** Lay the chords out as columns and Bottom / Middle / Top as rows; follow each
voice horizontally.
**Trade-offs** Makes common tones and unnecessary movement visible at a glance. Scales
to more voices (Chapter 27 uses nine rows) but gets crowded.

### Hold common tones, move to the nearest letter
**When** choosing inversions.
**How** Identify what two adjacent chords share, keep those notes in the same voice, and
move whatever is left the shortest distance.
**Trade-offs** Produces smooth, professional-sounding voice leading and automatically
generates the inversions. There is no single right answer — "There are many
possibilities for arrangement."

### Let the bass define the inversion
**When** distributing parts to a band.
**How** Assign the bass line first, strictly following the slash notes (root where no
inversion is given). Everyone above may play any chord tones in any order.
**Trade-offs** One player carries the harmonic responsibility; the rest gain freedom.
"The bass (and only the bass) will determine the inversion of the chord."

### Constrain the singer to chord tones
**When** writing a vocal line.
**How** For each chord, list its three (or four) notes; those are the only legal
options. On a D major chord: D, F#, or A. "All other notes are sour."
**Trade-offs** A hard guarantee against out-of-tune writing; also rules out passing
tones and non-chord melodic notes, which the book does not cover.

### End with a known cadence
**When** finishing a piece.
**How** Authentic **V → I** for the strongest close; plagal **IV → I** for a hymn-like
close; deceptive **V → vi** to fake an ending mid-song. Start beginner compositions on I.
**Trade-offs** Three formulas cover centuries of practice — "tried, tested, and approved
for your use." Predictable by design; the deceptive cadence works *because* the others
are predictable.

### Study by listening
**When** you want to hear the whole system in operation.
**How** Listen to J.S. Bach, Beethoven, Mozart, Handel, Vivaldi and Haydn.
**Trade-offs** Steiner's only prescribed listening. It reflects his stated fields —
Classical, Rock/Metal, Pop — and leaves jazz, where the extended chords of Chapter 17
live, entirely to the reader.
