## Core Frameworks & Mental Models

**The five rules for building scales** — the book's spine. Applied unchanged to every
scale type: (1) use every letter of the musical alphabet, (2) all letters in order,
(3) no repeated letters except the starting note, (4) sharps → no flats, (5) flats → no
sharps. These rules, not the ear, decide every C#-or-Db question in the book.

**The alphabet check** — before counting a single half step, write the letters out from
the root and number them 1–5 (triads) or 1–7 (sevenths). The slots the formula names
lock the *letters*. Only then do you count half steps, and the accidental is whatever
makes the locked letter hit the counted pitch — including Cb, Fb, B#, E#, double flats
and double sharps. Corollary: **re-spell the note, never the chord.** "A chord spelled
as F, Ab, and B is no longer an F diminished chord."

**Everything is a W/H or half-step formula.** Scales: major `W W H W W W H`, natural
minor `W H W W H W W`, melodic minor ascending `W H W W W W H`. Chords: the four triads
are the four permutations of 3 and 4 half steps — major (4)(3), minor (3)(4), augmented
(4)(4), diminished (3)(3) — and every seventh, sus and extended chord adds one more
interval to that spine. Each interval is measured from the *previous* note, not the root.

**The key as a whitelist.** Harmonise a scale by rotating it — use each of the seven
notes in turn as slot 1 and read slots 3 and 5 — and you get seven chords that are the
complete legal vocabulary for that key. Steiner's decision rule, repeated after every
chord: **"If the note isn't listed, it doesn't work in the key! If the chord isn't
listed, it doesn't work in the key!"** Diatonic chords are "guaranteed to work and sound
good"; anything else is out of key, and out of key means wrong.

**The three positional quality patterns** — memorise once, apply to all 13 keys:
- Major: **I ii iii IV V vi viiº**
- Natural minor: **i iiº III iv v VI VII**
- Harmonic minor: **i iiº III iv V VI VII**

Uppercase = major, lowercase = minor, lowercase + º = diminished. No augmented chords
occur naturally in major or natural minor.

**I – V – I, and the harmonic-minor repair.** Tonic = beginning, Dominant = tension,
Tonic = resolution; listeners are "pre-programmed" for it. Natural minor's i–v–i falls
short because the dominant is minor. The fix is the smallest possible change: **raise the
third of the V chord one half step and change nothing else** — do not propagate the
altered note to other chords. That single alteration is harmonic minor, and it is why
harmonic minor is the most widely used minor scale.

**Write the song without an instrument.** The book's stated goal and its recurring
assignment. Pick a key → harmonise it → choose six or more chords starting on I → end
with a cadence → arrange → assign parts. Justified by Beethoven composing while deaf.

**Arrangement = hold common tones.** Two suggestions do all the work: move to the next
closest letter, and keep the same note when changing chords. Track voices as Bottom /
Middle / Top rows. Holding common tones automatically produces inversions
(C → Em/B → Am/C). All-root-position playing is the amateur tell — "the more your hand
jumps around, the more the listener's ear jumps around in confusion."

**Only the bass determines the inversion.** In a band, the lowest-sounding instrument
alone fixes which inversion is sounding; every other player may use any notes of the
chord in any order. A singer's legal notes are exactly the notes of the current chord —
anything else is "sour."

**Three notations, chosen by context.** Figured bass (`E Major: I vi iii IV V I`) is
compact and transposes for free but is meaningless without the key. Chord abbreviations
(`Bb Gm Dm Eb F Bb`) need no key and are more popular today. Longhand dictation — the
"Mommy, please hold my hand" method — is never the answer at band practice.

**Say letter and quality, always.** "THERE IS NO SUCH THING AS A D CHORD, OR A B CHORD,
OR MINOR CHORD!" Vocabulary is treated throughout as a direct competence signal.

## Scope & Limits

**Edition.** First printing, November 2009, CreateSpace. ISBN-13 978-1-4499-2505-5.
140 pages, 28 chapters plus an answer key. The PDF has a clean text layer; no OCR was
needed. Note the book's own footer misspells the author as "Stenier" throughout, and the
table of contents prints "CHPATER 28."

**Stated audience.** Not for absolute beginners — Steiner assumes you already play
something. "If you are 100% completely new to music... this book might be a little bit
over your head."

**What is covered.** Note spelling; the five scale-building rules; major, natural minor,
harmonic minor and melodic minor; four triad types; six seventh types; three sus types
plus 7sus4; diatonic triads in three modes; Roman numerals; figured bass; chord
abbreviations; inversions; voice-leading arrangement; band part assignment; three
cadences.

**What is not covered.** Rhythm, meter, time signatures, note values and rests. Key
signatures as notation (the book always spells scales out). Bass clef, grand staff,
transposing instruments. Modes beyond major/minor. Modulation, borrowed chords, secondary
dominants, chromaticism. Counterpoint. Jazz harmony — Chapter 17 explicitly defers 9th,
11th and 13th chords to jazz study. Ear training beyond "play it and listen."

**Known errors and simplifications in the source, flagged where they occur:**
- Chapter 9's summary bullet prints the D augmented chord as "D, F# and A" (should be A#);
  Chapter 10's prints the F diminished chord as "D, F#, and A#" (should be F, Ab, Cb).
- Chapter 18's prose recap calls degree 4 the "submediant"; the book's own table correctly
  names it the subdominant.
- Chapters 19 and 21 print the seventh numeral as "VIII" in their summary rows.
- Chapter 21's bullet list of B harmonic minor chords names "C major" and "D major" for VI
  and VII; the table and answer key give G major and A major.
- Chapter 25's abbreviation line prints "G/G" where "G/B" is meant.
- Answer key: the Chapter 18 tables head the third column "ii" instead of "iii"; the Ab
  major table gives ii's fifth as E instead of F; the Chapter 10 G# diminished entry is
  written "G# D B" out of order; the Chapter 11 Db half-diminished and Db diminished
  sevenths print "A" where Abb is required.
- **Harmonic minor is presented as a chord alteration, not a scale.** Steiner shows the B
  natural minor and B harmonic minor *scales* as identical and says the difference "lies
  in the diatonic triads" — only the V chord's third is raised. Standard practice raises
  the seventh degree throughout the scale, which also yields an augmented III and a
  diminished vii. Readers moving to other theory texts should expect that difference.
- The book gives no whole/half-step formula for the harmonic minor scale.
- "Sus6" is the book's term for a 1-5-6 chord; most other texts call a chord containing a
  sixth an *added-sixth* chord and reserve "suspended" for sus2 and sus4.
- Diatonic-triad tables cover 13 keys per mode; the book treats these as exhaustive
  ("every scale you will ever use"), which holds for practical purposes but is a
  simplification of the full enharmonic space.
