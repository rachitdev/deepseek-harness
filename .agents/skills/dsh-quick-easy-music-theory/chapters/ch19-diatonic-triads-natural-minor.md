# Chapter 19 — Diatonic Triads (Natural Minor)

## Core Idea
"Music is comprised of many formulas that never change." The harmonisation procedure
from Chapter 18 transfers unchanged to the natural minor scale — but because the step
pattern differs, the *qualities* land in different places. Minor keys always give
i iiº III iv v VI VII.

## Frameworks Introduced

### The universal natural-minor pattern
```
    i    iiº    III    iv    v    VI    VII
```
- Tonic (1) always minor
- Supertonic (2) always diminished
- Mediant (3) always major
- Subdominant (4) always minor
- Dominant (5) always minor
- Submediant (6) always major
- Subtonic (7) always major

*(The book prints the seventh numeral as "VIII" in its summary rows; it is VII — the
seventh degree.)*

### Same procedure, new scale
Rotate the seven scale notes so each in turn is slot 1, read slots 3 and 5, name the
triad, assign the numeral by case.

## Key Concepts
- "Once again, there are seven notes we can use in this key. Thus, seven chords can be
  built using the seven notes."
- "There are no naturally occurring augmented chords in a natural minor scale."
- "There are only seven chords and seven notes that will work in each key."
- The refrain again: "If the note isn't listed, it doesn't work! If the chord isn't
  listed, it doesn't work!"
- Homework covers 13 natural minor keys: C, C#, D, D#, Eb, E, F, F#, G, G#, A, Bb, B.

## Mental Models
- **Same seven chord *qualities*, rotated.** A major key and its relative natural minor
  contain the same seven triads; the minor key just starts counting from a different
  one. Compare G major (G Am Bm C D Em F#º) with E natural minor (Em F#º G Am Bm C D).
- **The diminished chord moves.** In major it sits on 7; in natural minor on 2. Knowing
  where the diminished chord lives tells you which mode you are in.
- **v is the weak spot.** A *minor* dominant is the defect Chapter 21 is written to fix.

## Anti-patterns
- Carrying the major-key numeral pattern into a minor key.
- Using a major tonic in a natural minor key.
- Expecting an augmented chord to appear. It does not.
- Numbering the last chord VIII. There are seven degrees.

## Worked Example
*Harmonising E natural minor — the chapter's full build.*

E natural minor scale: **E F# G A B C D**

| Root | Rotate | 1-3-5 | Chord | Numeral |
|------|--------|-------|-------|---------|
| E | E(1) F#(2) G(3) A(4) B(5) | E G B | E minor | **i** |
| F# | F#(1) G(2) A(3) B(4) C(5) | F# A C | F# diminished | **iiº** |
| G | G(1) A(2) B(3) C(4) D(5) | G B D | G major | **III** |
| A | A(1) B(2) C(3) D(4) E(5) | A C E | A minor | **iv** |
| B | B(1) C(2) D(3) E(4) F#(5) | B D F# | B minor | **v** |
| C | C(1) D(2) E(3) F#(4) G(5) | C E G | C major | **VI** |
| D | D(1) E(2) F#(3) G(4) A(5) | D F# A | D major | **VII** |

Every one of these seven works in E natural minor; nothing else does.

Homework: harmonise all 13 natural minor keys, then write three instrument-free songs
of at least six chords each in three different minor keys, and play them all.

## Key Takeaways
- Natural minor keys always give i iiº III iv v VI VII.
- The diminished chord sits on degree 2, not degree 7.
- Same seven triads as the relative major, entered at a different point.
- No naturally occurring augmented chords.
- The minor v is the problem Chapter 21 solves.
