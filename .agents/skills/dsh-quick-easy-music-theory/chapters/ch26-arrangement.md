# Chapter 26 — Arrangement

## Core Idea
"You can pick the best sounding chords in the world, but if you don't have a good
arrangement, it's going to sound sub-par." Arrangement is choosing which inversion of
each chord to play so the voices move as little as possible. Two suggestions do all the
work, and the payoff is that your hand — and the listener's ear — stops jumping around.

## Frameworks Introduced

### Tips for making the best arrangement possible
Steiner's exact framing: "They aren't a set of rules, however they are highly
suggested."

1. **When moving from one note to the next, move to the next closest letter.**
2. **If at all possible, keep the same note when changing from one chord to the next.**

### The three-voice worksheet
Lay the progression out as Bottom / Middle / Top rows and track each voice horizontally
across the chord changes. This is the tool the entire chapter runs on.

| | Chord 1 | Chord 2 | Chord 3 |
|--|---------|---------|---------|
| Top Note | | | |
| Middle Note | | | |
| Bottom Note | | | |

### Voicing
**Voicing** — how you choose to invert a chord.

## Key Concepts
- Common tones are the lever: find what two adjacent chords share and hold it.
- Once a note other than the root ends up on the bottom, the chord has become an
  inversion, and the abbreviation gains a slash (Ch 25).
- There are no wrong answers in the homework — "There are many possibilities for
  arrangement in the following pieces."
- Arrangement is a competence signal: "I can immediately tell how much someone knows
  about music just by his or her arrangement... I can judge someone in 3 chords, and so
  can the top people in the music business."

## Mental Models
- **Hand movement = ear movement.** "The more your hand jumps around, the more the
  listener's ear jumps around in confusion."
- **Amateur tell.** All-root-position playing is the diagnostic: "Nothing screams
  'amateur' like hearing someone play a set of chords like this."
- **Slides, not leaps.** The goal state is "a few slides of the fingers" instead of
  "leaps and bounds across your instrument."

## Anti-patterns
- Playing every chord in root position by default.
- Moving all three voices when one would do.
- Letting the hand travel up the keyboard for a chord that could have been inverted
  downward.
- Writing good chords and then not arranging them at all — "The arrangement of your song
  will make it or break it."

## Reference Tables

**Before and after, the chapter's example progression `C → Em → Am`**

*Unarranged (all root position):*

| Voice | C | Em | Am |
|-------|---|----|----|
| Top | G | B | E |
| Middle | E | G | C |
| Bottom | C | E | A |

Every voice moves on every change; the hand travels the length of the keyboard.

*Arranged:*

| Voice | C | Em/B | Am/C |
|-------|---|------|------|
| Top | G | G | A |
| Middle | E | E | E |
| Bottom | C | B | C |

C → Em: only the bottom moves, C down to B. Em → Am: bottom B up to C, top G up to A,
middle E held.

**Common-tone analysis**

| Chord pair | Shared notes | Note that must move |
|------------|--------------|---------------------|
| C (C E G) → Em (E G B) | E, G | C → B |
| Em (E G B) → Am (A C E) | E | B → C, G → A |

**Resulting inversions**

| Chord | Bottom note | Inversion | Abbreviation |
|-------|-------------|-----------|--------------|
| C | C (root) | Root position | C |
| Em | B (fifth) | Second inversion | Em/B |
| Am | C (third) | First inversion | Am/C |

## Worked Example
*The chapter's full arrangement of `C → Em → Am`, reasoned the way Steiner does it.*

**Step 1 — the naive version.** Play C (C E G), then move the hand up to Em (E G B),
then all the way up to Am (A C E). "It's illogical because your hand is jumping around
far too much for no reason."

**Step 2 — fix the first change.** "What is the only difference between a C major chord
and an E minor chord? The B." Both contain E and G, so hold them:

| | C | Em/B |
|--|---|------|
| Top | G | G |
| Middle | E | E |
| Bottom | C | **B** |

One note moves, one half step down. "The Em chord now becomes an Em/B (second
inversion) because the bottom note is now the fifth (5)."

**Step 3 — fix the second change.** Em/B and Am share E. Hold the middle voice:

| | C | Em/B | Am/C |
|--|---|------|------|
| Top | G | G | **A** |
| Middle | E | E | E |
| Bottom | C | **B** | **C** |

"The Am now becomes an Am/C (first inversion) because the bottom note is the third (3)."

**Result:** `C → Em/B → Am/C`, played as C E G → B E G → C E A. Steiner's verdict:
"Congratulations! You now play and think like a true professional!"

## Key Takeaways
- Two suggestions: move to the nearest letter; hold common tones.
- Track voices as Bottom / Middle / Top rows.
- Holding common tones automatically produces inversions.
- All-root-position playing is the amateur tell.
- No single right answer — many valid arrangements exist.
