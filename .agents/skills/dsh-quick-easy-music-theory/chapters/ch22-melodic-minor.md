# Chapter 22 — Melodic Minor

## Core Idea
Melodic minor is two scales in one: it uses one pattern going up and a different pattern
coming down. The descending form is an exact replica of the natural minor scale; only
the ascending form is distinct. Steiner calls this "the least used minor scale in
history" and gives it the shortest chapter in the book.

## Frameworks Introduced

### The two melodic minor formulas
```
    Melodic Minor (Ascending)
     1     2     3     4     5     6     7     8
       (W)   (H)   (W)   (W)   (W)   (W)   (H)

    Melodic Minor (Descending)
     1     2     3     4     5     6     7     8
       (W)   (H)   (W)   (W)   (H)   (W)   (W)
```
The descending pattern is identical to natural minor (`W H W W H W W`). The ascending
pattern raises degrees 6 and 7 — it is a major scale with a flattened third.

### Direction-dependent spelling
The scale you write depends on which way the line is moving. This is the only scale in
the book where that is true.

## Key Concepts
- **Ascending** — playing a series of notes that go up in pitch.
- **Descending** — playing a series of notes that go down in pitch.
- "The melodic minor scale actually consists of two different scales."
- Steiner's own disclosure: "At this point in 2008, I have never once used the melodic
  minor scale in my professional songwriting career" — though he has studied it and
  played pieces written with it, and "plenty of other people have used it and made it
  sound great."
- No diatonic-triad chapter follows for melodic minor, unlike natural (Ch 19) and
  harmonic (Ch 21) minor.

## Mental Models
- **Ascending melodic minor = major scale with a lowered third.** Compare C melodic
  minor ascending (C D **Eb** F G A B C) with C major (C D **E** F G A B C). One note.
- **Descending = natural minor, no new work.** "Since you have already written out the
  natural minor scales, I'm not going to torture you with doing the same thing again for
  homework in this chapter."
- **Coverage proportional to usage.** The book allocates page count by how often
  something is used, not by taxonomic completeness. This chapter is the clearest example.

## Anti-patterns
- Using the ascending form on a descending line.
- Assuming melodic minor gets its own set of diatonic triads in this book. It does not.
- Skipping the scale entirely — Steiner still requires all 13 ascending forms as
  homework.

|-------|---------|

## Worked Example
*Building C melodic minor in both directions.*

**Ascending** — formula `W H W W W W H`, root C:

| Slot | Step from previous | Note |
|------|-------------------|------|
| 1 | root | C |
| 2 | W | D |
| 3 | H | Eb |
| 4 | W | F |
| 5 | W | G |
| 6 | W | A |
| 7 | W | B |
| 8 | H | C |

**C melodic minor ascending = C D Eb F G A B C.** Compare C major (C D E F G A B C) —
only the third differs.

**Descending** — formula `W H W W H W W`, which is C natural minor:

**C D Eb F G Ab Bb C** (read downward: C Bb Ab G F Eb D C).

The two forms differ in degrees 6 and 7: A/B ascending versus Ab/Bb descending.

Homework: write out all 13 ascending melodic minor scales. The descending forms are the
natural minor scales already completed in Chapter 5.

## Key Takeaways
- Two patterns: ascending `W H W W W W H`, descending `W H W W H W W`.
- Descending melodic minor *is* natural minor.
- Ascending melodic minor is a major scale with a flattened third.
- The least used of the three minor scales; no diatonic triads given for it.
