# Chapter 3 — Introduction to Building Scales

## Core Idea
A scale is not a mood; it is a *list of the notes you are allowed to use*. Pick a
tonal-centre note, pick a scale type, and the scale hands you the legal note set for
the whole song. Any note outside it — or any alternative spelling of a note inside
it — is wrong. Five rules govern the spelling, and they are the machinery that answers
Chapter 1's Burning Question.

## Frameworks Introduced

### The Five Rules For Building Scales
Steiner presents them "in no particular order," and they are used unchanged for every
scale type in the book (major, natural minor, harmonic minor, melodic minor):

1. You must use every letter of the musical alphabet.
2. All letters must be in order.
3. You cannot repeat any letters except for your starting note.
4. If there are sharps in the scale, then there cannot be any flats.
5. If there are flats in the scale, then there cannot be any sharps.

### Key selection as a two-part decision
"You need to pick a note that will be the tonal center of your song, and then decide
what kind of mood to wrap around that note." Note + scale type = key. Example given:
"I want to write a song in the key of F major" → tonal centre F, scale type major.

## Key Concepts
- **Scale** — a specific arrangement of notes with a distinct pattern of half steps and
  whole steps.
- **Key** — a group of notes based on a particular note and scale that forms the tonal
  centre of the music. Ex: the Key of G Major, the Key of F# Minor.
- **Enharmonic Spelling** — the same note spelled in different ways. Ex: F# is the same
  note as Gb; Eb is the same note as D#; B# is the same note as C.
- Every song has to be in a certain key.

## Mental Models
- **The scale is a whitelist.** "Basically, a scale becomes a list of notes that you
  are able to use in composing a song." Everything downstream — chords (Ch 18),
  figured bass (Ch 23), arrangement (Ch 26) — is filtering against this whitelist.
- **Rules as a checklist you run after every scale.** Steiner literally tick-marks
  all five rules against the finished C major scale in Chapter 4. The rules are a
  verification pass, not just a construction guide.
- **Rules 4 and 5 are a hunch-detector.** Before building, glance at the starting note:
  a flat root usually predicts a flat scale, so commit to flats early (see the Bb
  natural minor build in Chapter 5).

## Anti-patterns
- Skipping a letter (spelling F# as Gb in D major skips F entirely) — violates rules
  1 and 2.
- Letters out of alphabetical order — violates rule 2.
- Reusing a letter (C and C# in the same scale) — violates rule 3.
- Mixing sharps and flats in one scale — violates rules 4 and 5.
- Thinking a seven-letter scale has six notes — the F major scale in the homework has
  seven distinct notes (eight positions counting the repeated tonic).

**Rule-violation lookup**

| Symptom in your scale | Rule broken |
|-----------------------|-------------|
| A letter of the alphabet never appears | 1 |
| Letters not in alphabetical order | 2 |
| Same letter used twice (other than the start/end note) | 3 |
| A sharp appears in a scale that has flats | 4 |
| A flat appears in a scale that has sharps | 5 |

## Worked Example
*The chapter's own True/False homework, with the book's answer key.*

Question set: "Answer the following statements with True or False…"

| Statement | Book's answer | Why |
|-----------|---------------|-----|
| The F major scale contains the note Ab. | **FALSE** | F major is F G A Bb C D E — the third degree is A natural, and Ab would also break rule 3 (A already used). |
| The F major scale contains the note G. | **TRUE** | G is the second degree. |
| The note A# is part of the F major scale. | **FALSE** | The scale contains Bb; an A# would mix a sharp into a flat scale (rule 4) and duplicate the letter A (rule 3). |
| The F major scale has 6 notes. | **FALSE** | It has 7 distinct notes (8 with the octave repeat). |

Additional homework item 1: *memorise the rules* — "These rules will be extremely
important in the construction of scales."

## Key Takeaways
- Key = tonal-centre note + scale type.
- The scale is the complete list of legal notes for the song.
- Five spelling rules, applied identically to every scale type in the book.
- Enharmonic spellings are the same *sound* but not the same *spelling* — and only one
  spelling survives the rules.
