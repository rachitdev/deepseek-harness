# Chapter 1 — The Basics

## Core Idea
Everything in the book is built on twelve notes named with only seven letters plus
sharps and flats. Before any scale or chord can be spelled, you must know the order of
the twelve notes cold and know *which two letter pairs are only a half step apart*
(E–F and B–C). Steiner's stated prerequisite: this book is not for absolute beginners —
"if you are 100% completely new to music... this book might be a little bit over your
head." Get an instrument (he recommends piano) and a teacher first.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Musical Alphabet** — 7 letters, A B C D E F G, repeating after G.
- **Half step / whole step measurement** — every distance in the book is counted in
  half steps. On guitar or bass, one fret = one half step.
- **The Burning Question** — a recurring device in the book. Whenever an enharmonic
  choice appears (C# or Db?), Steiner poses a "Burning Question," gives a "Burning
  Answer," then a "Burning Reason." In Chapter 1 the answer is deferred: *it does
  matter, and you will find out why when we get to scales and chords.*

## Key Concepts
- **Half Step** — a note directly next to another. Ex: A# to B, or G to Gb.
- **Whole Step** — two notes away from each other. Ex: C to D, or G# to F#.
- **Sharp (#)** — play one note *up* from the given letter. Ex: F to F#, D to D#.
- **Flat (b)** — play one note *down* from the given letter. Ex: E to Eb, B to Bb.
- Most letter pairs are a whole step apart: C–D, D–E, F–G, G–A.
- Two letter pairs are only a half step apart: **E–F** and **B–C**. These are the
  "no black key between them" pairs, and they drive every scale spelling later.
- Every sharp and flat carries a letter prefix. **There is no such thing as "a flat
  note" or "a sharp note" by itself.**
- Black keys have two legal names (C# = Db). Which one you use is *not* free choice
  once you are inside a scale or chord — see Chapters 3, 4, 7.

## Mental Models
- **The piano is the reference instrument.** Steiner's whole method is "look at the
  piano diagram and count keys." Even guitarists should learn the keyboard layout,
  because the spelling logic is visual there.
- **Notes are building blocks, not trivia.** The chapter frames memorisation as
  foundation-laying: "How well you know this information will affect your ability to
  learn more advanced techniques later in this book."

## Anti-patterns
- Treating C#/Db as interchangeable in all contexts. Legal *now*, illegal inside a
  built scale or chord.
- Saying "a flat" or "a sharp" without a letter.
- Assuming every adjacent letter is a whole step — E–F and B–C are not.
- Playing an instrument without knowing what notes you are playing. Steiner: "I can't
  tell you how many musicians I have come across that don't know what notes they are
  playing on their instrument, or how to find them. Don't be one of them!"

## Worked Example
*Naming the black key between C and D.*

The key sits one note above C and one note below D.
- Starting from C and going one note **up** → call it **C#**.
- Starting from D and going one note **down** → call it **Db**.

Both names point at the same physical key. Steiner's Burning Question — "Does it
really matter what you call it?" — gets the answer "Yes," with the reason deferred
to the scale and chord chapters, where rules 1–5 for building scales (Ch 3) force
exactly one of the two spellings.

## Key Takeaways
- 7 letters, 12 notes, 2 half-step letter pairs (E–F, B–C).
- Half step = adjacent note = one fret. Whole step = two half steps.
- Accidentals always attach to a letter.
- Enharmonic freedom ends the moment you build a scale or a chord.
