# Chapter 20 — Tonic, Dominant, Tonic

## Core Idea
A short, deliberate detour: the I – V – I relationship is the most widely used chord
progression in the history of music, and it works because it maps a three-part
narrative — beginning, tension, resolution — onto three chords. Steiner inserts this
chapter specifically to set up the argument for harmonic minor in Chapter 21.

## Frameworks Introduced

### The three-part harmonic arc
| Chord | Function | Narrative role |
|-------|----------|----------------|
| I (Tonic) | Establishes the key | A nice beginning |
| V (Dominant) | Creates instability | Some tension in the middle |
| I (Tonic) | Returns home | A nice resolution |

### Prerequisite: you must have played your own compositions
"Through playing your various compositions thus far (you should have 6 of them: 3 major,
3 natural minor), you will begin to hear the unique sound properties that occur when
changing from chord to chord." The chapter assumes the Chapter 18 and 19 homework is
done — the point is meant to be *heard*, not read.

## Key Concepts
- The Tonic–Dominant relationship is "very important to say the least."
- "There have been countless songs created that consist entirely of Tonic (I) and
  Dominant (V) chords" — Steiner names *Twinkle, Twinkle Little Star* and *Mary Had A
  Little Lamb*.
- **"We are almost pre-programmed to love and desire the I – V – I relationship."**
- It "serves as our foundation for songwriting, and how we perceive the beginning and
  ending of a musical phrase."

## Mental Models
- **Listener expectation as a design constraint.** Steiner's central argument across
  Chapters 20, 21 and 28: our ears were trained from birth, so satisfying the trained
  expectation is not a cliché, it is the mechanism by which music reads as finished.
- **Two chords are enough.** If nursery rhymes work on I and V alone, complexity is
  optional; the arc is what matters.
- **This chapter is a setup.** It exists to make the Chapter 21 argument land: if I–V–I
  is the gold standard, then natural minor's i–v–i is a measurable shortfall.

## Anti-patterns
- Reading this chapter without having played the six compositions from Chapters 18–19 —
  the claim is auditory.
- Dismissing I–V–I as too simple to be worth studying.

## Worked Example
*The chapter's own demonstration, spelled out in D major (from Chapter 18's grid).*

| Step | Numeral | Chord | Notes | What the listener hears |
|------|---------|-------|-------|------------------------|
| 1 | I | D major | D F# A | A nice beginning |
| 2 | V | A major | A C# E | Tension in the middle |
| 3 | I | D major | D F# A | Resolution |

Compare with E natural minor (from Chapter 19's grid), where the same three positions
give:

| Step | Numeral | Chord | Notes |
|------|---------|-------|-------|
| 1 | i | E minor | E G B |
| 2 | v | B minor | B D F# |
| 3 | i | E minor | E G B |

Steiner's Chapter 21 verdict on that second version: "the natural minor scale's i – v –
i pales in comparison to the major scale's I – V – I."

## Key Takeaways
- I – V – I = beginning, tension, resolution.
- Most-used progression in music history; entire songs run on it.
- The preference is trained into listeners from childhood.
- A minor v does not deliver the same tension — the premise of Chapter 21.
