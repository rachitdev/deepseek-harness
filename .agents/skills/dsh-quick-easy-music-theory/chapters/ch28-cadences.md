# Chapter 28 — Cadences

## Core Idea
"Every good song needs a good ending." A cadence is a short chord formula that tells the
ear a phrase is finished. Three of them cover almost everything ever written — and one
of the three is deliberately a *fake* ending. Plus one piece of structural advice: start
on the Tonic.

## Frameworks Introduced

### The three cadences
```
Authentic Cadence     V    ->    I
Plagal Cadence        IV   ->    I
Deceptive Cadence     V    ->    vi
```

- **Authentic (V → I)** — "the most commonly used ending in history. Nothing says
  'ending' like hearing V to I."
- **Plagal (IV → I)** — "sometimes called the 'Amen cadence' because of its widespread
  use in hymns. Still, the IV to I chord progression makes for a highly effective
  ending."
- **Deceptive (V → vi)** — "As its name implies, it's actually not an ending. Rather,
  it's a fake ending. Since our most popular way to end a song is V to I, people almost
  expect to hear a I chord after you play a V. But, when you replace the I with a vi,
  the audience is pleasantly fooled."

### The opening rule
> "Start your beginning compositions on the Tonic (I) before exploring other
> possibilities. The purpose of the Tonic chord is to establish tonality, or simply put:
> Give your listeners a good beginning."

### The journey model
"A song takes a listener on a journey. Every journey has a beginning point and an ending
point. The stuff in the middle will determine what kind of journey the listener has
traveled on."

## Key Concepts
- **Cadence** — a series of chords that imply an ending to the ear.
- Trained expectation again: "Our ears have been trained since birth to recognize what
  the end of a song is supposed to sound like. If we do not hear one of the appropriate
  endings, we question the quality of the music."
- Steiner's own framing of how few there are: "That's it? That's all that has been used
  for countless years? For the most part, yes. It has been tried, tested, and approved
  for your use."
- The failure mode: a song that ends unexpectedly or abruptly, where "the audience pauses
  for a few seconds before realizing that the performance is over."

## Mental Models
- **A cadence is punctuation.** V→I is a full stop; V→vi is an ellipsis.
- **The deceptive cadence only works because the authentic one is expected.** It is
  parasitic on the listener's training — which is why you cannot use it as the default.
- **Endings are conventional, not creative.** Three formulas have sufficed for centuries;
  spend your originality elsewhere in the song.

## Anti-patterns
- Ending on a chord that gives no closure and leaving the audience unsure whether to
  applaud.
- Using a deceptive cadence as the actual final chord of a piece — it is a fake ending
  by definition.
- Starting a beginner composition anywhere other than the Tonic.
- Assuming any chord can precede I. Homework 4 asks the reader to test exactly this and
  judge the results by ear.

## Worked Example
*The chapter's four homework tasks, realised in C major using the Chapter 18 grid
(I = C E G, IV = F A C, V = G B D, vi = A C E).*

**1. A progression ending with an authentic cadence**

| | I | vi | IV | **V** | **I** |
|--|---|----|----|-------|-------|
| Chord | C | Am | F | **G** | **C** |

**2. A progression ending with a plagal cadence**

| | I | V | vi | **IV** | **I** |
|--|---|---|----|--------|-------|
| Chord | C | G | Am | **F** | **C** |

**3. A progression containing a deceptive cadence**

| | I | IV | **V** | **vi** | IV | V | I |
|--|---|----|-------|--------|----|---|---|
| Chord | C | F | **G** | **Am** | F | G | C |

The V→vi in the middle fakes the ending; the real ending is the authentic cadence at
the end. Note both progressions above start on I, as the chapter instructs.

**4.** Play ii→I (Dm→C), iii→I (Em→C), vi→I (Am→C) and viiº→I (Bº→C) and judge each by
ear against V→I and IV→I.

## Key Takeaways
- Three cadences: authentic V→I, plagal IV→I, deceptive V→vi.
- Authentic is the strongest and most common; plagal is the hymn ending; deceptive is a
  fake ending used mid-song.
- Begin compositions on the Tonic to establish tonality.
- Endings are conventional; the middle is where the journey happens.
