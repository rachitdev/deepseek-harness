# Chapter 12 — Introduction to Suspended Chords

## Core Idea
"Sus chords have no third (3)!" That single fact defines the whole family. The third is
replaced — by a second in a sus2, a fourth in a sus4, a sixth in a sus6 — and the fifth
stays. Because the third is what makes a chord major or minor, removing it removes the
happy/sad quality entirely, which is why sus chords sound unresolved and floating.

## Frameworks Introduced

### The three sus formulas
```
              sus4
        1        4        5
           (5)      (2)

              sus2
        1        2        5
           (2)      (5)

              sus6
        1        5        6
           (7)      (2)
```
Note the sus6 is written root → fifth → sixth, so the big 7-half-step jump comes first.

### Substitution rule
| Chord | 3 is replaced by | Fifth present? |
|-------|------------------|----------------|
| sus2 | the second (2) | Yes |
| sus4 | the fourth (4) | Yes |
| sus6 | the sixth (6) | Yes (it is the middle note) |

## Key Concepts
- "Sus" is shorthand for "suspended" — "somebody decided that the word 'suspended' took
  too much effort to say."
- Suspended chords "have the largest variation in the formula so far": the interval
  sizes range from 2 to 7 half steps, versus 3 and 4 for all the triads.
- All three sus types remain three-note chords.
- Three types "that you will see written": sus2, sus4, sus6.

## Mental Models
- **No third = no quality.** You cannot ask whether a sus chord is major or minor. That
  is the point of the sound.
- **sus2 and sus6 are interval mirrors.** sus2 is (2) then (5); sus6 is (7) then (2).
  Chapter 15 makes the near-symmetry explicit: "the only difference is that the
  intervals are reversed."

## Anti-patterns
- Including a third "for fullness" — that stops it being a sus chord.
- Assuming the numbers refer to note count rather than scale degree.
- Forgetting the fifth. All three formulas contain a 5.

## Reference Tables

**Sus chord formulas, side by side**

| Type | Slots | Interval 1 | Interval 2 | Total span (half steps) |
|------|-------|-----------|-----------|------------------------|
| sus4 | 1 – 4 – 5 | 5 | 2 | 7 |
| sus2 | 1 – 2 – 5 | 2 | 5 | 7 |
| sus6 | 1 – 5 – 6 | 7 | 2 | 9 |

**Which triad chapters do NOT apply**

| Property | Triads (Ch 7–10) | Sus chords (Ch 12–15) |
|----------|------------------|----------------------|
| Contains a third | Yes | No |
| Major/minor quality | Defined | Undefined |
| Note count | 3 | 3 |
| Contains a fifth | Yes | Yes |

## Worked Example
*This chapter is the family orientation; the concrete builds are in Chapters 13–15. Its
own worked content is the substitution table applied to a single root. Taking C:*

| Chord | Slots used | Letters (alphabet check from C) | Result |
|-------|-----------|--------------------------------|--------|
| C major triad | 1, 3, 5 | C, E, G | C E G |
| Csus2 | 1, 2, 5 | C, D, G | C D G |
| Csus4 | 1, 4, 5 | C, F, G | C F G |
| Csus6 | 1, 5, 6 | C, G, A | C G A |

Every one of the three sus chords keeps C and G and swaps only the middle note — the
E disappears in all of them. That disappearance is the whole family.

## Key Takeaways
- Sus = no third.
- sus2 = 1-2-5, sus4 = 1-4-5, sus6 = 1-5-6; all keep the fifth.
- Interval sizes: sus4 (5)(2), sus2 (2)(5), sus6 (7)(2).
- The next three chapters build each type; Chapter 16 adds a seventh to sus4.
