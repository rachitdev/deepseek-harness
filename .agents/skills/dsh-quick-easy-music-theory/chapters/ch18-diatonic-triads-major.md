# Chapter 18 — Diatonic Triads (Major)

## Core Idea
This is the chapter where scales and chords fuse. Take the seven notes of a major
scale, use each in turn as a root, and build 1-3-5 *using only notes already in the
scale*. You get seven chords — and those seven are the complete legal chord vocabulary
for that key. No counting of half steps is needed, "because of the predetermined notes
in the scale." This is how you write a song without an instrument.

## Frameworks Introduced

### The assignment
> "Write a song without an instrument. It better sound good when I play it."

Steiner's justification: Beethoven was deaf for the last 20 years of his life and
composed some of his most acclaimed music in that period.

### The harmonisation procedure
1. Pick a key. (He picks D major: "some keys are more difficult to work with than
   others due to variances in the number of sharps and flats they contain.")
2. Write out the seven notes of the scale.
3. Rotate the numbering so each note in turn is slot 1; read off slots 3 and 5.
4. Identify each resulting triad's quality (major / minor / diminished).
5. Label it with a Roman numeral.

### The Roman numeral system
- **UPPERCASE = major chord. lowercase = minor chord. lowercase + º = diminished.**
- Degree names apply regardless of quality.

| Degree | Major form | Minor form | Name |
|--------|-----------|-----------|------|
| 1 | I | i | Tonic |
| 2 | II | ii | Supertonic |
| 3 | III | iii | Mediant |
| 4 | IV | iv | Subdominant |
| 5 | V | v | Dominant |
| 6 | VI | vi | Submediant |
| 7 | VII | vii | Subtonic |

*(The chapter's prose recap mislabels degree 4 as "submediant"; the list above is the
book's own reference table and is correct.)*

### The universal major-key pattern
```
    I    ii    iii    IV    V    vi    viiº
```
- Tonic (1) always major
- Supertonic (2) always minor
- Mediant (3) always minor
- Subdominant (4) always major
- Dominant (5) always major
- Submediant (6) always minor
- Subtonic (7) always diminished

## Key Concepts
- **Diatonic Triads** — chords that naturally occur in a given scale.
- "There are only seven chords and seven notes that will work in each key."
- "There are no naturally occurring augmented chords in a major scale."
- The rule Steiner repeats after every chord: **"If the note isn't listed, it doesn't
  work in the key! If the chord isn't listed, it doesn't work in the key!"**
- In D major, a D major chord is guaranteed to work; D minor, D augmented, D diminished
  and anything containing Db or D# are all wrong.

## Mental Models
- **The key is a closed system.** Once the key is fixed, composition becomes selection
  from seven options rather than search over all possibilities.
- **Quality is positional, not memorised per key.** Learn I ii iii IV V vi viiº once and
  it applies to all 13 major keys.
- **Guaranteed to work.** Steiner's phrasing throughout — diatonic chords are
  "guaranteed to work and sound good." That guarantee is what makes instrument-free
  composition possible.

## Anti-patterns
- Using a chord built on a scale note but with the wrong quality (D minor in D major).
- Borrowing a chord from outside the key because it "sounds cool" — at this stage of the
  book, out of key means wrong.
- Looking for an augmented chord in a major key. There isn't one.
- Counting half steps when harmonising. The scale has already done that work.

## Worked Example
*Harmonising D major, then writing a song with no instrument — the chapter's full
demonstration.*

D major scale: **D E F# G A B C#**

| Root | Rotate the numbering | 1-3-5 | Chord | Numeral |
|------|---------------------|-------|-------|---------|
| D | D(1) E(2) F#(3) G(4) A(5) | D F# A | D major | **I** |
| E | E(1) F#(2) G(3) A(4) B(5) | E G B | E minor | **ii** |
| F# | F#(1) G(2) A(3) B(4) C#(5) | F# A C# | F# minor | **iii** |
| G | G(1) A(2) B(3) C#(4) D(5) | G B D | G major | **IV** |
| A | A(1) B(2) C#(3) D(4) E(5) | A C# E | A major | **V** |
| B | B(1) C#(2) D(3) E(4) F#(5) | B D F# | B minor | **vi** |
| C# | C#(1) D(2) E(3) F#(4) G(5) | C# E G | C# diminished | **viiº** |

**Steiner's song, written without touching an instrument:**

| Numeral | Chord | Notes |
|---------|-------|-------|
| I | D Major | D F# A |
| vi | B Minor | B D F# |
| IV | G Major | G B D |
| ii | E minor | E G B |
| V | A Major | A C# E |
| I | D Major | D F# A |

His verdict on playing it: **"SOUNDS GREAT!"**

Homework: harmonise all 13 major keys, then write three more instrument-free songs of
at least six chords each, in three different keys, and play them all.

## Key Takeaways
- Seven scale notes → seven chords → the entire legal vocabulary of the key.
- Major keys always give I ii iii IV V vi viiº.
- Uppercase = major, lowercase = minor, lowercase + º = diminished.
- Degree names: tonic, supertonic, mediant, subdominant, dominant, submediant, subtonic.
- No augmented chords occur naturally in a major key.
