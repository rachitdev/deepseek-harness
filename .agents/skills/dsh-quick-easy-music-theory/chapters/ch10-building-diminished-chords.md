# Chapter 10 — Building Diminished Chords

## Core Idea
Diminished chords stack two 3-half-step gaps. They are "seldom used due to its harsh
and unsettling sound," but they matter because a diminished triad occurs naturally on
the seventh degree of every major key and the second degree of every minor key. This
is also the chapter where Steiner establishes that a *white* key can legally be called
a flat — Cb and Fb are real spellings.

## Frameworks Introduced

### The Diminished Chord Formula
```
           Diminished
        1        3        5
           (3)      (3)
```

### The "re-spell to satisfy the alphabet" move
When counting lands on a pitch whose obvious name is the wrong letter, you do not
change the chord — you change the *name of the note*. B becomes Cb. E becomes Fb. A
becomes Bbb. The alphabet check is the authority.

## Key Concepts
- Root→third: **3 half steps**. Third→fifth: **3 half steps**.
- Diminished is dissonant with an evil, harsh sound (Ch 6: "think of zombies coming
  after you").
- **Burning Question:** "B isn't a black key! So how can you call that Cb? Can you
  refer to a white key on the piano as a flat or sharp? Is that musically 'legal'?"
  **Burning Answer: YES.** **Burning Reason:** the definition of flat — "to play one
  note down from the given letter" — says nothing about black keys. Also, "Guitars
  don't have 'black keys.'"
- Practicality vs legality: "Is it practical to always refer to the letter E as Fb?
  Absolutely not! But you cannot, under any circumstances, change the spelling of the
  chords."
- **"A chord spelled as F, Ab, and B is no longer an F diminished chord."**

## Mental Models
- **Spelling is orthography, not acoustics.** Steiner's recurring analogy: correct
  chord spelling is like correct English spelling. The sound may be identical; the
  written form is still wrong.
- **Throw yourself into the fire.** He deliberately picks the hardest example (F
  diminished, which needs Cb) so that "you won't have too much trouble spelling most
  of the other diminished chords."

## Anti-patterns
- Writing F diminished as F, Ab, B. Wrong chord — the fifth must be a form of C.
- Refusing Cb, Fb, or double flats (Bbb, Ebb, Abb) on the grounds that they "aren't
  real notes."
- Using the piano's black keys as the definition of what may carry an accidental.

## Worked Example
*Building an F diminished chord — Steiner's "throw you into the fire" build.*

1. **Root (1) = F.**
2. **Third (3):** count up 3 half steps from F → F#/Gb (1), G (2), G#/Ab (3). Black key.
3. **Alphabet check:** F=1, G=2, A=3, B=4, C=5. Slot 3 must be the letter **A**, slot 5
   the letter **C**. So the third is **Ab**, not G# — "because the letter G does not
   exist in this chord."
4. **Fifth (5):** count up 3 half steps from Ab → A (1), A#/Bb (2), B (3). You land on
   **B** — but the alphabet check demands some form of **C**.
5. **Re-spell:** B, one letter-step down from C, is written **Cb**. Rule satisfied, pitch
   unchanged.

**F diminished = F Ab Cb.**

What this means:
- Play F, Ab and Cb at the same time and you sound an "F diminished chord."
- If someone asks for an F diminished chord, you play F, Ab and Cb.

*(The book's first bullet here misprints the notes as "D, F#, and A#" — a carry-over from
Chapter 9. The chord is F Ab Cb.)*

## Key Takeaways
- Diminished = 1 –(3)– 3 –(3)– 5.
- The alphabet check can force Cb, Fb, and double flats. Write them.
- A white key may be spelled as a flat or a sharp; the definition of flat permits it.
- Re-spelling the note is allowed; re-spelling the *chord* is not.
