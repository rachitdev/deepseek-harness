# Chapter 2 — Reading Music

## Core Idea
Sheet music is the written form of the note names from Chapter 1. Steiner keeps this
chapter deliberately "short and sweet" because, in his experience, notation is *not*
the working communication medium between band members — figured bass (Ch 23) and
chord abbreviations (Ch 24) are. He includes it because he believes it is an important
skill, not because the rest of the book depends on it.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Line notes vs space notes** — a note is drawn either directly on one of the five
  staff lines or in the space between two lines.
- **Vertical position = pitch** — lower on the staff means lower pitch, higher on the
  staff means higher pitch; the closer two notes sit vertically, the closer they are
  in pitch.
- **Clef-first reading** — a clef precedes the notes and fixes what the lines mean.

## Key Concepts
- **5 Line Staff** — five lines drawn parallel and equidistant from each other in
  order to write musical notation.
- **Treble Clef / G Clef** — a clef placing G on the second line up in the staff. This
  is the clef used "in this case, and in most cases" throughout the book.
- Notation's cost/benefit: writing every idea out as sheet music "would take a huge
  amount of time" for a band or solo artist. Orchestras and chamber groups are the
  exception — they almost always use sheet music.

## Mental Models
- **Author : book :: musician : sheet music.** Steiner's analogy for why a written
  form exists at all — it is the primary way to communicate an idea to someone not in
  the room.
- **Meat and bones over completeness.** The chapter states the book's editorial
  filter explicitly: teach "what you really need to know to write better songs in the
  shortest amount of time." Notation is included but not leaned on.

## Anti-patterns
- Waiting to learn notation before learning scales and chords — the book's sequence
  goes straight from Chapter 1 to scale building.
- Assuming sheet music is how bands actually trade ideas. It usually is not.

## Reference Tables

**What the staff encodes**

| Element | Meaning |
|---------|---------|
| 5 line staff | The grid notes are written on |
| Line note | Note drawn directly on a line |
| Space note | Note drawn between two lines |
| Treble (G) clef | Places G on the second line up |
| Higher on staff | Higher pitch, higher on the instrument |
| Lower on staff | Lower pitch, lower on the instrument |
| Small vertical gap | Small difference in pitch |

## Worked Example
*Steiner's own teaching drill, given as the chapter's homework.*

1. Memorise every note location on the G Clef staff.
2. Method: flashcards. "I used to make flashcards for my piano students in order for
   them to learn all of the notes within a week."

Target: one week to fluent recognition of all staff positions in treble clef. The
chapter offers no other exercise — this is the entire practical content.

## Key Takeaways
- Five lines; notes sit on lines or in spaces; the clef defines which.
- Treble/G clef puts G on the second line up.
- Vertical distance on the page maps directly to pitch distance.
- Notation is worth knowing but is not this book's communication method — Chapters 23
  and 24 are.
