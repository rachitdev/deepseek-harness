# Chapter 27 — Composition

## Core Idea
Everything converges here: pick a key, harmonise it, write a progression, arrange it,
then hand out parts to actual players. The one hard constraint is that **only the
lowest-sounding instrument determines the inversion** — everyone else may play any notes
of the chord in any order. "No matter what instrument you are using to create the sound,
the process of creating the sound never changes."

## Frameworks Introduced

### The composition pipeline
1. Choose a key.
2. Look up its diatonic triads (Ch 18/19/21).
3. Write a progression in figured bass and/or chord abbreviations (Ch 23/24).
4. Arrange it for smooth voice movement (Ch 26).
5. Assign parts, bass first.

### The inversion authority rule
> "Changing which note gets played on the bottom is the only thing that makes a chord
> inversion, a chord inversion. Remember that! The notes the bassist plays will
> determine the inversion, with complete disregard to anything else anybody is playing.
> The bass is the lowest sounding instrument in the band; therefore the bass (and only
> the bass) will determine the inversion of the chord."

### Part-assignment analysis
- **Bass** — "MUST follow the inversion pattern... straightforward and non-negotiable.
  If no inversion is given, he plays the root note. If the inversion note is given, he
  must play that note."
- **Guitar** — Steiner chooses to follow the written inversions exactly. "Remember, the
  guitar does not have to do this, I simply wanted it to do so."
- **Keys** — he deliberately does *not* follow the inversions; he writes a new part.
- **Singer / second guitar** — may sing or play any note contained in the current chord.

### The out-of-tune definition
> "What does it mean to sing out of tune? Simple: The singer is not singing a note that
> is within the chord being played by the rest of the band, or singing a note that is not
> even in the scale."

Concretely: on a D major chord the singer has exactly three legal options — **D, F#, or
A. "All other notes are sour."**

## Key Concepts
- Orchestral instruments and rock instruments are interchangeable for this purpose —
  violins/violas/cellos/trumpets/saxes/clarinets or guitars/bass/keys, same process.
- "We can easily add as many musicians as necessary and create millions of different
  variations of the same chord progression."
- Other instruments "may play as few notes, or as many notes as they desire."

## Mental Models
- **One bass line, unlimited upper parts.** The harmonic identity is pinned by one
  player; everyone above is free within the chord.
- **Notes read bottom-up.** Steiner's part charts stack each player's notes lowest-first.
- **Listening as study.** Homework 1 is simply: listen to classical music — J.S. Bach,
  Beethoven, Mozart, Handel, Vivaldi, Haydn — "in order to hear the best possible
  demonstration of this knowledge."

## Anti-patterns
- Letting a guitarist or keyboardist "set" the inversion. They cannot; only the bass can.
- Giving a singer a note that is not in the current chord.
- Forcing every instrument to copy the same voicing. Allowed, but it wastes the freedom.
- Skipping the arrangement step and handing out root-position parts.

## Reference Tables

**The progression, in three notations**

| Figured bass (unarranged) | I | V | vi | I | V | viiº |
|---------------------------|---|---|----|---|---|------|
| Abbreviations | D | A | Bm | D | A | C#º |
| Figured bass (arranged) | I | V6 | vi | I64 | V | viiº64 |
| Abbreviations (arranged) | D | A/C# | Bm | D/A | A | C#º/G |

**The full band score (read each column bottom-up as one chord)**

| Part | I (D) | V6 (A/C#) | vi (Bm) | I64 (D/A) | V (A) | viiº64 (C#º/G) |
|------|-------|-----------|---------|-----------|-------|----------------|
| Singer | F# | E | F# | F# | E | C# |
| Guitar 2 | A | C# | B | A | C# | E |
| Guitar (top) | A | A | F# | F# | E | E |
| Guitar (mid) | F# | E | D | D | C# | C# |
| Guitar (low) | D | C# | B | A | A | G |
| Keys (top) | F# | E | D | D | C# | C# |
| Keys (mid) | D | C# | B | A | A | G |
| Keys (low) | A | A | F# | F# | E | E |
| **Bass** | **D** | **C#** | **B** | **A** | **A** | **G** |

The bass line D – C# – B – A – A – G is what makes those inversions real.

## Worked Example
*Composing the chapter's six-chord song for guitar, bass and keys.*

**Step 1 — key and progression.** D major:
```
D major:  I    V    vi    I    V    viiº
          D    A    Bm    D    A    C#º
```

**Step 2 — arrange it.** Applying Chapter 26's two suggestions gives:
```
          I    V6     vi    I64    V    viiº64
          D    A/C#   Bm    D/A    A    C#º/G
```
The bass now walks stepwise downward — D, C#, B, A, A, G — instead of leaping D, A, B,
D, A, C#.

**Step 3 — assign the bass.** Non-negotiable: play the slash note where one is given,
the root where none is. Result: **D C# B A A G.**

**Step 4 — assign the guitar.** Steiner chooses to mirror the inversions exactly, so the
guitar's lowest voice duplicates the bass line and the chord tones stack above it.

**Step 5 — assign the keys.** He deliberately writes a different voicing (the same three
notes rotated) to show that upper parts are free.

**Step 6 — add a singer and a second guitarist.** Both get notes drawn only from the
current chord. On chord 1 (D major = D, F#, A) the singer gets F#; giving the singer an
E would be out of tune "because there is no 'E' in a D major chord."

**Homework:**
1. Listen to Bach, Beethoven, Mozart, Handel, Vivaldi, Haydn.
2. Compose a 6-chord song, assign everyone parts, play it.
3. Re-write it with a different arrangement.
4. Re-write it again with another arrangement.

## Key Takeaways
- Key → diatonic triads → progression → arrangement → parts.
- Only the lowest instrument determines the inversion.
- Upper parts may use any chord tones, in any order, in any quantity.
- Out of tune = singing a note not in the current chord (or not in the key).
- The same progression supports millions of variations.
