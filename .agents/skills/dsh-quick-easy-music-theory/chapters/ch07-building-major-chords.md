# Chapter 7 — Building Major Chords

## Core Idea
A major chord is root, plus 4 half steps to the third, plus 3 more half steps to the
fifth. The counting gives you the *pitch*; the alphabet check gives you the *spelling*.
And a chord is never named by letter alone — the letter plus the sound quality together
are the chord's name.

## Frameworks Introduced

### The Major Chord Formula
```
        1        3        5
           (4)      (3)
```
Numbers in parentheses = number of half steps from one note to the next.

### The Alphabet Check
Before or during a build, lay out the letters starting from the root and number them
1–5. Whatever letters land on 1, 3 and 5 are the letters the chord *must* use. Example
for D:

| 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| D | E | F | G | A |

So a D-rooted triad is some form of D, some form of F, some form of A. When counting
lands on the black key F#/Gb, F# is forced — "If we called it Gb, it would then become
a fourth instead of a third."

### Chord naming = letter + quality
"Chords are always referred to by a combination of the root note and sound quality it
produces."

## Key Concepts
- Distance root→third in any major chord: **4 half steps**. Third→fifth: **3 half
  steps**.
- **It's the distance between the letters that defines the interval.** You may add a
  sharp or flat to make the half-step count work, but the letter itself is fixed.
- Playing the three notes simultaneously *is* the chord; being asked for the chord
  means playing those three notes.

## Anti-patterns
Steiner's banned phrases, verbatim:
- "Play a D chord"
- "Play a B chord"
- "I played a minor chord"
- "I like Eb chords"

**"THERE IS NO SUCH THING AS A D CHORD, OR A B CHORD, OR MINOR CHORD!"** There are C
major chords, B minor chords, G augmented chords, E diminished chords. Two parts to
every chord description — the letter and the sound quality. Don't leave out either.

Other anti-patterns:
- Choosing the enharmonic spelling by convenience rather than by the alphabet check.
- Spelling the third of D major as Gb — that makes it a fourth, not a third.

## Worked Example
*Building a D major chord — the chapter's second build, and the one that forces a
spelling decision.*

1. **Root (1) = D.**
2. **Third (3):** count up 4 half steps from D → D#/Eb (1), E (2), F (3), F#/Gb (4).
   Lands on a black key.
3. **Alphabet check:** with D as 1, the letters are D E F G A, so slot 3 is the letter
   **F** and slot 5 is the letter **A**. Therefore the black key must be spelled **F#**,
   not Gb — G belongs to slot 4.
4. **Fifth (5):** count up 3 half steps from F# → G (1), G# (2), A (3). The fifth is
   **A**.

```
        D        F#        A
           (4)       (3)
```

What this means, in Steiner's words:
- Play D, F# and A at the same time and you sound a "D major chord."
- If someone asks for a D major chord, you play D, F# and A at the same time.

The chapter's first build, C major, needs no accidental: C → (4 half steps: C#, D, D#,
E) → **E** → (3 half steps: F, F#, G) → **G**. **C E G.**

## Key Takeaways
- Major = 1 –(4)– 3 –(3)– 5.
- Alphabet check first; it decides sharp vs flat every time.
- The letter is fixed by position; the accidental is fixed by the half-step count.
- Always say letter *and* quality. "D chord" is not a thing.
