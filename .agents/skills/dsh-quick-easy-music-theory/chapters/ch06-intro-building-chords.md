# Chapter 6 — Introduction to Building Chords

## Core Idea
Harmony is a solved science, not a matter of taste. The masters "developed a science"
that answers three questions: which notes sound good together, how to build chords
without touching an instrument, and which chords work together in a progression. Every
chord in the next several chapters is a root, a third and a fifth — only the half-step
distances between them change.

## Frameworks Introduced

### The Three Questions harmony answers
1. How do you know which notes sound good when played together?
2. How can you build chords without an instrument?
3. How do you know which chords will sound good when played in a progression?

Questions 1 and 2 are answered by Chapters 7–17; question 3 by Chapters 18–21.

### The Four Primary Types of Chords
**Major, Minor, Augmented, and Diminished.** Each is described by consonance and by a
one-image mood cue:

- **Major** — consonant; happy, bright. *Think of a sunny day.*
- **Minor** — consonant; sad, solemn. *Think of a rainy day.*
- **Augmented** — dissonant; unsettling, mysterious. *Think of a haunted house.*
- **Diminished** — dissonant; evil, harsh. *Think of zombies coming after you.*

### What all four share
1. All chords will have a root (1), a third (3) and a fifth (5).
2. All of them have 3 notes.

### Prerequisites to build any of them
1. The musical alphabet (A B C D E F G)
2. How to count up to 5
3. The piano, or a piano diagram

## Key Concepts
- **Chord** — any combination of 3 or more different notes played at the same time.
- **Harmony** — a combination of notes that when played together produces a pleasing
  effect.
- **Chord Progression** — a series of chords, played one right after the other.
- **Consonant** — pleasing to the ear.
- **Dissonant** — not pleasing to the ear.
- **Interval** — the distance between two notes.
- Melody-only writing is a career risk: "If your songs consist of one note at a time,
  chances are your career will be short lived."

## Mental Models
- **1-3-5 is the skeleton; half steps are the flesh.** Every triad chapter that follows
  reuses the same `1 (x) 3 (y) 5` template and only swaps x and y.
- **Mood is a lookup, not a feeling.** Steiner assigns each chord type a fixed
  emotional label so you can choose harmony from intent before you hear it.
- **You do not need an instrument to compose** — the arithmetic is sufficient. This
  claim is cashed out in Chapter 18 ("Write a song without an instrument") and
  Chapter 27.

## Anti-patterns
- Writing single-note lines only, with no harmony.
- Trying to find chords by strumming until something sounds good, instead of computing
  them.
- Believing you need more than counting to five and a piano diagram to build a triad.

## Worked Example
*The chapter is an orientation and contains no build of its own — its concrete content
is the classification above. The pattern it sets up is filled in immediately:*

| Chord type | Root→3rd | 3rd→5th | Chapter |
|-----------|----------|---------|---------|
| Major | 4 half steps | 3 half steps | [7](ch07-building-major-chords.md) |
| Minor | 3 | 4 | [8](ch08-building-minor-chords.md) |
| Augmented | 4 | 4 | [9](ch09-building-augmented-chords.md) |
| Diminished | 3 | 3 | [10](ch10-building-diminished-chords.md) |

Read down the two numeric columns: 4/3, 3/4, 4/4, 3/3 — the four permutations of three
and four half steps. That is the entire triad system.

## Key Takeaways
- Chord = 3+ different notes sounded together; the four primary types are all 3 notes.
- Every triad is root–third–fifth.
- The four types are the four combinations of 3 and 4 half steps.
- Tools required: the alphabet, counting to five, a piano diagram.
