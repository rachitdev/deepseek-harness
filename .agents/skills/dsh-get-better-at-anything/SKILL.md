## Core Frameworks & Mental Models

**The book's master claim.** Skill is not a general faculty that gets stronger with exercise. It is a large, specific stock of stored patterns. Therefore improvement comes from three levers, and *only* three: **See** (getting other people's knowledge into your head), **Do** (practice that makes it retrievable at the right moment), and **Feed Back** (information that corrects it). Every maxim is an elaboration of one of the three, and most learning failures are a missing ingredient, not a missing talent.

**1. Problem solving is search, so the fix is knowledge, not effort.** A problem space (start, goal, legal moves) is nearly always too large to search. *Weak methods* — generate-and-test, means-ends analysis, planning, hill-climbing — are universal, untrainable, and fail routinely. Improvement means acquiring *strong methods*: domain-specific heuristics that prune the search. **Decision rule:** before grinding on a hard problem, find out how a competent person represents it.

**2. The bottleneck governs instruction.** Working memory holds ~4 items (Miller said 7±2). Effortful search consumes the very capacity needed to learn the pattern being used, so you can solve a problem without learning how. **Decision rule for novices:** worked examples and goal-free problems beat problem solving. **Decision rule as skill grows (expertise-reversal effect):** flip it — fade examples, raise complexity, shift to self-assessment.

**3. Success motivates; failure demotivates.** Bandura's ranking of self-efficacy sources — personal mastery > vicarious experience > verbal persuasion > physiological state — means "believe in yourself" is a weak lever and engineered early wins are a strong one. **Decision rule:** when someone stalls, look for a *missing fluent prerequisite* before concluding they lack aptitude.

**4. Experts can't tell you what they know.** Skill becomes recognition and recedes from awareness. Three barriers: tacit knowledge, the curse of knowledge, lore. **Decision rule:** don't ask experts for advice; ask for specific incidents, watch them work while thinking aloud, and map who *they* consult.

**5. Difficulty must be tuned, not maximized.** Some difficulties are desirable (retrieval, spacing, interleaving), some are pure waste (excess cognitive load, out-of-reach problems). The instrument is the **practice loop**: see an example → attempt a problem → get feedback, cycled with rising difficulty. Experts stay in the loop via **progressive problem solving** — they keep raising their own problem difficulty, which is why mastery never feels easy.

**6. The mind is not a muscle.** Transfer scales with **shared production rules** (IF–THEN units), not with generic mental exercise. Skills are also **asymmetric** — practising comprehension does not build production. **Decision rule:** decompose a broad ambition ("be more creative", "think strategically") into named, practisable component skills, or you will train nothing.

**7. Variability builds control, repetition builds execution.** Contextual interference: random/varied practice performs worse *during* practice and transfers better *afterward*. **Decision rule:** block first for basic correctness, then interleave; match your practice variability to the variability the real task demands.

**8. Quality is a function of quantity.** Simonton's equal-odds baseline: the hit-to-attempt ratio is roughly flat across a career, so total output determines total masterworks. Nobody — creator, expert, or investor — can pick the winners in advance. **Decision rule:** optimize for shots taken, not for the perfection of any one shot.

**9. Diagnose the learning environment before trusting experience.** Klein–Kahneman conditions: intuition is genuine skill only where the environment offers **valid cues** *and* an **opportunity to learn them**. Where prediction requires summing many weak cues, a crude formula beats an expert (Meehl, 100+ studies). **Decision rule:** poker-like environment → trust trained intuition, overruled by math; pundit-like environment → use a model, log decisions, score calibration, argue in a group.

**10. Practice must contact reality.** Skills are dialogues with a physical *and* social environment. Simulators beat the real thing early and decelerate to zero benefit; optimize for **functional**, not visual, fidelity. Access is part of the problem: study the **legitimate path** into a field and how **transparent** real practice is, and treat skill and signal as separate accounts.

**11. Improvement dips before it rises.** Any real technique change regresses you to Fitts & Posner's cognitive phase, and the new method is worse than the old until it becomes fluent. Old patterns also block new ones (Einstellung, functional fixedness, folk physics). **Decision rule:** prefer renovation to rebuilding — seek the ridge between peaks; use constraints rather than self-monitoring to break habits; hire eyes (a coach need not be better than you).

**12. Fear is a feedback problem.** Avoidance sustains anxiety twice: it blocks disconfirming evidence and pays you in relief (negative reinforcement). Exposure is the active ingredient; relaxation, gradualness, and belief-talk are optional. Learned safety is a second, weaker, context-bound memory, so vary contexts and refresh. **Courage is action in spite of fear, not the absence of it.**

**The Conclusion's three questions for any pursuit:** How can I improve the way I learn from others? How can I make my practice more effective? How can I enhance the quality of my feedback? (Tyler Cowen's test: *"What is it you do to train that is comparable to a pianist practicing scales?"*)

## Scope & Limits

- **Edition:** first edition, Hachette Go, 2024. 295 pages including endnotes and index. Chapter and page mapping follows the book's own table of contents.
- **What is covered:** all 12 maxims across the three parts. The Introduction ("How Learning Works") and Conclusion ("Practice Made Perfect") are not separate chapter files; their synthesis is folded into this file's Core Frameworks section, into `patterns.md`, and into `cheatsheet.md`.
- **What is not covered:** the endnotes and bibliography are not reproduced. Readers wanting primary sources should consult the book's notes, which Young explicitly offers as a jumping-off point.
- **Genre caveat:** this is a popular research synthesis, not a textbook or meta-analysis. Young himself notes the science of learning is "diverse and often contentious," and that not everyone will agree with his conclusions. Effect sizes and study details are reported as the book reports them; where the book gives a range or a qualification, this skill preserves it.
- **Material that dates:** the poker material (chapter 9) is written against a pre-solver and early-deep-learning landscape and notes that engines have since overtaken humans. The critique of learning styles, brain training, and minimally guided discovery reflects the evidence base as of publication. Figures such as the US professional-licensing cost estimate ($183–197 billion/year) and Tiger Woods's major count (15 vs. Nicklaus's 18) are as of the 2024 text.
- **Extraction quality:** the source text was extracted cleanly with a full text layer. A small number of OCR-style character substitutions appear in the original extraction (e.g. "S3.9 million" for "$3.9 million", "aUow" for "allow"); these have been silently corrected to the evident intended reading in the chapter files, and no figure has been invented where the extraction was ambiguous.
