## 2. Master decision table — what to change when learning stalls

| Symptom | Diagnosis | Fix | Ch |
|---|---|---|---|
| Solving problems but not improving | Means-ends analysis eating working memory | Worked examples; goal-free problems | 2 |
| Understand the example, can't do it alone | No retrieval practice | Add the "do" leg of the loop | 5 |
| Performs in practice, fails in the real task | Blocked practice; missing contextual interference | Interleave; vary conditions | 7 |
| Performs the drill, fails the whole task | Part-task practice never integrated | Loop drills with whole-task practice | Concl. |
| Slow, frustrated, "not a math/language person" | Missing fluent prerequisite | Diagnose and overlearn the building block | 3 |
| Confident but inaccurate | Wicked learning environment | Model + logged decisions + calibration scoring | 9 |
| Plateau at competent | Stopped raising problem difficulty | Progressive problem solving | 5, 8 |
| Certified but can't perform | Practice never met reality | Put learner in the seat with a safety net | 10 |
| Technically excellent, still fails | Untrained social half of the skill | Standardized protocols + assertiveness training | 10 |
| Same error every time | Buggy algorithm | Stop, diagnose the rule, restart correctly | 11 |
| Right in class, wrong in life | Misconception running in parallel | Confront with simulation/prediction; practise to fluency | 11 |
| Knows the fix, reverts under pressure | Replacement not yet more fluent than incumbent | More practice on the new method before performing | 11 |
| Avoids practising at all | Fear, not ability, is binding | Fear hierarchy + exposure + group | 12 |
| Lots of good work, no recognition | Below the frontier, or a signalling gap | Get to the frontier; make success visible | 8, 10 |

## 3. Novice vs. expert — flip every setting

| Setting | Novice | Expert |
|---|---|---|
| Examples vs. problems | Study examples (worked-example effect) | Solve problems (expertise reversal) |
| Guidance | High, explicit, scripted | Low, unstructured |
| Practice schedule | Blocked | Random / interleaved |
| Text organization | Well-organized (Kintsch) | Poorly organized helps more |
| Problem difficulty | Near-certain success | Deliberately at the edge |
| Feedback source | External corrective | Increasing self-assessment |
| Simulation | Highest value | Diminishing, then negative |
| Realism | Simplify (drop the crosswinds) | Full real-world complexity |
| The core challenge | Getting knowledge **into** your head | Getting it **out** at the right time |

## 5. Selection matrix — which practice format?

| Goal | Format | Because |
|---|---|---|
| Learn a new pattern | Worked example + matched problem | Load is the binding constraint (2) |
| Retain a fact set | Spaced retrieval | Storage is skipped when re-access is cheap (5) |
| Choose correctly among known actions | Interleaved / random practice | Trains the control process (7) |
| Define a concept against neighbours | Juxtaposition: max-different same-concept vs. min-different different-concept | Boundaries are learned from contrast (7) |
| Build flexible adaptation | Variable practice matched to real-task variability | Contextual interference (7) |
| Iron out a tricky component | Part-task drill | Compresses volume, reduces load (Concl.) |
| Integrate and keep meaning | Whole-task practice | Drill automaticity ≠ task fluency (Concl.) |
| Break an entrenched habit | Task constraints | Avoids the yips (11) |
| Calibrate judgment | Logged decisions + probability scoring + after-action report | Outcome feedback alone is insufficient (9) |
| Reduce fear | Graded, precisely matched exposure, in a group | Extinction + habituation, socially supported (12) |

## 6. Trust-your-gut decision rule (Ch 9)

```
Are the environment's cues adequately valid?
  NO  -> Use a model. Log decisions. Score calibration. Argue in a group.
  YES -> Has the person had sufficient experience with rapid feedback?
           NO  -> Treat confidence as noise. Build the feedback loop first.
           YES -> Does the prediction rest on a few strong cues,
                  or on summing many weak ones?
                    Few strong  -> Trust the intuition; let math overrule it.
                    Many weak   -> Formula wins. Adjust its output only for
                                   a genuine "broken leg" cue.
```

## 7. Weak methods and when they fail (Ch 1)

| Weak method | Fails when |
|---|---|
| Generate-and-test | The problem space is large |
| Means-ends analysis | Subgoals multiply; also consumes the capacity needed to *learn* |
| Planning | The simplified space omits what actually matters |
| Hill-climbing | Things must get worse before they get better |

Antidote in every case: a **strong method** borrowed from someone who has one.

## 8. Cognitive load effects — quick reference (Ch 2)

| Effect | Prescription |
|---|---|
| Worked-example | Novices study solved problems |
| Goal-free | Remove the specific goal to suppress means-ends analysis |
| Split-attention | Integrate labels with referents; don't force shuttling |
| Redundancy | Delete duplicated content across channels |
| Expertise-reversal | Reverse all of the above as skill grows |

## 9. Tells and smells

| Tell | Diagnosis | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| "I'm just not a ___ person" | Missing prerequisite mistaken for talent | Find and overlearn the building block (3) |
| Expert says "you just get a feel for it" | Tacit knowledge | Ask for specific incidents; observe think-aloud (4) |
| Expert explanation skips steps | Curse of knowledge | Ask what a beginner wouldn't know; demand every intervening step (4) |
| Textbook contradicts practitioners | Lore at the frontier | Go to the community, not the literature (4) |
| Practice feels smooth and satisfying | Probably blocked, probably not transferring | Interleave (7) |
| Practice feels awkward and error-prone | Possibly a desirable difficulty | Check retention later, not performance now (5, 7) |
| Confidence rising faster than accuracy | Wicked environment | Keep score (9) |
| Only your own outcomes are visible | Asymmetric sampling (the rejected candidates) | Log counterfactuals; find a reference class (9) |
| Rich narrative increases your certainty | Story over aggregation | Return to base rates (9) |
| "It works fine in the simulator" | Fidelity mismatch or past the knee | Check functional correspondence; graduate out (10) |
| Newcomers stationed away from real work | Transparency failure | Get a window onto actual practice (10) |
| Effort rewarded but invisible | Signalling gap | Make the result legible (10) |
| More practice makes the error worse | Buggy algorithm | Stop and diagnose the procedure (11) |
| Correct in the exam, Aristotelian in life | Parallel misconception | Simulate + predict + practise to fluency (11) |
| Reverts to the old method under pressure | New method not yet fluent | More practice before performing (11) |
| Feels a full rotation, delivers a half | "Feel is not real" | Hire eyes (11) |
| Relief on cancelling the commitment | Negative reinforcement of avoidance | Exposure, without the escape (12) |
| Needs a companion / crutch to cope | Safety behaviour | Remove it, or the exposure proves the wrong thing (12) |
| Predicted terror far exceeds felt terror | Overprediction of fear | Log the prediction, then the reality (12) |

## 10. Process templates

### The practice loop (Ch 5)
```
1. SEE      study a worked example, all steps visible
2. DO       attempt a matched problem unaided
3. FEEDBACK compare, correct, note the discrepancy
4. TUNE     raise difficulty / fade the example / interleave
   -> repeat
```
Diagnose a stalled learner by asking which leg is missing. Sequencing within the loop matters far less than including all three.

### The Renaissance / jazz imitation ladder (Ch 2, Ch 7)
```
copy the masterwork -> copy from casts (simplified) -> work from life
charcoal -> grisaille -> full colour
play along with recordings -> transcribe solos -> improvise
```

### Mastery learning cycle (Ch 3)
```
teach -> practise -> DIAGNOSTIC test (never graded)
      -> pass?  yes -> enrichment activity
                no  -> reteach a DIFFERENT way -> practise -> retest
```

### Cognitive task analysis, lightweight (Ch 4)
```
1. Ask for specific incidents, not advice
2. Timeline the incident decision by decision
3. Watch them solve a real problem, thinking aloud (PARI)
4. Ask what a novice would get wrong here, and why
5. Map who they call when stuck (sociogrammetry; usually 3-4 calls to the answer)
```

### Superforecasting protocol (Ch 9)
```
1. Decompose the question into sub-questions
2. Establish the base rate for the reference class
3. Adjust for case-specific evidence
4. State a probability, not a direction
5. Debate it in a group
6. Score it later; check calibration, not just accuracy
```

### Gosport training design (Ch 10)
```
- Learner holds the controls from day one
- Instructor keeps a backup set (safety net)
- Deliberately induce the failure states and require recovery
- Communicate DURING performance, not only after
- Cut theory in favour of doing
```

### Exposure protocol (Ch 12)
```
1. List the fears; list the avoidance behaviours and safety crutches
2. Build a hierarchy, mild -> extreme, matched precisely to the real fear
   (situational + cognitive + physiological)
3. Enter; do not escape; do not seek reassurance; drop the crutches
4. Stay until the fear falls
5. Repeat across VARIED contexts; refresh periodically
6. Do it with other people
```

### The three-question audit (Conclusion)
```
Q1  How can I improve the way I learn from others?
      - examples with every step
      - enough background for the examples to make sense
      - varied examples to generalize the pattern
Q2  How can I make my practice more effective?
      - tune complexity to current expertise
      - build the see/do/feedback loop
      - loop between drills and whole-task practice
      - name the specific skills; the mind is not a muscle
Q3  How can I enhance the quality of my feedback?
      - keep score and track performance
      - conduct after-action reports
      - form a brain trust
```
Cowen's test: *"What is it you do to train that is comparable to a pianist practicing scales?"*

## 11. Feedback environment checklist (Conclusion)

Good feedback is:
- used to **assist learning**, not to reward or punish performance
- focused on **corrections to the task**, not judgments of the individual
- grounded in a relationship of **mutual trust and respect**

Watch for: corrective feedback blended with evaluative stakes (the grade that decides whether you graduate); harsh feedback that demotivates; and **bulk praise** ("you're so smart!"), which also diminishes effort by suggesting no improvement is needed.
