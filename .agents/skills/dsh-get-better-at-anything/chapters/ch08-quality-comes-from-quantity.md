# Chapter 8 — Quality Comes from Quantity

*Part II — Do: Learning from Practice. Maxim 8 of 12.*

> "Well, you just have lots of ideas and throw away the bad ones!" — Linus Pauling, on the secret to his scientific insights

Chapter's opening questions: Are geniuses typically prolific? How large a role does chance play in invention? How can you improve your creative output without sacrificing quality?

## Core Idea

Across science, art and invention, **the most acclaimed creators are the most prolific**. Quality is largely a function of quantity because, beyond the frontier of existing knowledge, which particular attempt succeeds is substantially random. Creative achievement therefore requires (1) enough expertise to reach the frontier, (2) an environment receptive to the idea, and (3) enough shots taken that chance can operate.

## Frameworks Introduced

### The equal-odds baseline (Dean Simonton)
Divide a creator's highly acclaimed works by their total output to get a **quality ratio**. Simonton: "This ratio of hits to total shots does not change in a regular pattern with age. The ratio neither increases nor decreases, nor exhibits any other form. This remarkable result suggests that quality is a function of quantity." Once someone begins contributing original work, **every attempt has roughly equal potential for world-changing impact**. Within a career, the periods of best work are also the periods of most work.

### Price's law (Derek John de Solla Price)
Half of a field's scholarly output comes from approximately **the square root of the number of contributors**. A subdiscipline with 100 publishing authors gets about half its papers from ~10 researchers.

### The three explanations of creative accomplishment
| # | Explanation | Core claim | Key evidence | What it fails to explain |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Expertise** | Herbert Simon: "Creativity is 'thinking writ large.'" Same problem-space search as ordinary problem solving, differing in difficulty and social significance. Einstein: "science was nothing more than the refinement of everyday thinking" | Success concentrates in one field; long training periods required (Hayes's data below) | The flat equal-odds ratio (rising expertise should raise quality over a career), and why some experts are routine and others inventive |
| 2 | **Environment / Zeitgeist** | Ideas are a product of their cultural moment. Francis Bacon on his own contribution: "a birth of time rather than of wit." The field of experts decides what counts as creative | Multiple discoveries; Csikszentmihalyi's field-of-experts argument; social gatekeeping in peer review; commercial reception | Why particular individuals are far more inventive than peers in the same milieu |
| 3 | **Randomness** | Donald Campbell (1960): creativity as **blind variation with selective retention**, paralleling natural selection — chance variation plus heritable accumulation of what works | Accidental inventions; Edison's cut-and-try method; drug discovery today | Why expertise and environment matter at all |

**The combined model.** The existing stock of knowledge defines what innovations are possible. Expertise gets you to the frontier — below it, insights may be *individually* creative but not *socially* creative. Beyond the frontier, chance operates twice: **inside the search** (by definition, if the space collapsed to one obvious answer it would not be beyond the frontier, so random exploration is required even for the most expert) and **in reception** (nobody, creators included, can reliably foresee long-term impact, so every work is partly a gamble).

### The investment theory of creativity (Sternberg & Lubart)
Creators are stock pickers, betting on which ideas, methods, or areas will rise in value. Like speculation, outcomes depend heavily on chance. **The difference between routine and creative experts is largely appetite for risk.** Supporting datum: historian Anton Howes finds inventors are much more likely to have had contact with another inventor before inventing anything themselves — suggesting the *willingness* to take risky bets is culturally transmitted, separately from the expertise.

### Invention vs. innovation (Edison's distinction)
An **invention** is a creative work judged on technical merits. An **innovation** is measured by social impact. Edison: "Anything that won't sell, I don't want to invent. Its sale is proof of utility and utility is success." — not greed: "My main purpose in life is to make enough money to make ever more inventions."

## Key Concepts

- **Individual vs. social creativity — the Gauss anecdote.** Asked at six to sum 1 + 2 + ... + 10, Gauss answered "55!" immediately: the numbers pair into five sets summing to eleven (1+10, 2+9, 3+8, ...). Ingenious, but the trick was already known to mathematicians, so it had **no social influence** despite signalling a creative mind.
- **Multiple discoveries.** Darwin and Wallace on evolution; Newton and Leibniz on calculus; Bell and Gray filing telephone patents within hours; **four** scientists discovering sunspots all in **1611**; **no fewer than nine** inventors claiming the optical telescope. Even the *concept* of multiple discovery has been rediscovered repeatedly — Robert Merton counted **eighteen** independent suggestions of it in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries alone. Priority disputes with sealed, postdated manuscripts only make sense if discoveries were already "in the air."
- **Demand-side limits.** Csikszentmihalyi: "The common belief is that if creativity is rare, it is because of supply-side limitations; in other words, because there are few geniuses. The truth seems to be that the limits to creativity lie on the demand side. If there is too little creativity, it is because both individually and collectively we cannot change our cognitive structures rapidly enough to recognize and adopt new ideas." He estimates roughly **ten thousand people** constitute the modern art field whose acceptance an artist needs.
- **Moderate originality wins.** Computer analyses of musical compositions find those of **moderate melodic originality** are most popular. Too original is incomprehensible.
- **Inventions that went unused for want of an environment.** The wheel existed in Mesoamerica centuries before Europeans arrived but appears only in children's toys — no native beasts of burden to make carts practical. Movable type existed in Korea centuries before Gutenberg, but the literary language used Chinese characters, requiring thousands of unique symbols and enormous material cost.
- **Serendipity's roll call**: penicillin (Fleming noticing mould suppressing bacterial samples), saccharin (Fahlberg accidentally ingesting a reaction product), superglue (Harry Coover, while seeking cheap plastic gun sights for the military), Teflon, dynamite, vulcanized rubber, safety glass, Viagra. Paul Souriau: *"Le principe de l'invention est le hasard."* Modern cases: semaglutide (Ozempic) developed for diabetes; sildenafil (Viagra) developed for hypertension.
- **The workaholism finding.** Herbert Simon reportedly worked **100 hours/week** at his most productive; Einstein developed stomach problems working on general relativity; Balzac wrote **85 novels** at fifteen hours a day for twenty years. Distinguished physical and social scientists work **60–70 hours per week with few vacations**; "Type A" psychologists are more likely to be highly cited than "Type B." Edison: "genius is one percent inspiration and ninety-nine percent perspiration" — and he was an absent father and husband.

## Mental Models

- **The creative portfolio.** Creative projects are high-risk stocks; pair them with "the intellectual equivalent of treasury bonds" — low-risk reliable work and income to fall back on — so the risky bets stay palatable.
- **Ripeness.** Some ideas are ready; others lack key components. Robert Kanigel on Lasker Award-winning neuroscientist Solomon Snyder: he "had almost a sixth sense for scientific questions apt to leave him batting his head against the wall — and he avoided them."
- **Heroic → institutional innovation.** Edison lived through the transition from the lone inventor profiting from a gadget to industrial labs and university departments. If invention is search, deeper search demands more training and specialization — hence today's large teams of doctorate-holding specialists working on billions of transistors per square inch or ultracold superconductors. Edison's success with little schooling partly reflects how impoverished electrical understanding then was.

## Anti-patterns

- **Perfectionism / creative resistance** — withholding work until it is flawless reduces shots taken.
- **Assuming you can pick your winners.** Edison could not: the electric lighting system made his legacy; the iron-mining electromagnet venture squandered his fortune.
- **Abandoning a field for polymathy.** Expertise concentrates; Edison's apparent range was almost all novel applications of electrical circuitry.
- **Working on unripe problems.**
- **Letting noncreative work expand.** Zuckerman's Nobel laureates reported research slowing markedly after the prize — overwhelmed by interviews, public functions, and prestigious committees, where before they had laboured in obscurity with full hours for work. Edison's own productivity dipped as business interests grew and he managed ever-larger engineering teams.
- **Raising output by lowering standards.** The author's own caveat: "I could write more books if I simply banged on the keyboard and published whatever nonsense text emerged."

## Reference Tables

### The four strategies for creating more
| # | Strategy | Mechanism | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **The assembly-line method** | Routinize the *non*-creative parts so the creative parts get more shots | Jerry Seinfeld separated phases into different writers' rooms — one for pitching ideas, one for outlining, another for edits; ideas came from writers' real lives but the workflow ensured polished episodes, not half-baked ideas, aired. Generally: routines, checklists, systematic phases. A scientist can streamline grant writing and paper submission to protect lab time. Also cures **creative resistance** — "when the working up and releasing of new creative works is on autopilot, there's little time for paralytic self-censoring" |
| 2 | **Allow ideas to ripen** | Avoid impasses you cannot yet cross; wait for missing components | Edison ran many inventive projects simultaneously so he could switch when stuck and exploit chance discoveries. Many authors keep extensive notes on potential stories until enough pieces assemble. You often can't predict the solution, but you *can* estimate the size of the gap |
| 3 | **Moderate the risks** | Make volatility survivable so you can keep betting | Edison rationalized that he could always go back to being a telegraph operator; early frugal living made financial reversal less terrifying. Build a portfolio: risky projects plus reliable fallback income |
| 4 | **Spend less time in noncreative work** | Raise the ratio of creative hours to total hours | Feynman feigned irresponsibility to avoid time-wasting departmental assignments; Cal Newport's strict boundaries around "deep work" |

### Time-to-first-masterwork (John Hayes)
| Domain | Sample | Finding |
|---|---|---|
| Composers | 76 famous composers, 500 notable works | Only **three** works occurred before the tenth year of musical instruction — and those in years **eight and nine** |
| Painters | 131 painters | At least **six years** before a recognized masterwork; masterwork quantity rose steadily over the following six years |
| Poets | 66 poets | **No** notable poems before five years into a career; **55 of 66** had none until after the tenth year |

| Source | Finding |

## Worked Example — Thomas Edison as the equal-odds machine

**Output.** 1,093 patents — a strong claim to being the most inventive person in history. Multiplex telegraphy (multiple signals down one telegraph wire); the fluoroscope (first device for clear X-ray pictures); the kinetograph (an early motion picture camera); the tasimeter (measuring infrared light); the carbon microphone (a practical necessity for telephones); rechargeable batteries; and — while almost completely deaf — the phonograph. Menlo Park became the model that inspired Bell Labs, General Electric and DuPont. On his death in 1931, shutting off all electricity for two minutes was briefly considered as tribute, then abandoned because the outage would cause chaos.

**Expertise narrowing the search (why the lightbulb).** Arc lighting already existed; early incandescent bulbs existed but burned out fast and drew too much power to be commercial. Edison's move came from **Ohm's law**: a high-resistance filament draws less current than a low-resistance one. While competitors chased robust materials that could survive intense heat and current, Edison pursued **increasingly delicate** filaments — thinner wire, higher resistance, less current, economically viable light. He also invented the surrounding system: **parallel circuit connections, high-efficiency dynamos, and the central power station**. The choice was driven by a cost analysis of lighting, not a technical one.

**Quantity as method.** He tested countless materials before carbonized paper strips worked, then tested **thousands of other carbonized plant fibres**, settling on bamboo. Seeking a rubber substitute later in life he examined **fourteen thousand plants** for high latex content cultivable in a temperate climate. When Walter Mallory offered sympathy over months of fruitless battery work: **"Why, man, I've got a lot of results. I know several thousand things that won't work!"** Tesla's derision: "If Edison had a needle to find in a haystack, he would proceed at once with the diligence of the bee to examine straw after straw until he found the object of his search." The book's defence: chemical theory was nascent; few reliable predictions were available except by trying.

**The missed bet that proves the model.** Edison observed his bulbs blackening from the inside except for a "shadow" left by the filament's positive end, and reasoned correctly that carbon particles were shooting off the negative end. Adding a second wire inside the bulb, he got current to cross the vacuum — the **"Edison effect."** He treated it as a curiosity. It was the first step toward **vacuum tube electronics**, the building block of early computers; with more theoretical knowledge he might have pursued the electron and ushered in the electronic age. He left it untrodden to chase commercial lighting.

**Training even without schooling.** Largely unschooled but a voracious reader and experimenter: "My refuge was the Detroit Public Library. I started with the first book on the bottom shelf and went through the lot, one by one. I didn't read a few books. I read the library." First patent at **22** (an electronic voting machine); first widely celebrated invention at **27** (multiplex telegraphy) — consistent with Hayes's decade-of-training pattern.

**The cost.** Weeks living at the laboratory, returning home only to pass out in soiled clothes. The famous photograph of him slumped and disheveled beside his phonograph followed a **seventy-two-hour** nonstop tinkering binge on sound quality. The (probably hyperbolic) anecdote: asked why he was working so late and told it was midnight — "Midnight! Is that so. I must get home then, I was married today." On the financial swings of a life spent ploughing every profit back into risky ventures, often to the brink of bankruptcy: **"At least I did not have ennui."**

## Key Takeaways

1. The most eminent creators are the most prolific; hit rate stays flat, so total shots determine total hits.
2. You cannot pick your winners in advance — neither can experts, investors, or the creators themselves.
3. Expertise buys you a seat at the frontier; below it your best insights are individually but not socially creative.
4. Environment gates acceptance — moderate originality travels, extreme originality often doesn't, and unreceptive contexts kill good inventions (Mesoamerican wheel, Korean movable type).
5. Chance operates inside the search and in reception; embrace variation-and-selection rather than seeking a mechanism that avoids it.
6. Risk appetite, not knowledge, separates routine experts from creative ones — and it appears to be culturally transmitted.
7. Raise output structurally: assembly-line the routine parts, let unripe ideas wait, hedge the risk, and defend the ratio of creative hours.
8. Expect a decade of training before a masterwork, in composition, painting, or poetry.
