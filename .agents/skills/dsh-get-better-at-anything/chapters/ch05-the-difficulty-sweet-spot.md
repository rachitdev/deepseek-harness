# Chapter 5 — The Difficulty Sweet Spot

*Part II — Do: Learning from Practice. Maxim 5 of 12.*

> "This is a fundamental truth about any sort of practice: If you never push yourself beyond your comfort zone, you will never improve." — Anders Ericsson and Robert Pool

Chapter's opening questions: When is difficulty helpful for learning? Should you solve problems first or study an example? How can you integrate examples, action, and feedback into a practice loop?

## Core Idea

Progress lives in a narrow band between too easy and too hard. Some difficulties are **desirable** (retrieval, spacing) because they force the brain — a relentless effort-saving machine — to actually store knowledge; others are pure waste (excess cognitive load, inaccessible problems). The practical instrument is the **practice loop**: see an example → solve a problem → get feedback, cycled repeatedly, with difficulty tuned upward as skill grows. And because experts continually raise their own problem difficulty (**progressive problem solving**), mastery never feels easy.

## Frameworks Introduced

### The practice loop
| Component | Why it is required | Failure mode if omitted |
|---|---|---|
| **See** (examples) | You cannot invent every pattern; examples show the ocean of possibilities | You must reinvent strategies, or never find them |
| **Do** (solve problems) | "Seeing can assist with doing, but it can never replace it." Action guides attention — students don't study worked examples until they hit a problem needing one | Nothing is internalized; the brain skips storage |
| **Feedback** | Tells you which attempts were wrong and how | Years of unguided effort (Butler's early rejection slips) |

**Tuning the loop as skill grows:** examples fade out; problems grow in complexity; **self-assessment increasingly replaces external feedback** as your sense of excellent work refines.

### Desirable difficulties (Robert and Elizabeth Bjork)
- **Retrieval over review** — successfully recalling strengthens memory far more than re-seeing. Why flashcards beat re-reading notes.
- **Spaced over massed practice** — massing improves performance fast and produces equally fast forgetting. Cramming fills your head temporarily and empties it before you can use the material.
- **Why they work** (John Anderson's rational-adaptation account): if the solution is visible whenever you need it, there is no reason to store it; if a problem recurs in a burst, the answer is probably needed only temporarily and can be discarded; if cues are always present, retrieval effort can be saved.

### Progressive problem solving and the writer's paradox
Expert writers thinking aloud look like **novices**: effortful search, frequent impasses, means-ends analysis. Children write with expert-like **fluency**. Scardamalia and Bereiter found children were bewildered to hear that adults sometimes spend fifteen minutes thinking before starting to write. Resolution: **they are solving different problems**. Children use a **knowledge-telling strategy** — write things as they come to mind (which persists into adulthood in stream-of-consciousness emails). Experts set themselves harder problems: an original story, a persuaded audience, something interesting said well. Same pattern elsewhere: physicists at the frontier are not doing textbook problems faster; grandmasters are not practising checkmating novices. "The mindlessness of expertise seems mostly at play when we are dealing with skills we aren't trying to improve" — driving is automatic because we do not care to solve its deeper problems.

### Zones of optimal challenge
| Theory | Author | Claim |
|---|---|---|
| **Zone of proximal development** | Lev Vygotsky | Learning happens in the gap between what you can do with assistance and what you can do alone |
| **Zone of learnability** | Walter Kintsch | Low-prior-knowledge students learn best from well-organized texts; **high-knowledge students learn better from poorly organized ones** |
| **Expertise-reversal effect** | Cognitive load theorists | Interventions that help beginners lose value and eventually turn negative as skill grows |

## Key Concepts

- **Pure discovery learning fails.** Richard Mayer proposes a "three strikes rule" against it. Sweller, Kirschner and Clark go further against all minimally guided instruction: "After a half century of advocacy associated with instruction using minimal guidance, it appears that there is no body of research supporting the technique. In so far as there is any evidence from controlled studies, it almost uniformly supports direct, strong instructional guidance."
- **Practice is not the enemy of understanding.** Anderson, Simon and Reder: "Nothing flies more in the face of the last 20 years of research than the assertion that practice is bad. All evidence, from the laboratory and from extensive case studies of professionals, indicates that real competence only comes with extensive practice."
- **Sequencing is contested but secondary.** The book's verdict: "The effect of sequencing may be of theoretical interest, but it has fewer practical consequences than the harms of omitting one of the ingredients altogether."
- **Desk-drawer syndrome** — filing work away unseen to avoid rejection. "Such ego-saving maneuvers can reduce the sting of rejection, [but] they also guarantee stagnation."

## Mental Models

- **Renting an audience.** Butler on workshops: "A workshop is a way of renting an audience, and making sure you're communicating what you think you're communicating. It's so easy as a young writer to think you've been very clear when in fact you haven't."
- **The ocean problem.** Butler: "One of the problems we have as writers is that we either know too much or not enough... We know that there is an ocean of possibilities out there, and we're overwhelmed. We don't know how to take from the ocean just what we need."
- **Scaffolding** — a temporary structure removed once the building stands. Training wheels; motherese (exaggerated infant-directed speech) as instinctive scaffolding.
- **No one becomes a master.** Hemingway, as quoted: writers "are all apprentices at a craft where no one ever becomes a master."

## Anti-patterns

- **Cramming / massed practice** — fast gains, fast loss.
- **Reviewing notes instead of retrieving.**
- **Pure discovery / minimally guided instruction.**
- **Withholding work from feedback (desk-drawer syndrome).**
- **Cut-and-paste copying** without understanding — the legitimate half of the critique of imitation; completion problems are the fix.
- **Letting fear of a bigger format stall you.** Butler's move to novels — the move that secured her professional status — "was likely delayed by at least a few years owing to her apprehensions about tackling more complex works."
- **Staying in the automated zone.** If you never raise the problem difficulty, you plateau by design.

## Reference Tables

### The three difficulty-tuning strategies
| # | Strategy | Mechanism | Concrete forms |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **The workshop method** | Rents an audience; runs a complete practice loop; acts as a forcing mechanism against avoidance | Clarion-style: write nightly, read peers' work, have all of it dissected next day, led by an experienced teacher |
| 2 | **Copy–Complete–Create** | A continuum from zero generation to full generation, keeping load manageable while forcing engagement | Study the worked example → **completion problems** (parts deleted) → produce it yourself in varied contexts. Van Merriënboer found completion problems speed novice programming acquisition; **cloze deletion** flashcards do the same for language, avoiding both context-free isolated words and whole-sentence recall |
| 3 | **Scaffolding** | Temporarily reduce degrees of freedom — subtractively *or* additively | Training wheels; motherese; Scardamalia and Bereiter's sentence-evaluation prompts ("People may not believe this," "I think this could be said more clearly") which elicited expert-like reflective writing from children years early; practising a conversation with the explicit aim of using one grammatical pattern |

### The sequencing debate: problem-first vs. example-first
| Position | Evidence |
|---|---|
| **Problem first** — Manu Kapur's **productive failure** | Students tackle a difficult but comprehensible problem without instruction, typically fail to solve it expertly, then are shown the canonical procedure contrasted with their attempts (e.g. drafting the more *consistent* batter → then variance is taught). Kapur's **2021 meta-analysis reviewed over 160 experimental effects** and found a benefit |
| **Invention first** — Daniel Schwartz and Taylor Martin | Let students invent methods first; they benefit from noticing gaps and recognizing where knowledge applies |
| **Example first** | Ashman, Kalyuga & Sweller (lightbulb-efficiency science lesson): example-first students performed better. Glogger-Frey et al.: same direction — "a worked example is more effective than working on an open (inventing) problem when providing equal time on task during such a preparatory activity" |
| **Guidance matters, timing doesn't** | Matlen & Klahr (control-of-variables strategy): the highest-guidance group performed best, with **no difference for timing** — students "learned and transferred relatively well as long as they received high guidance at some point during instruction" |

## Worked Example — Octavia Butler's escalating difficulty ladder

The chapter's case history, read as a sequence of deliberately raised problem difficulties.

**Starting conditions (no See, no Feedback).** Father dead at seven; mother with a third-grade education working as a domestic servant; bullied until she outgrew her attackers at six feet. An aunt: "Honey, negros can't be writers." Butler: "I had no examples, I had no idea what I was doing wrong in my work. This is true of a lot of people who are beginning to be writers. They don't know what's wrong. They don't know why it keeps getting rejected."

| Stage | Problem she set herself | What changed in the loop |
|---|---|---|
| Age 10 | Write at all | — |
| Age 13 | Submit for publication — found a discarded copy of *The Writer* on the bus, mailed a story, got her first rejection. A fraudulent literary agent then took **$61**, more than a month's rent | Still no examples, no feedback |
| Teens–20s | Sheer volume. Woke at **3 a.m.**, wrote all morning, then odd jobs — "I preferred blue-collar work, because with white-collar work you have to pretend you were enjoying it, and I wasn't." Pawned a spare typewriter when food ran low; was happiest when laid off | **Do** only. Imitative content: "stories about thirty-year-old white men who drank and smoked too much" |
| Screen Writers' Guild free class | Sought direct criticism from teacher Sid Steeple: "Whatever you wrote he would go over it and talk to you about it and you might go home feeling like you didn't much like him but it was the kind of criticism I needed" | **Feedback** added |
| Clarion Workshop (6 weeks, reached by Greyhound to Pennsylvania after Harlan Ellison's encouragement) | **A new short story every night**, dissected in class the next day | Full loop, high tempo. She discovered "the things I had been learning in English class weren't that useful... academic writing is just plain different from fiction." **Sold her first story by the end** — "Childfinder," about covert groups recruiting children with unusual mental abilities |
| Post-Clarion | Novels instead of short stories (better advances). The length intimidated her; her decomposition: "I had been able to finish some short stories, which were about twenty pages long, and I finally decided to try writing twenty-page chapters until I finished each novel" | Scaffolding by chunking. Sold *Patternmaster* to Doubleday |
| Next five years | **Five novels in five years**; full-time writer by the third | Volume at higher complexity |
| Fourth novel — *Kindred* | Deliberately harder: a Black woman thrown back in time to the antebellum South, requiring far more research and historical accuracy than she was used to. She spent advance money travelling to Maryland to visit plantations where scenes would be set | Her most commercially successful work; secured her legacy |

**Why it never got easier.** Lifelong creative blocks; a compulsive rewriter who threw out large sections; once offered to return an advance for a novel that wasn't meeting her standards. Asked at the peak of her success whether her writing was "organic": "No, it's work... It's certainly not a matter of sitting there and having things fall from the sky."

**Butler's own practice-loop prescription for stuck writers** — the chapter's most directly reusable artifact:
> "If they have difficulty with beginnings — they have wonderful stories to tell but don't know where to begin or how to begin — I have them look at work they enjoy reading... I then ask them to copy half a dozen beginnings; I ask them to copy directly, word for word... This is not about imitating someone else's beginnings; that's why I want at least a half a dozen. It's about learning what is possible."

Note the design: **six** examples, copied **verbatim**, precisely so that no single one becomes the template. The output is a sampled distribution of options, not a model to clone.

## Key Takeaways

1. Build a practice loop — see, do, feedback — and check which of the three is missing before diagnosing anything else.
2. Not all difficulty helps. Retrieval and spacing help; excess cognitive load and out-of-reach problems don't.
3. Retrieval beats review; spacing beats cramming. The brain stores only what it cannot cheaply re-access.
4. Escalate deliberately (progressive problem solving) — otherwise automaticity becomes a ceiling.
5. Expert difficulty is self-imposed; if a skill feels easy you have stopped setting harder problems.
6. Pure discovery underperforms; so does pure observation. Sequencing matters far less than including all three ingredients.
7. Use workshops for the forcing function; copy→complete→create for graded generation; scaffolds to strip or add degrees of freedom.
8. Fade the scaffolds: fewer examples, harder problems, more self-assessment.
