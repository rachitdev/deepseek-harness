# Chapter 2 — Creativity Begins with Copying

*Part I — See: Learning from Others. Maxim 2 of 12.*

> "Rules are not the fetters of genius. They are the fetters of men with no genius." — Joshua Reynolds, painter

Chapter's opening questions: Can you solve a problem without learning how to solve it? How much of creativity comes from borrowing others' ideas? Does imitation lead to a shallow understanding?

## Core Idea

Imitation is not the enemy of originality; it is its precondition. The mechanism is the **mind's bottleneck**: working memory holds only a handful of items, and effortful problem solving (especially means-ends analysis) consumes the very capacity needed to *learn* the pattern being used. Hence Sweller's finding that you can solve a problem without learning how you solved it. **Worked examples** and **goal-free problems** remove that overhead — which is exactly what Renaissance apprenticeship did by starting with copying.

## Frameworks Introduced

### Cognitive load theory (John Sweller)
- **Intrinsic load** — the unavoidable mental effort of the learning itself (studying a worked example requires engagement).
- **Extrinsic load** — all mental effort *not* directed at learning. The target for elimination.
- **The worked-example effect** — for novices, studying a solved problem (with all intervening steps) beats solving the problem yourself.
- **The goal-free effect** — removing the specific goal ("find as many unknown quantities as you can" instead of "find angle X") suppresses means-ends analysis and frees capacity for pattern learning.
- **The split-attention effect** — materials that force you to shuttle between separated pieces (a diagram and a numbered key) add extrinsic load. Put labels where their referents are.
- **The redundancy effect** — duplicating the same content in two channels (reading slides aloud verbatim; a diagram plus identical text) *worsens* learning; the viewer burns capacity filtering. Sweller: "Redundancy is anything but harmless. Providing unnecessary information may be a major reason for instructional failure."
- **The expertise-reversal effect** — these prescriptions flip as expertise develops. Once patterns are in memory, learners benefit more from problem solving than from more examples.

### The mind's bottleneck — three landmark results
| Researcher(s) | Year | Contribution |
|---|---|---|
| George Miller | 1956 | "The magical number seven, plus or minus two" — the same limit appears in discriminating pitch (good to 2–3, errors beyond 6), loudness (~5 distinguishable volumes), saltiness, area of squares, hues, and memory for digits/words. *Footnote in the book: contemporary work (Cowan) puts the real number closer to four.* |
| Atkinson & Shiffrin | 1968 | The **modal model** — parallel sensory input squeezed through a central short-term bottleneck, connected to a dormant long-term store |
| Baddeley & Hitch | 1974 (as described) | **Working memory** — short-term storage *plus* the ability to manipulate and transform its contents |
| Ericsson & Kintsch | 1995 | **Long-term working memory / retrieval structures** — experience lets you build retrieval cues so long-term memory extends short-term capacity |

### Chunking as the escape hatch
Miller's own loophole: the limit is on *items*, not information. `N U F H S B L A I` is nine unmanageable letters; reorganized as **FBI, USA, NHL** it is three chunks. Chunks are built by experience — the beginner is juggling more items than the expert *for the same problem*.

### Epistemology vs. pedagogy (Paul Kirschner)
The fallacy of confusing **how experts invent new knowledge** with **how novices should be taught what is already known**. Experienced practitioners, who genuinely learn better by doing, wrongly extrapolate that method to beginners.

## Key Concepts

- **Renaissance apprenticeship sequence** — the concrete curriculum: (1) copy masterworks; (2) draw from plaster casts of sculptures (3-D → 2-D, no moving model); (3) draw from life, with fundamentals secure enough to attend to posture and expression. Media progressed charcoal → grisaille (black and white) → full color oil/tempera.
- **Cennini's timetable** (turn of the 15th century): one year as a shopboy drawing on a little panel; a good six years serving under a master learning all branches; six more years painting, gilding, working on walls — "drawing all the time, never leaving off, either on holidays or on workdays."
- **Da Vinci's own ordering**: "Of the order of learning to draw: first draw from drawings by masters done from works of art," then "having acquired that practice, under the criticism of his master, he should next practice drawing objects in relief of a good style."
- **Expert gaze following** — eye-tracking studies show students learn more when they can follow experts' eye movements. This may explain the workshop's power: you get not only the master's methods but his *eye* for the problem.
- **Complexity vs. volume** — cognitive load theory bites when many unfamiliar elements must be integrated at once (Japanese SVO→SOV word order; quantum electron orbitals; every brushstroke's hue, saturation, and tonal value plus perspective, lighting, size). It matters far less for low-interactivity material (vocabulary lists, periodic-table masses, traffic-sign recognition), which immersion handles fine.

## Mental Models

- **Craft before creativity.** "Originality is simply an exploration of an undervisited region of the problem space." Technical virtuosity and vision are complementary: "It is easier to see further when you have first mastered the techniques. It is only possible to bend the rules when you know which ones can be broken."
- **Solving ≠ learning.** Success on a problem is not evidence you extracted the method.
- **The pendulum of art education** — Greece (art celebrated, artist an artisan) → medieval guilds → Vasari elevates the artist to intellectual, founds the first academy in Florence, 1561, under Cosimo de' Medici → Rousseau ("I shall take good care not to provide him with a drawing master, who would only set him to copy copies... Nature should be his only teacher") → Franz Cizek's creative self-expression movement, more extreme than Rousseau, "not taught at all in the usual sense of teaching." Arthur Efland's framing: "When the artist rose to the position of genius... how does one instruct a genius?" Juliette Aristides on the cost: the artist "can become stuck in a perpetual adolescence where his passion outstrips his ability to perform."

## Anti-patterns

- **Making beginners discover everything.** Piaget: "Each time one prematurely teaches a child something he could have discovered for himself the child is kept from inventing it..." — the chapter's evidence contradicts the strong form of this.
- **Diagrams with separated keys/legends** (split attention).
- **Saying aloud exactly what is on the slide** (redundancy).
- **Worked examples with steps omitted** — much harder to follow. Amateur artists could not learn from finished copies alone; observing the *process* mattered.
- **Keeping novices on worked examples too long** — expertise reversal; eventually copying must be replaced by original observation. Da Vinci, despite prescribing copying, was a strong proponent of learning from nature as ability developed.
- **Experts designing beginner curricula from their own current experience** (epistemology/pedagogy fallacy).

## Reference Tables

### The five applications of cognitive load theory
| # | Application | What to actually do |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Seek out worked examples | Choose resources dense in fully worked solutions; later cover the answers to convert them into practice |
| 2 | Reorganize confusing materials | Recopy diagrams so labels sit next to referents; rewrite formulas with each variable's plain-English meaning in place |
| 3 | Use the power of pretraining | Isolate hard components and commit them to memory first (flashcard vocabulary before speaking; colour mixing, light-to-shadow, perspective before painting) |
| 4 | Introduce complexity slowly | Start simple, add features steadily — as video game tutorial levels expose a few mechanics and let goal-free exploration do the teaching |
| 5 | Put craft before creativity | Master technique first; originality is exploration of an undervisited region of the space |

### The experimental results, side by side
| Study | Design | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Sweller, goal-free (trigonometry) | Standard find-the-missing-angle problems vs. "find as many unknown quantities as you can"; transfer test = draw a diagram from given sines and cosines | **8 of 10** goal-free students solved the novel problem vs. **3 of 10** conventional |
| Sweller, worked examples (algebra) | Worked solution paired with a matching problem vs. solve-it-yourself (with a worked example supplied after 5 minutes of failure) | Equal on similar problems; on a **novel question format, 75% of the worked-example group solved it vs. 0% of the problem-solving group** — and the problem-solving group had spent **three times as long** training |
| Klahr & Nigam, control of variables | 112 third- and fourth-graders; direct instruction + examples vs. discovery | **77%** of instructed students ran ≥3 of 4 unconfounded experiments vs. **23%** of discovery students. Students who did well on the test — *however they learned it* — also did better at a later science fair |

## Worked Example — Sweller's accidental discovery

The chapter reproduces Sweller's own account of the experiment that started cognitive load theory:

1. **The task.** Undergraduates had to transform a given number into a goal number, with only two legal moves: multiply by 3, or subtract 29. Example: 15 → 16. Solution: 15 × 3 = 45; 45 − 29 = 16.
2. **The hidden structure.** Every problem in the set had exactly one solution, and every solution required *alternating* ×3 and −29 a specific number of times.
3. **The anomaly.** "My undergraduates found these problems relatively easy to solve with very few failures, but there was something strange about their solutions. While all problems had to be solved by this alternation sequence... very few students discovered the rule." Sweller: "Whatever the problem solvers were doing to solve the problems, learning the alternating solution sequence rule did not play a part."
4. **The diagnosis.** Means-ends analysis — Simon and Newell's weak method (see chapter 1) — solves the problem but requires holding so much of it in mind that no capacity remains to generalize the procedure. "Perhaps we should be showing students how to solve problems rather than having them solve the problems themselves?"
5. **The test.** Suppress means-ends analysis by removing the goal → the goal-free trigonometry experiment (8/10 vs. 3/10 above). Then replace search entirely with worked examples → the algebra experiment (75% vs. 0% on transfer, at one third the training time).
6. **The reception.** "It was the worst possible time to be publishing papers calling into question the efficacy of using problem-solving as a learning device... The research on worked-examples was treated with hostility, or more commonly, ignored, a state of affairs that lasted about two decades."

**Reading it as a design pattern:** whenever a learner *can* succeed at a task by brute search, ask whether that search is consuming the capacity that would otherwise encode the method. If yes, strip the goal or supply the solution.

## Key Takeaways

1. Solving a problem and learning its method are different events; effortful search can crowd out the second.
2. Working memory holds ~4 (Miller said 7 ± 2) items. All instructional design flows from that number.
3. For novices, worked examples and goal-free tasks beat problem solving — often dramatically on transfer, at a fraction of the time.
4. Learning by example is not shallower: Klahr and Nigam found instructed students transferred at least as well.
5. Cut extrinsic load: fix split attention, delete redundancy, pretrain components, ramp complexity.
6. Reverse the prescription as expertise grows (expertise-reversal effect) — eventually you must do, not watch.
7. Copying is upstream of originality, not opposed to it. The Renaissance workshop produced breathtakingly original work *through* years of imitation.
