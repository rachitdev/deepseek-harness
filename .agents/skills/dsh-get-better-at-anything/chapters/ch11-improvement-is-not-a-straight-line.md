# Chapter 11 — Improvement Is Not a Straight Line

*Part III — Feedback: Learning from Experience. Maxim 11 of 12.*

> "Wisdom doesn't consist in knowing more that is new, but less that is false." — Henry Wheeler Shaw

Chapter's opening questions: When do we first need to get worse, before getting better? What are the risks of unlearning? Why does progress eventually stall without corrective feedback?

## Core Idea

Past mastery interferes with future mastery. Automatized motor programs, habitual solution patterns, and intuitive folk theories all resist replacement — and the replacement is *worse than the incumbent* until it has been practiced enough to compete. So the path to a higher peak runs **down into a valley first**, and some people never climb out. Improvement therefore depends less on adding knowledge than on **removing what is false**, which requires corrective feedback, a deliberate return to conscious control, and enough practice on the new method that it becomes fluent under pressure.

## Frameworks Introduced

### Fitts & Posner's three phases of motor learning (1967)
1. **Cognitive phase** — the performer tries to understand the task, what is required, and how to perform it. Movements are under conscious, deliberate control while the learner works out the correct technique.
2. **Associative phase** — with a basic understanding in place, the performer tries different things; major errors are gradually eliminated and performance smooths out. Movements get calibrated across conditions (woods vs. irons, fairway vs. rough).
3. **Autonomous phase** — errors weeded out, skill becomes effortless; the explicit instructions from phase 1 may be *forgotten entirely*. The skill becomes almost reflex-like.

Two consequences the chapter draws out:
- Once autonomous, **internally focused thoughts about bodily mechanics interfere**. Best performance comes from an **external focus on the goal** of the movement, not on how to execute it.
- Any significant change **regresses you to the cognitive phase**, and you must travel the whole road again — through the associative phase, weeding errors across varied conditions, until the new movement is automatic enough that **the old one won't spontaneously resurface under tournament pressure**.

### Flexibility vs. a new motor program
No skilled movement repeats with machine-like precision — if it did, athletic performance would be impossible, since wind, grass, and ground firmness vary. All skills carry built-in flexibility to adapt. **But flexibility is bounded.** You can move hunt-and-peck typing from a large keyboard to a small one; no amount of that practice ever turns you into a touch typist. Finding a genuinely new way to perform a skill requires **building a new motor program from scratch**, not tweaking the existing one.

### Einstellung (Abraham Luchins)
Perseveration of a habitual mode of problem solving — a mindset *"which predisposes an organism to one type of motor or conscious act."* See the Worked Example.

### Functional fixedness (Karl Duncker)
Perceiving an object in one role makes alternative uses hard to see. The candle problem: subjects must affix candles to a wall given boxes, candles, and tacks. When the boxes were **empty**, *all* subjects found the solution — tack the boxes to the wall as little platforms holding the candles. When the boxes came **filled with the tacks**, **fewer than half** solved it. Seeing the box as a *container* rather than a *platform* reshaped the whole problem.

### Non-monotonic learning
Performance that gets **worse before better** as a normal part of acquisition:
- **Overregularization in the past tense.** English-speaking children first use irregular verbs picked up from adults ("I went there," "I did it"). Then they learn the -ed rule and enter a period of overregularization ("I goed there") — *less* grammatical than before. Finally they learn which verbs are exceptions and use both forms correctly. Young children are also frequently impervious to correction, as any parent adjusting a toddler's pronunciation has found.
- **The intermediate effect (Vimla Patel).** Early medical students and expert clinicians both make **few elaborations** when reasoning about patient problems; **intermediate students draw far more inferences and dredge up more medical information than either**. Novices elaborate little because they have little to recall; experts elaborate little because they know the likely answer and focus only on critical information, ignoring irrelevancies.
- **The moderate experience hypothesis (Robert Siegler).** People carry the **greatest variety of strategies — good and bad — at intermediate levels of experience**: enough knowledge for multiple approaches, not enough for the optimal method to have suppressed the alternatives.

### The three tactics for unlearning
1. **Introduce new constraints.**
2. **Find a coach.**
3. **Renovate rather than rebuild.**

## Key Concepts

- **Deliberate practice as a deliberate regression.** Ericsson's model holds that stagnation results from the transition to Fitts & Posner's autonomous mode. The **"deliberate" in deliberate practice means shifting back to the cognitive phase**, where aspects of the skill are under direct conscious supervision — combined with immediate feedback so conscious adjustments can be made. Woods fits the model exactly: relentless practice plus consciously reasoned adjustments driven by feedback.
- **Constraints as indirect correction.** Constraints can be **prohibitions** (write an essay without adverbs; paint without color) or **requirements** (hit with an undersized racquet face, forcing center contact). Constraints are a hallmark of good design practice because, absent restrictions, the solution space is so large that **stale ideas overwhelm the search for original ones**. Crucially, a well-chosen constraint pushes you away from a bad habit **without focusing attention on it** — unlike direct instruction on body mechanics, which can cause the **"yips,"** where excessive attention to one's own movement ruins the golf swing.
- **"Feel is not real."** Golf-improvement lore capturing the fact that performers systematically misperceive their own movement. A putter thinks he is barely holding the grip while strangling the club; a golfer imagines a full rotation while bringing the club only halfway. A distorted self-image is what makes unaided self-improvement so hard.
- **A coach need not be better than you.** Tiger Woods worked extensively with coaches who were far worse golfers. The coach's advantage is structural: **they can observe you without spending mental bandwidth performing the skill themselves.**
- **The ship of Theseus strategy.** Radical shifts in perspective — golf swing or scientific worldview — are safer made **one plank at a time**. "Seek the ridge between mountains rather than recklessly climb down to the valley below."
- **Buggy algorithms (Brown & VanLehn).** Children's arithmetic errors work like buggy computer code: not random slips but a *different, consistently applied procedure*. Classic bug in multi-digit subtraction: **always subtract the smaller digit from the larger one**. On "22 − 14 = ?", instead of borrowing from the tens column to compute 12 − 4 = 8, the student swaps the digits and writes 4 − 2 = 2 in the ones column. **Extra practice makes such a student worse**, further ingraining the wrong procedure. The fix is a teacher stopping the student, explaining the mistake, and restarting on correct footing.
- **Classroom knowledge that never leaves the classroom.** Physics students learn to calculate force and momentum like Newton but **continue to think like Aristotle outside class**. Economics students learn welfare-enhancing trade but **think like mercantilists when evaluating public policy**. And the folk idea that the mind is like a muscle survives despite the evidence (chapter 6).
- **Two kinds of misconception.** (a) **Popular but shallow** — e.g. *learning styles* (visual/auditory/kinesthetic matching), poorly supported yet persistent because it's intuitively plausible and few people know the research against it. Nobody arrives at it spontaneously. (b) **Deep and intuitive** — e.g. **impetus theories of motion**, which develop outside instruction and serve as useful approximations in our high-friction world. These are not replaced; the formal system is **learned in parallel**, and with enough expertise the naive intuition can be *suppressed* on problems that demand physics.
- **Why unlearning stalls even with feedback.** Unless the replacement strategy is sufficiently practiced, it remains **too effortful to compete with the old pattern** — so golfers regress to the old swing under pressure, and physics novices revert to intuition outside class because formal reasoning is effortful and error-prone while intuition is fast and easy. Experts lean on training not only because they distrust intuition but because **their training is so well practiced it costs little to execute.**
- **Simulations against misconceptions.** Physics Nobelist Carl Wieman argues for hands-on physics simulations letting students **predict** the motion of objects in idealized experiments, making the correct mental model salient. Interactive simulations improve conceptual knowledge over traditional instruction alone. Pure play with simulators won't produce scientific understanding — it must be combined with traditional equation work.
- **Paradigm inertia.** Max Planck: **"science proceeds one funeral at a time."** New doctoral students are likelier to lead a revolution because they are not burdened by calcified habits of thought. Einstein fought bitterly against the nondeterministic aspects of quantum mechanics despite having won his Nobel for discoveries that led to it.
- **Everyday valleys.** The career change that promises stability but restarts you at the bottom of a new industry; the search for a satisfying relationship that requires ending a dead-end one; the strategy against an upstart rival that requires winding down your existing business. Each requires descending from your current hill, with a real risk of never rising from the valley.

## Mental Models

- **Hills, valleys and ridges.** Improvement is hill-climbing on a landscape with multiple peaks. A higher peak requires descent. Prefer the **ridge** (incremental transition) to the **valley** (demolition) whenever a ridge exists.
- **The ship of Theseus.** Total replacement achieved plank by plank, never leaving you without a ship.
- **Regression to the cognitive phase.** The universal price of any real technique change: automatic becomes effortful again.
- **Buggy code, not carelessness.** Reframe persistent errors as a wrong *procedure* running correctly, which tells you that more repetition is the wrong prescription.
- **The gravity of old habits.** Under pressure the old pattern reasserts itself unless the new one is genuinely more fluent.

## Anti-patterns

- **Prescribing more practice for a buggy procedure.** It ingrains the bug. Diagnose the procedure first.
- **Attending to your own mechanics while performing an autonomous skill.** This is what produces the yips; use external focus during performance and reserve internal focus for practice.
- **Rebuilding from scratch by default.** Woods survived it; many did not — see the casualty list below.
- **Assuming the new method will feel better immediately.** It won't. Woods needed **eighteen months** and an underperforming tournament season.
- **Trusting your felt sense of your own technique.** Feel is not real.
- **Learning a rule and not learning its scope.** The ball-race study (below) shows a formally correct principle applied where it does not apply.
- **Waiting for the old guard to update.** Planck's rule: some paradigms change by replacement of people, not minds.

## Reference Tables

### Swing-change casualties vs. Woods
| Golfer | Outcome after rebuilding the swing |
|---|---|
| **David Gossett** | Prodigy; won the US Amateur at 19. Botched change scuttled his career. *"Chasing the almighty, great swing is not real."* |
| **Chip Beck** | Four PGA Tour wins; wanted to hit the ball higher. Quit golf a few years later to become an insurance salesman |
| **David Duval, Ian Baker-Finch, Seve Ballesteros** | All redesigned their swings. **None ever played competitively again** |
| **Tiger Woods** | 18 months of grinding + one underperforming season → **eight tournament wins the following year**, a feat not achieved since 1974; youngest golfer to complete the Career Grand Slam |

Sports journalist Scott Eden on the orthodoxy Woods defied: *"Long grooved into the frontal lobes of golf's conventional wisdom is the notion that every person has a 'born,' or 'natural,' swing,"* and *"to meddle with your natural swing is to meddle with your soul."* One commentator likened Woods's decision to Michael Jordan switching to left-handed jump shots for fun.

### Diagnosing what kind of unlearning you face
| Situation | Signature | Prescription |
|---|---|---|
| Bad habit learned early | Consistent wrong procedure; practice makes it worse | Corrective feedback, restart on correct footing |
| Deep intuitive misconception | Right answers in class, wrong reasoning outside | Learn formal system in parallel; simulate + predict; practice until suppression is cheap |
| Shallow popular misconception | Idea absorbed from culture, never derived | Exposure to the disconfirming research |
| Environment/body changed | Old method no longer fits | Renovate; expect a non-monotonic dip |
| Non-monotonic developmental dip | Performance drops mid-acquisition (overregularization, intermediate effect) | Don't panic; it resolves with continued exposure |

## Worked Example — Luchins's water-jar problem and the Einstellung effect

The experiment as the chapter reproduces it:

1. **The task.** Given jars of specific volumes, obtain an exact target quantity of water.
2. **A sample problem.** Obtain **20 liters** using only a **29-liter** jar and a **3-liter** jar. Solution: fill the 29-liter jar, then remove 3 liters, **three times** → 29 − 3 − 3 − 3 = 20.
3. **The training set.** Luchins presented a *sequence* of problems all solvable by the same pattern: **B − A − 2C** (fill the second jar, remove the first jar, remove twice the third). Subjects repeated this pattern many times.
4. **The critical trial.** A puzzle solvable **either** by the learned complicated pattern (B − A − 2C) **or** by a far simpler one: **A − C**.
5. **The result.** In the initial experiment, **none of the subjects noticed the simpler solution.** Every one of them ran the habitual routine.
6. **The name.** Luchins called this perseveration of a habitual problem-solving mode **Einstellung** — the mindset *"which predisposes an organism to one type of motor or conscious act."*

**The companion demonstration — Duncker's candle problem.** Same materials in both conditions (boxes, candles, tacks); the only difference is whether the boxes arrive **empty** or **filled with the tacks**. Empty boxes: **all** subjects found the platform solution. Filled boxes: **fewer than half** did. The book's Figure 11 illustrates this. Perceiving the box in its container role suppressed its platform role.

**The companion demonstration — the ball race (misconception after instruction).** Researchers showed a simulated race of two balls down differently shaped tracks that **began and ended at the same heights** but had different dips and hills. In one simulation, **one ball sped up as it climbed a hill**, catching the other. Few physics-*naive* students judged this realistic. Distressingly, **students who had taken the college physics class frequently said the unusual motion was realistic** — justifying it by invoking **conservation of mechanical energy**, which requires only that the balls have the *same speed* at the same final height, **not that they arrive at the same time**. Novices can apply what they learned in class; they often cannot tell **when it applies**.

**What the three together demonstrate:** prior success (Luchins), prior perceptual framing (Duncker), and prior instruction (the ball race) each install a pattern that runs automatically and blocks the better answer. None is corrected by trying harder; each requires being confronted with the failure and then practicing the alternative until it is cheap to run.

## Key Takeaways

1. Real technique change means regressing to the cognitive phase and re-earning automaticity. Budget for a performance decline — Woods paid eighteen months and a season.
2. Flexibility within a skill is bounded. Some improvements require a new motor program, not a tweak. Know which you are attempting.
3. Prevention beats unlearning: get correct technique and a good coach early, before habits calcify.
4. Persistent errors are usually a wrong procedure running correctly. More practice ingrains them; only diagnosis and correction help.
5. Confronting a misconception is necessary but insufficient — the replacement must be practiced until it is more fluent than the incumbent, or the old pattern returns under pressure.
6. Getting worse mid-way is often normal, not failure: overregularization, the intermediate effect, and the moderate experience hypothesis all predict a dip.
7. Use constraints, not self-monitoring, to break habits during performance — self-monitoring causes the yips.
8. Hire eyes, not superiority. A coach worse than you at the skill can still see what you cannot.
9. Prefer renovation to demolition. Change one plank at a time and look for the ridge between peaks.
