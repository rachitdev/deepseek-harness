# Chapter 7 — Variability Over Repetition

*Part II — Do: Learning from Practice. Maxim 7 of 12.*

> "I used to think, 'How could jazz musicians pick notes out of thin air?' I had no idea of the knowledge it took. It was like magic to me at the time." — Calvin Hill, jazz bassist

Chapter's opening questions: How can we learn to improvise? How does varied practice lead to flexible thinking? When is varied practice more helpful than repetition?

## Core Idea

Flexible skill comes from **variability in practice**, not from repeating one thing until smooth. Three kinds of variability matter: mixing what you practise within a session (**contextual interference**), seeing a full range of examples and near-miss non-examples (**interleaving / concept formation**), and holding **multiple representations** of the same material. Crucially the maxim is *Variability **Over** Repetition*, not *instead of* — variation is built on top of fluent, repetitively-acquired components.

## Frameworks Introduced

### Contextual interference (William Battig, 1965)
The "somewhat paradoxical principle": training conditions that produce **greater interference between items — and thus worse performance during practice — often accelerate learning of new tasks**. Interference had been viewed as the enemy of memory; Battig showed it could be a tool.

**Mechanism:** blocked practice makes acquiring an individual pattern easy but leaves the *decision* of which action to take undeveloped, because practice is predictable. Randomized schedules train **control processes** — choosing what to do. Supporting evidence: interference benefits are larger when practice requires choosing between different movements (playing one note instead of another) than when executing the same movement at varying intensities (same notes at different volumes). This is exactly the hard part of improvisation — deciding which note comes next.

### Concept formation through juxtaposition
Learning a concept means learning both what examples share and what separates them from near-misses. A child learning "red" from fire engines alone cannot judge a tomato or a rose. The design rule the chapter extracts from Direct Instruction (Engelmann and Becker):
> **Juxtapose maximally different examples of the same concept with minimally different examples of different concepts.**

Teaching the letter *d*: show it in many typefaces (d, *d*, **d**, ...), then mix in *a* (similar shape, different sound and name), *t* (similar-sounding name, different shape), and *p* (similar in both name-sound and shape). The book's demonstration that this is non-trivial: if you cannot read Chinese, try discriminating two highly similar characters across several fonts.

### Multiple representations
"The more ways you have of thinking about music, the more things you have to play in your solos." — Barry Harris. Chords, scales and intervals all represent pitch relationships but divide the possibilities differently. Berliner: "For learners, the discovery of scales and their theoretical relationship to chords constitutes a major conceptual breakthrough with immediate application." Greg Langdon's "revelation": *G harmonic minor scale up from the fifth degree* has the same pattern as *E-flat major arpeggio with half steps added below each degree*.

**Feynman's version** (quoted at length in the chapter): two theories A and B that look psychologically completely different but yield identical computed consequences are, scientifically, indistinguishable — "However, for psychological reasons... these two things are very far from equivalent, because one gives a man very different ideas from another... any theoretical physicist that's any good knows six or seven different theoretical representations for exactly the same physics."

**Simon and Newell's demonstration — number scrabble.** Tiles 1–9 face up; players alternately take a tile; first to hold three tiles summing to 15 wins (e.g. 2 + 7 + 6). The game is **structurally identical to tic-tac-toe**: label a 3×3 grid with 1–9 (a magic square) and taking a tile equals placing a mark; you hold three summing to 15 iff you have made a row, column, or diagonal. "Just because two representations are formally equivalent does not mean they are psychologically equivalent, and what might be obvious in one format may require intensive thinking to deduce in another."

Kurt Lewin: "There is nothing so practical as a good theory." Theories are tools; more tools better fitted to reality solve more problems.

## Key Concepts

- **Woodshedding** — retreating into the figurative woodshed for repetitive solitary practice. Standard among skilled jazz performers, including Charlie Parker, who reportedly retreated into lengthy practice after being outmatched at an early Minton's appearance.
- **Faithful imitation as the starting point.** Berliner's study found hours spent playing along to recordings, not improvisation, was the typical beginning. Bassist George Duvivier developed an unusual fingering technique to master a recorded solo, repeating it endlessly — only on hearing the band live did he discover what he thought was one solo was **two musicians playing on the same instrument**.
- **Aural vs. visual representation as a handicap when unbalanced.** Trombonist Melba Liston "knew what notes didn't fit the chords and didn't sound good," so she "tiptoed around" them — an ear-only strategy that broke down on complex tunes. Saxophonist Gary Bartz struggled to improvise on "You Stepped on a Dream" until trombonist Grachan Moncur taught him harmonic theory. Conversely, Berliner documents a classically trained student whose exclusive dependence on written music had undermined his aural skills, so his retention of material learned from recordings lagged badly; closing the gap took years inside the jazz community.
- **Perceptual discrimination training.** Tommy Turrentine's teacher sustained a B on the piano, told him to remember it, then struck iron poles on the walk home and quizzed him on the note against the remembered key. Howard Levy: transcribing from recordings, mistakes and all, builds discrimination. A novice: "In the beginning I couldn't hear the chords... Today, I might not be able to identify all the chords in the tunes I hear, but I can hear it when they do change, and that's a big step from where I started."
- **Progressive fineness of categories.** Carmen Lundy could at first only recognize "jazz licks"; with experience, "bebop licks," and eventually "Charlie Parker licks and Sonny Rollins licks."
- **Recognizing parallel cases.** Chuck Israels: an "essential ingredient in learning to be a musician is the ability to recognize a parallel case when you're confronted with one." He and peers played games identifying a song from its chord progression alone. Transposition exercises add variety *and* reveal hidden links — Berliner: "Discovering that certain, apparently different phrases gleaned from disparate solos share a similar basis is a familiar revelation for students."

## Mental Models

- **Improvisation as language.** Infinite expressive capability under strict vocabulary and syntax. James Moody on notes in the wrong context: "a scream in the middle of a peaceful street scene." Wynton Marsalis: "Jazz is not just, 'Well, man, this is what I feel like playing.' It's a very structured thing that comes down from a tradition and requires a lot of thought and study."
- **Variability is a desirable difficulty** (chapter 5) — it makes practice performance worse and learning better, which is why it stays underused.
- **Ramp variability, don't start at maximum.**

## Anti-patterns

- **Blocked homework and unit-segregated exams.** "Most classroom homework assignments minimize contextual interference by carefully segregating questions taught in each unit. Some exams even feature an order of questions that mirrors their order taught within the curriculum, furthering the distortion."
- **Trusting your judgment about which schedule works.** Kornell and Bjork: participants believed they learned better from blocked than interleaved presentation **even after completing a final task on which they performed better following interleaving**.
- **Adding variability before basic competence exists.** Wulf and Shea: complex motor skills and low-prior-experience performers often benefit from *blocked* practice. Cognitive-load reading: variability adds load, so if the task is already too hard under ideal conditions, extra load hurts.
- **Interleaving unrelated domains.** Mixing math with history homework yields no interference benefit (though spacing benefits may still apply).
- **Over-abstracting.** Chess strategy and business strategy share the word but not the specific knowledge.
- **Refusing theory.** Miles Davis, a Juilliard alumnus, rebuked musicians who avoided music theory: "I would go to the library and borrow scores by all those great composers, like Stravinsky, Alban Berg, Prokofiev. I wanted to see what was going on in all of music. Knowledge is freedom and ignorance is slavery, and I just couldn't believe someone could be that close to freedom and not take advantage of it."

## Reference Tables

### The four strategies
| # | Strategy | How to run it |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Shuffle your studying** | Randomize within a session. Test flashcards across topics; shuffle practice problems so you can't tell the source unit; mix backhand and forehand. **Card-deck protocol:** list the exercises you want to practise (Spanish conjugations, guitar chords, programming functions, badminton serves, physics problems), assign each a playing card, shuffle, draw, practise one at a time. Only requirement: each item must be short enough to do several per session |
| 2 | **Play with more performers** | Rotate collaborators — jazz players routinely improvised with band members met that evening. Professionally, seek high-variability posts early. Gary Klein: "In research on firefighters, we observed that **ten years with a rural volunteer fire department were not as valuable for skill development as a year or two in a decaying inner city**. Urban firefighters are exposed to a wider variety of fires and a vastly higher incidence rate." Accountants and lawyers cut their teeth at big firms with diverse clients; doctors rotate through multiple emergency rooms before family practice |
| 3 | **Learn the theories** | Theories create multiple representations and are hard to derive from experimentation; good ones are rare, so read books. Treat it as an investment: it makes other knowledge easier to acquire rather than producing results directly. Practical theories, professional rules of thumb, and industry standards count — ask people in the field what tools and theories they use |
| 4 | **Get it right, then vary your practice** | Heuristic (the book states no science exists for the exact line): if you **cannot perform the skill correctly in isolation**, more variability will not help; once you can perform it correctly **most of the time**, variability slows learning but builds control processes. Jazz soloists repeat a solo against the recording until error-free, and only then add embellishments, interpretations, and new sections |

### The variability experiments
| Study | Design | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **Shea & Morgan** (motor) | Knock down a sequence of wooden barriers with a tennis ball on a red, blue or white light cue. **Blocked** (one sequence at a time) vs. **random** (all three interleaved) | Blocked group faster **during training**. On two novel sequences (black and green) the **random group was faster**, and the advantage held on a **10-day delayed test** |
| **Van Merriënboer, de Croock & Jelsma** (troubleshooting) | Engineering students diagnosing a simulated chemical plant; 4 malfunction types × 12 problems = **48 problems**, blocked vs. random | Random group did worse during practice; **no difference** on a later test of studied malfunctions; **random group better on a new malfunction** |
| **Hatala et al.** (medical) | Students learning ECG readings, disease patterns mixed vs. one disease at a time | Mixed presentation better. Similar results for organic-chemistry molecule categories, painting styles by artist, and bird and butterfly species |
| **Spanish conjugations** | Multiple conjugations practised together | Better retention **when practised over multiple sessions** (not when the conjugations were first introduced) |
| **Japanese learners of English grammar** | Varied vs. consistent practice sequences | Varied better on follow-up tests |
| **English listening practice** | Multiple speakers vs. single speaker | **More experienced** learners benefited from multiple speakers; **lower-experience** learners from a single speaker |
| **Mathematics practice variation** | Minimally vs. substantially different questions | Less prior knowledge → minimally different questions better; more prior knowledge → substantially varied questions better |
| **Kornell & Bjork** (metacognition) | Belief vs. performance after blocked and interleaved study | Learners believed blocking worked better; tests said otherwise, even after they had experienced the interleaving advantage |

### When variability helps — decision rules
| Condition | Prescription |
|---|---|
| Cannot yet perform the skill correctly once | Repetition / blocked practice |
| Can perform it correctly most of the time | Introduce variability |
| Final skill demands variation (jazz improvisation, emergency medicine, three Romance languages) | High variability pays |
| Final skill is fixed (classical pianist playing Beethoven's Ninth; living only in France) | Repetition is fine — variable practice is slower and buys nothing |
| Items are confusable with each other | Interleave them |
| Items are from unrelated domains | Interleaving buys nothing (spacing may still) |

## Worked Example — Bebop at Minton's Playhouse as a variability engine

**The setting.** Early 1940s, second floor of Minton's Playhouse in Harlem, on the customary Monday night off for working musicians. Thelonious Monk, Charlie Christian, Dizzy Gillespie, Charlie "Bird" Parker. **Bebop** was a reaction against swing: swing was big-band, prearranged, danceable; bebop used few instruments and emphasized improvised solos over complex chord changes and rhythms. Free of paid-performance constraints, players escalated each other's technique.

**The feedback loop.** Miles Davis: "You brought your horn and hoped that Bird or Dizzy would invite you to play with them up on stage. And when this happened, you'd better not blow it... People would watch for clues from Bird and Dizzy, and if they smiled when you finished playing, then that meant your playing was good." The variability was enforced socially — Charles Mingus reduced a saxophonist to tears by talking over his solo: "Play something different, man; play something different. This is jazz, man. **You played that last night and the night before.**"

**Evidence the skill was genuinely improvisational.** As early as **1944**, Charlie Parker improvised multiple distinct solos across different takes of the same song while recording for Tiny Grimes's quintet. Miles Davis, mid-solo, had a trumpet valve stick and simply continued, treating the now-unreachable notes as a new constraint. Berliner: "listeners might well imagine that jazz was thoroughly composed and rehearsed before its presentation. Yet artists commonly perform without musical scores and without a specialized conductor."

**Evidence it was disciplined, not free.** Davis chewed out a young Lonnie Hillyer for not knowing his chords after he clashed with the band during a solo. One student's account of a teacher's exercise: "My teacher would have you improvise over a song, and when you tried, he'd sit at another piano and **play your improvised lines a fraction of a second behind you**. It could drive you nuts."

**The cultural precondition.** Berliner's anecdote from a gospel singer: a classically trained pianist filling in for a church choir asked the director for her "music"; told they used no sheet music and that she should improvise her part in relation to the choir, she apologized that "[without] music" she could not accompany them. "The choir members were equally astonished by the pianist's remark, never having met a musician who was dependent upon written music."

**The deliberate variability drills the chapter records:**
| Musician | Drill |
|---|---|
| Barry Harris (teaching scales) | Not "straight up and down the octave" but improvising with scales — inversions, all intervals, chord combinations. A student: "I never get tired of playing with them" |
| Jimmy Cheatham (workshops) | "Exhaust all the possibilities" — work through every available permutation |
| Henry "Red" Allen | Learned to play in **all keys by playing along to records set to different speeds** — each speed shifting the pitch, giving variety from the same source material |
| Tommy Turrentine's teacher | Remember a sustained B; identify notes rung from iron poles on the walk home against it |
| Chuck Israels and peers | Identify a song from its chord progression alone; transposition exercises |

**The synthesis:** every element of bebop training is one of the three variabilities — mixed practice within a session, contrasted examples that sharpen categories, and multiple representations (ear, eye, chords, scales, intervals) — sitting on a base of woodshedded, note-perfect repetition.

## Key Takeaways

1. Blocked practice looks better and teaches less; random practice looks worse and transfers more (Shea & Morgan; van Merriënboer).
2. Contextual interference builds the *control process* — deciding what to do — which is the hard part of any adaptive skill.
3. Interleave confusable concepts; juxtapose maximally different examples of one concept with minimally different examples of neighbouring concepts.
4. Collect multiple representations of the same material; formal equivalence is not psychological equivalence (number scrabble vs. tic-tac-toe).
5. Your intuition about practice schedules is wrong, and stays wrong even after you experience the benefit.
6. Vary only after basic correctness; low-experience learners and very hard motor skills need blocking first.
7. Match variability to the variability the real task demands.
8. Seek high-variability environments and collaborators early in a career.
