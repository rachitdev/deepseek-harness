# Chapter 3 — Success Is the Best Teacher

*Part I — See: Learning from Others. Maxim 3 of 12.*

> "It is the duty of a student to get everything out of a teacher, and the duty of a teacher to get everything out of a student." — Kevin Kelly, founding executive editor of *Wired*

Chapter's opening questions: Does early success fuel future motivation? Are there skills that can bootstrap intelligence? Does a failure to learn stem from a lack of talent, or missing fundamentals?

## Core Idea

Sophisticated skills rest on fluent, overlearned foundations. When a building block is missing, learning is slow, frustrating, and eventually abandoned — and the learner misattributes the failure to talent ("I'm not a math person"). **Success, not failure, is the best motivator**: Bandura's self-efficacy research shows that direct experiences of mastery are the strongest driver of motivation, and repeated early failure produces learned helplessness, not grit. Design learning so that early wins are near-guaranteed, then raise difficulty.

## Frameworks Introduced

### Bootstrapping skills
A foundational skill that unlocks disproportionate downstream learning. Reading is the archetype; the manual alphabet was Helen Keller's.

### Bloom's two-sigma problem
One-on-one tutoring raises achievement by roughly **two standard deviations** over conventional instruction. Bloom's argument: since that gain is demonstrably achievable, deficits cannot be blamed on student ability or the subject's intrinsic nature. The *problem* is finding classroom-feasible methods that approach it.

### Mastery learning
Cycles of **teach → practice → test → corrective feedback**, with these inversions of classroom convention:
| Convention | Mastery learning's replacement |
|---|---|
| Tests produce grades | Tests are diagnostic only, never punitive; used to identify who needs more support |
| Grade on a curve | Assume **95% of students can master the material**; define what everyone should be able to do and expect all to succeed |
| Early scores count toward the final grade | Early stumbles are corrected, not punished (otherwise a student who fails early needs a perfect final just to scrape a C+ — absurd if the final is truly comprehensive) |
| Reteach the same way | **Never teach the material the same way twice** — new examples, explanations, practice activities, so a dead-end route has an alternative |
| Students compete | Cooperation is rational; one student's gain is not another's loss |
Those who pass first time get enrichment activities. Extra time is front-loaded and largely recouped later.

### Direct Instruction
Distinct from mastery learning (which is agnostic about teaching method). DI systematically decomposes complex skills into components and empirically tests the instructional sequence, producing **highly scripted lessons that rapidly alternate examples → teacher-led practice → feedback**. **DISTAR**, the DI reading program (systematic phonics), was the most successful program studied for reading achievement in a large-scale experiment.

### Bandura's self-efficacy
Motivation = **outcome expectations** ("will this action produce the result?") × **self-efficacy expectations** ("can I perform the action?"). Four sources, ranked:
| Rank | Source | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Major | **Personal mastery** (direct experience of success) | Strongest — cannot be discounted the way vicarious success can |
| Major | **Vicarious experience** (seeing someone like you succeed) | Why role models you can see yourself in matter |
| Minor | **Verbal persuasion** | Encouragement, the cheering crowd |
| Minor | **Bodily/physiological state** | Racing heart, shaking hands read as inability |

Self-efficacy is **task-granular** — distinct from self-concept (global self-view) and self-esteem (self-worth). You can be a confident programmer who wilts at giving a stage presentation.

## Key Concepts

- **Why reading is hard**: (1) memorize ~52 arbitrary symbols (a few hundred counting numerals, symbols, fonts); (2) develop **phonological awareness** — consciously segmenting speech into manipulable sounds (say *stink* without the *t*; find the odd one among *tub, rug, mud, bean*) — one of the strongest predictors of early reading, and not spontaneous, because word and phoneme boundaries are psychological, not acoustic; (3) map symbols to those sounds (hard in regular-spelling Spanish or Italian, excruciating in English's thousands of inconsistent sound-spelling patterns); (4) **overlearn** it all to automaticity so cognitive load frees up for interpretation.
- **Systematic phonics wins.** Marilyn Adams's comprehensive review: "By an overwhelming margin, the programs that included systematic phonics resulted in significantly better word recognition, better spelling, better vocabulary, and better reading comprehension." Methods that have children guess from word shape, illustrations, or story context fare worse.
- **Pseudoword tell.** Strong readers pronounce nonwords conforming to English spelling (*bluck*, *squimper*) far better — not by laborious spelling-out but from fluent stored spelling-to-sound associations.
- **Expert readers still decode.** Eye-tracking shows fluent readers fixate nearly every word and resolve identity via letter patterns; the process has simply become unconscious.
- **Confidence follows competence**, never the reverse. "Fake it till you make it" and phony praise target the two *weak* moderators.

## Mental Models

- **The virtuous cycle**: early mastery → less effort → more voluntary practice → more mastery → self-concept as "a reader / a math person" → still more practice.
- **The vicious cycle**: missing prerequisite → can't follow lectures → homework frustrating → no real-world application → negative self-concept calcifies.
- **Kindling a fire**: a spark needs shelter from wind and easily-catching material; a heavy log that would smother a spark is good fuel for a roaring fire. Raise challenge only after motivation is burning.
- **Failure is a luxury good.** "Failure, when it is helpful, tends to be built on top of a history of previous successes. It's only when we believe we can eventually succeed within a pursuit that persisting through failure even makes sense."
- Bandura's own formulation: "Successes raise mastery expectations; repeated failures lower them, **especially if mishaps occur early in the course of events**... Once established, efficacy expectancies tend to generalize to related situations."

## Anti-patterns

- **Grading on a curve** — fine as a sorting mechanism, terrible if the aim is that everyone learn something useful; it makes helping a struggling peer irrational.
- **Counting early quizzes toward the final grade** — mathematically extinguishes hope for early strugglers.
- **Reteaching the failed explanation verbatim.**
- **Diagnosing "no talent" before auditing prerequisites.** "Before declaring that you're unfit to learn a particular subject, you should always ask yourself if you've actually mastered the prerequisite material that's assumed before you start."
- **Whole-word / guessing approaches to reading.**
- **Treating tutoring as remedial only.** Strong students scoff at tutors; intellectual greats did not — Robert Boyle was extensively tutored by George Starkey in the chemical arts; Terence Tao received tutelage from Paul Erdős. Whole disciplines trace teacher–student lineages back to their founders.
- **Confidence-building by affirmation** rather than by track record.

## Reference Tables

### The three lessons
| # | Lesson | Practice |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Begin with the basics** | Audit prerequisites; not just *having* the knowledge but fluent, overlearned skill (algebra is brutal without automatic times tables; literary analysis impossible if decoding consumes bandwidth) |
| 2 | **Hire help** | Tutors, coaches, mentors — especially watching your actual practice. A small coaching-to-practice ratio still pays, since a little instruction supports a lot of practice |
| 3 | **Confidence comes from competence** | Build a track record on easy tasks with plenty of help; graded readers using known vocabulary; master equations before word problems |

### Effect sizes cited
| Intervention | Effect |
|---|---|
| One-on-one tutoring (Bloom) | ~2 standard deviations (two sigma) |
| Mastery learning (meta-analyses) | 0.5–1.0 standard deviations; works in elementary, high school, and college; **largest effects among lower-ability students** |
| Direct Instruction | "May have even stronger effects than mastery learning"; DISTAR most successful reading program in a large-scale experiment |

## Worked Example — Two case histories, one mechanism

**(a) Helen Keller and the manual alphabet.**
- At 19 months, illness (deemed "brain fever," most likely scarlet fever or cerebral meningitis) leaves her deaf and blind. Communication reduced to a few dozen improvised hand signs; by age six she is having tantrums **almost hourly**. "Sometimes I stood between two persons and touched their lips... This made me so angry I kicked and screamed until I was exhausted."
- Her mother, having read of the educated deafblind woman Laura Bridgman, contacts Michael Anagnos at the Perkins Institute for the Blind, who recommends Anne Sullivan.
- **The instructional sequence Sullivan used is the chapter's point.** Give the doll → let her hold it → spell `d-o-l-l` into her palm → she imitates → **immediate success**: "When I finally succeeded in making the letters correctly I was flushed with childish pleasure and pride. Running downstairs to my mother I held up my hand and made the letters for doll." Weeks of objects-with-spellings follow, still as a game. Then the mug/water confusion, the well house, water flowing over her hand: "I knew then that 'w-a-t-e-r' meant the wonderful cool something that was flowing over my hand. That living word awakened my soul... I left the well-house eager to learn. Everything had a name, and each name gave birth to a new thought."
- **Result of the bootstrap**: Radcliffe College of Harvard; twelve books; Latin, French and German; activism for women's suffrage, pacifism, socialism, disability rights. Sullivan even accompanied her to college, transcribing lectures — the tutoring relationship extended for decades. Keller's later successors went further still: Leonard Dowdy married and worked thirty years; Haben Girma became the first deafblind woman to graduate Harvard Law School.

**(b) Juel and Roper-Schneider — the reader-series experiment.** All children received phonics instruction, then were split by *practice material*:
| Group | Practice books | Outcome after one year |
|---|---|---|
| Phonics-oriented | Texts stressing words decodable by the rules being taught | Better recognition of **new** words; also better at sounding out **pseudowords whose spelling-sound correspondences were never explicitly taught** |
| Frequency-oriented | Texts stressing frequently used words | Worse on both |
The phonics-practice children were **extending** their lessons to untaught patterns — the virtuous cycle in miniature: success → fluency → generalization.

**(c) Ritchie et al. — reading bootstraps intelligence.** Identical twins measured for reading ability and IQ at ages 7, 9, 10, 12, 16. Early on, reading and intelligence were not highly correlated. But **when one twin of a pair had stronger reading ability at an earlier time point, that predicted higher intelligence scores at a later time point** — and the gains were not confined to verbal IQ. Twin design controls genetics, supporting a causal reading → intelligence bootstrap.

## Key Takeaways

1. Diagnose learning failure as a **missing prerequisite** before diagnosing it as a missing talent.
2. Foundations must be *fluent*, not merely known — overlearn to automaticity so working memory is free for the real task.
3. Engineer early wins. Self-efficacy comes from personal mastery first, vicarious success second; persuasion and pep talks are weak.
4. Repeated early failure produces learned helplessness and avoidance; failure is only productive on top of a record of success.
5. Use mastery learning's inversions: non-graded diagnostic tests, no curve, correction before progression, never reteach identically.
6. Systematic phonics for reading; more generally, teach the arbitrary building blocks explicitly rather than hoping for discovery.
7. Buy tutoring where you can afford it, at high-leverage moments, and drop the stigma.
