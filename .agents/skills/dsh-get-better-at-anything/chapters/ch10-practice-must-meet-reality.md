# Chapter 10 — Practice Must Meet Reality

*Part III — Feedback: Learning from Experience. Maxim 10 of 12.*

> "We learn how to behave as lawyers, soldiers, merchants or what not by being them. Life, not the parson, teaches conduct." — Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr.

Chapter's opening questions: How important is realistic practice? Why do classroom skills often fail to translate to real-life proficiency? How can you gain access to the situations where a skill is actually used?

## Core Idea

Skills are **dynamic** — a running dialogue between the performer, the physical environment, and the surrounding social world. Practice that severs that dialogue produces certified people who cannot perform (British pilots sent to the front never having flown their aircraft) and, at the far end, people with world-class technical proficiency who still fail because the *social* half of the loop was never trained (Tenerife). Simulation and classroom work are genuinely valuable — most of all at the start — but their value decelerates, and no simulator can reproduce a workplace culture. Getting good therefore has two components most learners only plan for one of: **acquiring the skill**, and **getting access to the situation where the skill is really used**.

## Frameworks Introduced

### The Gosport system (Robert Smith-Barry, 1916–17)
The four reforms that created modern pilot training:
1. **Dual controls** — pupils steer, with backup controls for the instructor in emergencies.
2. **Deliberate adversity** — instead of smooth flying in ideal conditions, instructors deliberately put the plane into spins or dives and made pupils recover.
3. **The Gosport tube** — a hose from a funnel strapped over the instructor's mouth to the student's headphones, enabling in-flight instruction over the aircraft's deafening noise. *Feedback delivered inside the performance, not after it.*
4. **Pupil-first curriculum** — classroom theory cut back in favor of flying time; the student should "always be in the pilot's seat."

### The simulator curve (Stanley Roscoe)
Benefit of simulator time is a **decelerating curve**, not a constant.

| Stage | Simulator vs. equal time in the real aircraft |
|---|---|
| First hours | **Better** than the aircraft — simplification aids understanding |
| Middle | Still helpful, but **less** effective than aircraft time; still cost-effective at 5–20% of the cost |
| Late | **Unhelpful**, and in some studies actively harmful — students learn to lean on features of the simulator absent from the plane |

Roscoe, 1971: *"the first hour of instruction in a ground trainer can save more than one hour in pre-solo flight training. The fifteenth hour in a ground trainer surely would not."*

### Functional vs. physical fidelity
The Moroneys (William and Brian): *"Despite the emphasis on high fidelity and 'realism,' simulators are not realistic. In a sense, the lack of realism may contribute to their effectiveness."* And: *"Precise replication of the control, display, and environmental dynamics is based on the unsupported belief that higher fidelity simulation results in greater transfer of training from the simulator to the actual aircraft."*
- What matters is **functional correspondence**: the information you use to decide, and the actions you take, must be analogous to the real thing.
- What does *not* matter much: fancy graphics and experiential match. New pilots want the latter; the research favors the former.

### Situated cognition
Ulric Neisser — who launched the cognitive revolution with *Cognitive Psychology* — attacked its detachment in his follow-up, *Cognition and Reality*: laboratory studies of problem solving and list learning may be missing the essentials of real thinking. Situated cognition studies how thinking is **constrained and facilitated by the physical and social world**.

### Legitimate, peripheral participation (Jean Lave & Etienne Wenger)
How newcomers become encultured in a **community of practice**. From Lave's fieldwork with West African tailors: apprentices rarely learned through explicit instruction from a master; gradual introduction to the *real work* of the craft made them, "with remarkably few exceptions, skilled and respected master tailors." Two conditions:

- **Legitimacy** — the community's acceptance of your path to full participation. An uncredentialed research assistant and a doctoral student may do identical lab work; only the latter is on a legitimate path to becoming a scientist. No amount of legal knowledge substitutes for a law degree in a court's eyes. Credentials are only the *visible* form: a company that informally promotes only from within is making an implicit claim about which paths are legitimate.
- **Transparency** — being able to observe and understand actual practice. Their grocery-store case: apprentice meat cutters were stationed at the *wrapping station* while experienced butchers worked in a separate part of the facility. One apprentice: *"I'm scared to go in the back room. I feel so out of place there. I haven't gone back there in a long time because I just don't know what to do when I'm there."* Without a window onto practice, apprentices were left with formal instruction emphasizing skills seldom used on the job.

Their conclusion: learning is communal, not purely in-the-head, and **apprenticeship makes more sense as a path to practice than extensive schooling**.

### The three lessons for learning in real life
1. **Study how entry into practice really works.**
2. **Separate skills from signals.**
3. **Learn the lore of the tribe.**

## Key Concepts

- **The outfielder's trick.** Computing a fly ball's trajectory requires differential equations covering gravity, wind, and spin — beyond most people. Yet outfielders catch balls by **running so as to hold a constant angle to the ball**, arriving where it lands without calculus. The would-be physicist can practice the trajectory calculation in a classroom; the outfielder cannot. Expert knowledge often **leans on the world** rather than on an explicit internal theory.
- **Improvisation everywhere.** The mechanic who runs the engine to listen to a strange sound, the entrepreneur who builds a test product before ramping production, the chef who tastes the sauce before adding spice — all are in a constant feedback loop with the environment. You cannot describe what they know without describing where they practice.
- **Social improvisation.** An insurance claims processor consults peers on a tricky case; over time those conversations become a *collective interpretation* of the protocols. A new hire must adapt not only to the standard operating procedures but to senior colleagues' reading of them. Scientists submit to peer judgment before a finding is a "fact"; lawyers negotiate what a "reasonable person" standard means. This embedded knowledge is hard to extract from the context that generated it.
- **Standard operating procedures are negotiated, not installed.** How procedures are actually used "always emerges organically from people practicing the craft."
- **Raw flying skill is no longer the bottleneck.** Mavin and Murray: after decades of engineering and training improvement, *"raw flying skill no longer played a significant part in aviation accidents."* The residual failures are interpersonal.
- **Signaling theory of education.** Much schooling filters applicants rather than building skill. Bryan Caplan's *The Case Against Education* argues the data fit signaling better than **human capital** (school makes you productive) or **ability bias** (smart people go to school and would earn well regardless). Two exhibits: **sheepskin effects** — the return to an extra year of college jumps sharply in the *graduating* year, which human capital cannot explain; and **GED holders** — same academic skills as high school graduates on paper, yet a much weaker wage premium.
- **The mixed case is the normal case.** Most real opportunities blend genuine improvement with credible signaling. The programmer who takes a certification may learn no more than the informal studier, but only she can put it on a résumé. The manager who leads a successful project gains some leadership insight, but **visibility of the success matters more for promotion**. "It's not enough to get good: you need to find a way to show it."
- **Lore as a membership signal.** Bernard Brodie, the father of pharmacology, on filtering neurotransmitter research: *"When the experiments were good, we called it serotonin. When I heard it pronounced serotonin, I knew the experiments were bad and I stayed home."* Pronunciation alone marked insiders.
- **Cultural literacy (E. D. Hirsch).** A major function of schooling is acquiring the lore of educated society. Publications like the *New York Times* or the *Atlantic* presume you know what the Emancipation Proclamation is or why the Magna Carta mattered; lacking that lore, informal discussion is impenetrable. Every specialization then develops its own argot.
- **Two ways in.** For written material: read slowly and look up every word or phrase you're unsure of — painstaking at first, then fluent. In person: be the one who asks the silly definitional question. A short-term embarrassment is the price of understanding the work.

## Mental Models

- **The dialogue.** Piloting is a dialogue between pilot and aircraft *and* between pilot and social environment. Model any skill as a two-channel conversation and ask which channel your practice omits.
- **The back room you're scared to enter.** The transparency problem in one image — you are formally in the community but structurally unable to see the work that matters.
- **The decelerating simulator curve.** Every proxy for reality has a point past which more of it stops helping and starts teaching artifacts. Ask where you are on that curve.
- **Fokker fodder.** Smith-Barry's phrase for undertrained recruits: certified, deployed, and destroyed because certification tracked classroom hours rather than airtime.
- **Skill and signal as two separate accounts.** Every activity deposits into one, the other, or both. Budget deliberately.

## Anti-patterns

- **Rationing the real thing by teaching theory instead.** Extensive classroom prep did ration scarce aircraft effectively; it just didn't produce pilots. The same substitution shows up as residency-slot shortages limiting doctors (not auditorium seats), and as language learners without access to native speakers.
- **Chasing simulator realism.** Fidelity of graphics and motion is the wrong target; functional correspondence is the right one.
- **Training under full real-world complexity from the start.** In a 1990 landing-procedure study, one group trained **with** crosswinds and one **without**. Testing occurred *with* crosswinds — and the group that trained **without** them performed better. The crosswind distortion obscured how the controls influenced the plane's motion.
- **Assuming culture will fix itself.** Sloppy communication norms and a hierarchical cockpit culture caused Tenerife. Improvement came from **greater reliance on formal training**, not from hoping aviation culture would self-correct. Informal communities produce bullying and harassment as readily as mutual support.
- **Romanticizing learning by doing.** Experts often can't articulate their skill and often haven't time to teach; interns are frequently cheap labor rather than apprentices; incumbents erect entry barriers that limit competition and sustain prestige. Kleiner and Vorotnikov estimate professional licensing costs the US economy **$183–197 billion per year**. Shaw, *The Doctor's Dilemma* (1906): *"All professions are conspiracies against the laity."*
- **Optimizing for the wrong criterion inside a legitimate path.** Brennan: *"The grad students who spend the most time 'perfecting their teaching' often fail out of their programs and never get careers as teachers. It may not be fair, but it's how it is."*
- **Working around an obstacle you can't see.** The chapter's blunt formulation of why you must research how a field actually works, even when you dislike the answer.

|---|---|
| Jacobs, Prince, Hays & Salas (1990 meta-analysis) | **Over 90%** of reviewed studies favored simulator-plus-aircraft training over aircraft-only |
| Carretta & Dunlap (1998 review) | Simulators helped, but the benefit **diminished after 25 mock sorties** |
| Rantanen & Talleur (2005) | Same curve: better than equal aircraft time for the first few hours, less effective (still helpful) after, eventually unhelpful |

## Worked Example — Smith-Barry's Gosport reform, with the before/after numbers

The case history the chapter reproduces in full, as a training-design intervention:

1. **The situation (1916).** Germany held the air. Anthony Fokker's Eindecker introduced **synchronization gear**, firing a machine gun through the spinning propeller — earlier wing-mounted guns were wildly inaccurate and propeller armor plating exposed pilots to ricochet. Aerial dogfighting became necessary, and Britain was far behind. **By 1916, over a third of Royal Flying Corps pilots were lost** — among the highest casualty rates in all the British armed forces.
2. **The real cause was training, not technology.** The peacetime curriculum emphasized mechanical operation of the aircraft, its construction, and the theory of flight. Actual flying experience was often absent. Instructors demonstrated the controls on test flights — where no second seat existed, **the student held on to the wing spars during the demonstration**. In theory the pupil then took the pilot's seat; in practice many instructors refused, fearing a crash. **Pilots reached the front lines never having flown the type they were certified to operate.** In April 1916 alone, RFC commander Hugh Trenchard wrote **six letters** to the Air Ministry and War Office about training quality; another officer reported receiving his **third pilot that week** who had "never flown any of the type of aircraft I have in this country."
3. **The critic.** Major Robert Smith-Barry — who survived a 1914 crash that broke both legs and left a permanent limp — wrote in 1916: *"They've only seven hours flying sir—and it's bloody murder. They've barely learned to fly, let alone fight."* He called the recruits **"Fokker fodder."**
4. **The assignment.** Trenchard, impatient: *"Don't worry us anymore with your complaints. If you think you can do better, go and do it"* — and put him in charge of training at Gosport.
5. **The intervention.** Dual controls; deliberate spins and dives to recover from; the Gosport tube for in-flight instruction; theory cut in favor of flying time; students always in the pilot's seat.
6. **The results.**
   - Training-flight crash rate: **~1 in 10 → about 3 percent**, *while increasing the difficulty of the maneuvers taught.*
   - 1918 offensive buildup: **354 percent rise in fleet size** against only a **65 percent rise in casualties** — combat flying got safer even as Britain pushed into German territory.
   - Severity shifted. Historian Robert Morley: before the reforms, the *"majority of crashes were fatal and the result of grievous pilot error"*; afterward accidents *"occurred almost entirely during landings and tended to be nonfatal. In many cases, they were not severe enough to destroy the aircraft."*
7. **The diffusion.** Adopted throughout English-speaking countries after the war. Morley: *"The dual-control aircraft and the pupil-first philosophy remain bedrock principles of both civilian and military pilot training to this day."* Trenchard later called Smith-Barry **"the man who taught the air forces of the world how to fly."**

### The counter-case — Tenerife, March 27, 1977
What realistic *technical* practice cannot fix:
- Fog rolled into Los Rodeos Airport, Tenerife. Traffic had been diverted there after Canarian separatists bombed Gran Canaria Airport.
- KLM captain **Jacob Veldhuyzen van Zanten** — KLM's **chief pilot instructor, with over eleven thousand hours** — was eager to leave before fog dropped visibility below the takeoff minimum, which would strand him, his crew and **235 passengers** overnight.
- He pulled on the throttle; the first officer reminded him they had no takeoff clearance. *"No, I know that,"* he replied. *"Go ahead, ask."*
- The first officer radioed that they were **"ready for takeoff"** — nonstandard phrasing. The tower replied **"OK… stand by for takeoff, I will call you."** At that exact moment Pan Am 1736, on the same frequency, transmitted **"No… uh, we're still taxiing down the runway."** The KLM cockpit heard only **"OK"** followed by a squelch of interference.
- Believing himself authorized, Veldhuyzen van Zanten accelerated. He saw the taxiing Boeing 747 too late to stop, pulled up hard, scraped his tail along the tarmac — and collided. **583 people died**, still the worst aviation accident in history.
- Aftermath: KLM executives initially suggested Veldhuyzen van Zanten *conduct the investigation*, not realizing his flight had caused it.
- Assigned causes: nonstandard and ambiguous phraseology on both sides; Pan Am's failure to clear the runway at the indicated exit; above all the captain's decision to take off without confirming clearance. Though collegial with his crew, **his authority within the company likely made it harder for the copilot to press his doubts.**
- The reforms: **standardized communication protocols** that leave no room for misunderstanding, and **interpersonal training encouraging lower-ranking pilots to assert themselves** when they perceive risk.

## Key Takeaways

1. Dynamic skills are learned in contact with their environment. Classroom theory rations scarce practice resources; it does not substitute for them.
2. Simulation is most valuable at the very beginning, and its returns decelerate to zero (or below). Plan a graduation point out of the simulator.
3. Optimize simulators for **functional** correspondence — same information, same decisions, same actions — not visual realism.
4. Simplify early. The crosswind study says removing real-world noise can produce *better* performance under that noise later.
5. Technical mastery does not confer social competence. Tenerife was flown by an eleven-thousand-hour chief instructor.
6. Access is part of the skill problem. Map the legitimate path into your field and the transparency of actual practice before investing years.
7. Getting good and being seen to be good are separate projects. Signals are real economic goods; plan for both.
8. Learn the tribe's lore deliberately — read slowly and look things up, or pay the small social cost of asking.
9. Informal learning communities are not automatically benign; formal training exists partly to correct their pathologies.
