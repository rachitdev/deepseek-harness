# Chapter 4 — Knowledge Becomes Invisible with Experience

*Part I — See: Learning from Others. Maxim 4 of 12.*

> "We know more than we can tell." — Michael Polanyi, philosopher and chemist

Chapter's opening questions: Why are experts not always the best teachers? How does expertise transform explicit thinking into tacit knowledge? Can we make experts' intuitions clearer, so we can learn from them?

## Core Idea

As skill develops, the basis of that skill recedes from conscious awareness. Expertise becomes **recognition** rather than reasoning, so experts produce excellent answers they cannot explain — and they omit as "obvious" the very facts a novice needs. Three barriers therefore block learning from experts: **tacit knowledge**, the **curse of knowledge**, and unwritten **lore**. The remedy is a deliberate extraction discipline: **cognitive task analysis**.

## Frameworks Introduced

### The three barriers to learning from experts
| Barrier | What it is | Example from the chapter |
|---|---|---|
| **Tacit knowledge** (Polanyi) | Things we know but cannot say how we know | Franklin knowing at a glance how to prepare delicate DNA fibres she had never handled |
| **Curse of knowledge** | Communication omits what the speaker believes is redundant; the expert overestimates the listener's knowledge | Franklin could easily explain why the phosphate backbones must be outside — obvious to a physical chemist, not to Watson or Crick, neither of whom was a chemistry expert |
| **Lore** | Knowledge live inside a community but not written down; dominant at a field's frontier | Jerry Donohue telling Watson and Crick their textbook was simply wrong about the enol/keto forms |

### Intuition as recognition (Simon)
Simon: intuition "is nothing more and nothing less than recognition." The expert, holding countless stored patterns, perceives that a position resembles one seen before and merits the same response — avoiding search entirely.

### Recognition-primed decisions (Gary Klein)
From field research with experienced firefighters: under danger and time pressure they do *not* generate multiple options and weigh pros and cons. They match the situation to a typical one from memory and go with the first option automatically, engaging in extensive problem solving **only when something signals the situation is atypical**.

### Intuition as comprehension (Kintsch's Construction–Integration model)
The alternative account, needed because chess middlegames put masters in positions no one has ever seen. Multiple conflicting representations are unconsciously constructed and then stabilize into the single most likely interpretation. "He met the man at the bank" activates both the financial and river senses; "They withdrew two hundred dollars" suppresses the river reading. Reaction-time studies on words like *river* and *money* show both meanings are briefly active, with the inconsistent one suppressed before consciousness. This account explains **adaptive expertise** — coherent answers to novel combinations of constraints. (The book flags in a footnote that coherent ≠ correct — see chapter 9.)

### Cognitive task analysis (CTA)
A family of techniques for extracting knowledge from experts, successor to behavioural task analysis (which catalogued physical movements, e.g. a riveter on an assembly line). CTA "becomes more valuable as the nature of the work becomes more conceptual than physical, when the tasks can't be boiled down to procedures, and when experts clearly outperform novices." One review lists **over sixty techniques**, from structured interviews and concept mapping to detailed computer simulations of expert performance. A thorough analysis can take **hundreds of hours**. Uses: understanding shopper buying behaviour, building curricula that cut training time, engineering professional tools, basic psychological research.

## Key Concepts

- **De Groot (1946), *Het denken van den schaker* (Thought and Choice in Chess)** — the origin of scientific expertise research. Think-aloud comparison of masters and club players found masters do **not** search deeper. Nor do experts of any kind show much enhanced problem solving outside their domain.
- **Chase & Simon (early 1970s)** — replicated and extended de Groot. Masters *intuit* better candidate moves; the initial moves they explore are superior. Mechanism: memory. Shown a naturally occurring board briefly, experts reconstruct it accurately; novices recall a few pieces poorly. **Scramble the board and the expert advantage vanishes.** Experts also place pieces in meaningful chunks with pauses between (a knight-and-two-rooks fork; a queenside-castled king).
- **Universality of the recall effect** — medicine, programming, electronics, athletics, music all show enhanced recall of naturally occurring patterns, with the advantage sharply declining for atypical formats.
- **Klein's chess study** — the first move generated was far better than chance and frequently the best move considered even after long deliberation. Magnus Carlsen: "I usually know what I am going to do after 10 seconds; the rest is double checking... Often I cannot explain a certain move, only know that it feels right, and it seems that my intuition is right more often than not."
- **Poincaré (*Science et méthode*, 1908)** on why experts do not search randomly: "The sterile combinations do not even present themselves to the mind of the inventor... All goes on as if the inventor were an examiner for the second degree who would only have to question the candidates who had passed a previous examination."
- **Apprenticeship at the elite level** — Harriet Zuckerman's survey of American Nobel laureates: **more than half** had worked in an apprentice-like relationship with another laureate, and in **69%** of those cases the master had **not yet won** the prize when the mentorship began — so the effect came from the master's acumen, not institutional prestige.

## Mental Models

- **Expertise as compression, then amnesia.** Formal rules and procedures are gradually replaced by situation recognition; when there is no reasoning left, there is nothing to report.
- **Why popular science books lurch** between aggressive dumbing-down and impenetrable passages: the expert author cannot calibrate what will be obvious.
- **Knowledge lives in networks, not heads.** The information you need is usually held diffusely; the first step is often mapping contacts, not finding "the expert."

## Anti-patterns

- **Asking an expert for advice.** "Ask for advice and you might get a sermon, when what you want to hear is the knowledge they think is too obvious to be worth mentioning." Self-report methods assume respondents can perform self-CTA and report tacit knowledge; "the evidence suggests quite the opposite: people have considerable difficulty reporting on their own cognitive processes."
- **Assuming a world-class expert will be a good introductory teacher.**
- **Trusting textbooks at the frontier** — the enol/keto error nearly sank the DNA model.
- **Treating expert intuition as reliable everywhere** — it degrades badly in uncertain, poor-feedback environments (chapter 9).

## Reference Tables

### The three CTA insights, usable informally
| # | Insight | Method | How to run it |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Ask for stories, not advice** | **Critical Decision Method** | Have the expert recount a specific challenging incident. Act like a journalist: gather facts, establish a timeline, walk decisions step by step, then ask why at each point. Essential when the events (a difficult surgery, a rescue, a rare business call) are too infrequent to observe |
| 2 | **Talk through tough problems** | **PARI** (Precursor, Action, Result, Interpretation) | Experts generate typical problems, then swap them with other experts; the solver thinks aloud, then rehashes afterwards. Two advantages: the problem context cues knowledge that is unretrievable in the abstract, and watching the process is itself one of the best ways to learn |
| 3 | **See where they seek answers** | **Sociogrammetry** | Ask experts who *they* go to for advice on particular topics; map knowledge as a social network |

### Simon's referral protocol (quoted in full in the chapter)
> "On the receipt of an inquiry, I pick up the phone and call the person, among my acquaintances, whose field of expertness is as close as possible to the target (it need not be very close at all). I ask my respondent, not for the answer to the question, but for the name of the person in his or her circle of acquaintance who is closest to being an expert on the topic. I repeat the process until I have the information I want. It will be a rare occasion when more than three or four calls are required."

## Worked Example — Rosalind Franklin, DNA, and invisible expertise

**The technical problem.** X-ray crystallography images are neither reflections nor silhouettes but **diffraction** patterns: X-ray waves scattering off atoms interfere — crest on crest amplifies, crest on trough cancels — so that only spacings matching the crystal's regular atomic arrangement survive onto the film. Reading the image requires both diffraction theory and hard-won practical craft with unruly materials.

**Franklin's preparation.** Trained as a physical chemist at Cambridge; mastered crystallography studying the crystal structure of **coals** — determining why some coals reduce to graphite on heating and others do not. Coal is not a perfect crystal; its impurities and irregularities made it excellent, difficult practice. Recruited to King's College London and assigned DNA.

**The tacit craft the chapter enumerates:**
- Living molecules are wet. DNA has two forms — compact **A** and slightly stretched **B** — and which dominates depends on **sample humidity**. Earlier photographs were smudged because samples flipped form mid-exposure.
- Usable samples required grinding animal organs and purifying extracts, by processes that "weren't well understood." Rudolf Signer's Swiss high-molecular-weight preparation held a near monopoly on usable samples. Maurice Wilkins on their quality: "It's just like snot!"
- Good pictures meant carefully pulling threads of DNA, using a **series of salt solutions** to hold ideal wetness, and exposures of **a hundred hours or more**.
- Result: J. D. Bernal called her images "among the most beautiful x-ray photographs of any substance ever taken."

**Expertise as instant recognition.** Shown Watson and Crick's first model — three phosphate backbones inside, bases out — Franklin immediately saw the flaw: far too little water, and the sodium ions they placed on the outside would be wrapped in water molecules and unable to bind as modelled. Her photographs told her phosphates must be **outside**, bases in the middle. When Linus Pauling entered with a similar three-chain model, she wrote directly to the celebrated chemist to point out his mistake. She and her assistant Raymond Gosling went back and forth for months imaging A and B forms, narrowing the atomic structure.

**Where lore decided the race.** Watson and Crick's route was cleared by three pieces of unwritten or contested knowledge:
1. **Donohue's correction** — textbooks said the nucleic acid bases oscillate between enol and keto forms in roughly equal ratio (imagine Lego bricks whose shape keeps changing), making an interior fit implausible. Donohue, on the basis of only a few published studies, said the textbook was wrong and the keto form was actual. Using keto, the bases clicked together.
2. **Avery's transformation experiment** — injecting harmless bacteria with purified DNA from a pathogenic strain made them toxic, heritably. Not yet consensus; many still bet on proteins as the hereditary molecule. Watson's insistence on DNA rested on his hunch that Avery was sound.
3. **Chargaff's ratios** — adenine ≈ thymine, guanine ≈ cytosine, with the A-T/G-C balance varying by sample. In retrospect a decisive clue to base pairing; at the time squishy — Watson's colleague Roy Markham insisted Chargaff was wrong, probably on the basis of studies where cytosine was absent because some viruses use an alternative chemical.

**The outcome.** Watson and Crick cracked the structure using Franklin's X-ray photographs **without her knowledge or consent**; without them they might not have escaped their three-chain models. Given a few more months with her own data, the structure might have been hers. She died of cancer in 1958; the Nobel went to Watson and Crick in 1962, and is not awarded posthumously.

**What to extract from the case:** every ingredient of Franklin's advantage — humidity control, salt-solution sequences, which sample source to use, what a diffraction pattern implies about water content — is exactly the kind of knowledge that never appears in a paper. It is recoverable only by watching, by asking for the story of a specific difficult imaging session, or by apprenticeship.

## Key Takeaways

1. Skill migrates from explicit rules to recognition; the better someone gets, the less they can tell you.
2. Expert superiority is pattern-specific, not general — scramble the board and it disappears.
3. Recognition-primed decision making is the normal mode of expert action under pressure; deliberation is the exception triggered by atypicality.
4. Three barriers block transfer: tacit knowledge, the curse of knowledge, and lore. Design your questions to defeat each.
5. Ask for **stories about specific incidents**, not advice. Interview like a journalist: facts, timeline, decision-by-decision.
6. Watch experts solve real problems and think aloud (PARI); the context cues knowledge that interviews cannot reach.
7. Map who the experts consult (sociogrammetry); Simon's phone protocol usually reaches the answer in three or four calls.
8. At the frontier, apprenticeship substitutes for literature — half of American Nobel laureates trained under another laureate, usually before that mentor was famous.
