# Patterns — Get Better at Anything

The book's reusable moves, grouped by its three parts, plus the Conclusion's integration patterns. Each is stated as **When / How / Trade-offs**.

---

## Part I — See: Learning from Others

### 1. Borrow the representation before you start searching
**When** — you face a hard problem and have no evidence your framing is the one competent people use (Ch 1).
**How** — find out how someone competent describes the problem space: what are the start and goal states, what counts as a legal move, what are the major moves. Only then begin work.
**Trade-offs** — costs time up front and can feel like procrastination; a borrowed representation can also import the field's blind spots. Wiles spent eighteen months in the library before attacking Fermat's Last Theorem.

### 2. Wait for the problem to ripen
**When** — you are considering a long commitment to an unsolved problem (Ch 1, Ch 8).
**How** — work near people actively pushing the frontier; they give strong hints about which regions are worth entering. Wiles committed only after Frey's 1984 link to Taniyama–Shimura made the problem attackable with his own specialty.
**Trade-offs** — you may wait forever for a signal that never comes; ripeness is judged, not measured. The alternative — goal-free exploration — is the move when there is nobody to learn from.

### 3. Study a worked example instead of solving the problem
**When** — you are a novice, the material has high element interactivity, and the goal is to learn the method rather than to obtain the answer (Ch 2, Ch 5).
**How** — use examples that show *every* intervening step, including the mental ones experts skip. Pair each example with a matched problem.
**Trade-offs** — reverses as expertise grows (expertise-reversal effect). Examples alone produce no retrieval practice: "Seeing can assist with doing, but it can never replace it."

### 4. Strip the goal to suppress means-ends analysis
**When** — a learner is solving problems successfully but not learning the pattern (Ch 2).
**How** — replace "find angle X" with "find as many unknown quantities as you can."
**Trade-offs** — feels aimless, and offers no criterion for completion; unsuitable once the learner needs to practise targeted retrieval.

### 5. Cut extrinsic load
**When** — instructional material is correct but exhausting (Ch 2).
**How** — fix split attention (put labels where their referents are); delete redundancy (don't read slides verbatim, don't duplicate a diagram in prose); pretrain components; ramp complexity.
**Trade-offs** — over-simplification can strip out the desirable difficulties in Ch 5. Sweller: "Redundancy is anything but harmless."

### 6. Copy first, originate later
**When** — entering a creative craft (Ch 2, Ch 7).
**How** — follow the Renaissance sequence: copy masterworks → draw from casts → draw from life; charcoal → grisaille → full colour. In jazz: play along with recordings, transcribe solos note for note, before improvising.
**Trade-offs** — long. Cennini's timetable ran to thirteen years. Risk of stalling in imitation if you never leave; the fix is deliberate escalation, not skipping the phase.

### 7. Engineer early wins
**When** — a learner is stalling, avoiding, or attributing failure to talent (Ch 3, Ch 12).
**How** — check for a missing fluent prerequisite; supply it; design the first tasks to be near-certain successes; then raise difficulty. Personal mastery is the strongest source of self-efficacy, vicarious success the second.
**Trade-offs** — too easy for too long wastes time and violates the difficulty sweet spot. Praise and pep talks target the two weak sources and can backfire (bulk praise diminishes effort).

### 8. Run mastery learning's inversions
**When** — designing a course, curriculum, or onboarding program (Ch 3).
**How** — tests diagnostic, never punitive; no curve, assume ~95% can master it; correct before progressing rather than averaging early stumbles into the final grade; **never reteach the same way twice**; enrichment for those who pass first time.
**Trade-offs** — front-loads time (largely recouped later) and demands more instructional material, since every topic needs at least two distinct explanations.

### 9. Extract knowledge from experts by incident, not by advice
**When** — the person who knows cannot articulate what they know (Ch 4).
**How** — ask for stories about specific incidents, with facts, timeline, and decision-by-decision reasoning. Watch them solve real problems while thinking aloud (PARI). Map who *they* consult; Simon's phone protocol usually reaches the answer in three or four calls.
**Trade-offs** — expensive. A thorough cognitive task analysis can take hundreds of hours. CTA earns its keep only when work is conceptual and experts clearly outperform novices.

### 10. Apprentice at the frontier
**When** — the knowledge you need is lore, not literature (Ch 4, Ch 10).
**How** — get into the room. Zuckerman's survey: more than half of American Nobel laureates had worked in an apprentice-like relationship with another laureate — and in 69% of those cases the master had not yet won when the mentorship began.
**Trade-offs** — access is gated by legitimacy and transparency (Ch 10); apprenticeships are frequently exploited as cheap labour, and picking the master well matters more than picking the institution.

## Part II — Do: Learning from Practice

### 11. Build the practice loop
**When** — always; it is the book's default unit of practice design (Ch 5).
**How** — see an example → attempt a problem → get feedback, cycled. Diagnose stalled learning by asking which of the three is missing.
**Trade-offs** — sequencing within the loop is contested and matters far less than including all three. Omitting feedback is the most common and most costly failure.

### 12. Prefer retrieval to review, spacing to massing
**When** — the goal is durable retention (Ch 5).
**How** — flashcards and self-testing over re-reading; distribute sessions over time.
**Trade-offs** — both feel worse and are rated less effective by students even when they demonstrably learn more. Cramming genuinely does raise short-term performance — it just empties out before use.

### 13. Escalate your own problem difficulty
**When** — a skill has become automatic and progress has stopped (Ch 5, Ch 8).
**How** — set yourself a harder problem, not more repetitions of the current one. Experts thinking aloud look like novices because they are solving harder problems, not the same ones faster.
**Trade-offs** — deliberately reduces fluency and enjoyment; "the mindlessness of expertise seems mostly at play when we are dealing with skills we aren't trying to improve," which is fine for driving and fatal for a craft.

### 14. Fade the scaffolds
**When** — the learner has internalized the patterns (Ch 5, Ch 2).
**How** — fewer examples, harder and less structured problems, more self-assessment. Use copy → complete → create as a graded generation ladder.
**Trade-offs** — fading too early reintroduces the load problem; fading too late produces expertise reversal, where the support actively harms.

### 15. Decompose the ambition into practisable skills
**When** — you have a broad goal ("be more creative," "think strategically," "communicate better") (Ch 6, Conclusion).
**How** — name the specific component skills and the specific situations they run in. "A symphony can only be performed by playing the notes."
**Trade-offs** — loses motivational grandeur, and over-atomizing produces drills that never integrate; pair with whole-task practice.

### 16. Predict transfer from shared production rules
**When** — deciding whether training X will help with Y (Ch 6).
**How** — ask which IF–THEN units, including mental actions, the two tasks share. Anderson's model predicts observed transfer almost perfectly from shared production count.
**Trade-offs** — the model underpredicts slightly (general productions it omits), and abstraction is real but slow — Fong/Krantz/Nisbett showed statistical heuristics do transfer, after many examples.

### 17. Practise the direction you actually need
**When** — the skill has an input side and an output side (Ch 6).
**How** — treat comprehension and production as separate skills. Practising differentiation does not improve integration; understanding a sentence pattern does not build the ability to generate one.
**Trade-offs** — doubles practice volume for two-directional skills. Canadian French-immersion students show what the asymmetry costs when only one side is practised.

### 18. Interleave after basic correctness is established
**When** — the learner can already perform each item roughly right and now needs to choose among them (Ch 7).
**How** — randomize the practice schedule so the *decision* of which action to take is trained, not just the execution.
**Trade-offs** — makes practice performance visibly worse, which learners mistrust even after experiencing the benefit. Low-experience learners and very hard motor skills need blocking first.

### 19. Juxtapose to define the concept
**When** — teaching a category, especially against confusable neighbours (Ch 7).
**How** — Engelmann's rule: juxtapose maximally different examples of the same concept with minimally different examples of *different* concepts. Teaching *d*: many typefaces of *d*, mixed against *a*, *t*, and *p*.
**Trade-offs** — designing good minimal contrasts is hard and domain-specific; badly chosen near-misses teach the wrong boundary.

### 20. Collect multiple representations
**When** — you keep hitting problems your current framing makes hard (Ch 7, Ch 11).
**How** — deliberately acquire alternative framings of the same content — chords, scales, and intervals; number scrabble and tic-tac-toe; Feynman's "six or seven different theoretical representations for exactly the same physics."
**Trade-offs** — each representation costs learning time and formal equivalence guarantees nothing about usefulness. But "just because two representations are formally equivalent does not mean they are psychologically equivalent."

### 21. Optimize for shots, not for the perfect shot
**When** — working in a domain where impact is unpredictable (Ch 8).
**How** — raise output structurally: assembly-line the routine parts, let unripe ideas wait rather than blocking, hedge across projects, and defend the ratio of creative hours. The hit rate is flat, so total attempts determine total hits.
**Trade-offs** — quantity without frontier expertise produces individually creative but socially invisible work; and prolific output has documented personal costs (Edison as absent father; Einstein's stomach problems).

### 22. Bet like an investor
**When** — choosing between a routine and a risky project (Ch 8).
**How** — treat ideas as undervalued positions. Risk appetite, not knowledge, is what separates routine experts from creative ones — and Howes's inventor data suggests it is culturally transmitted, so proximity to risk-takers matters.
**Trade-offs** — most bets lose. Moderate originality wins reception; extreme originality is often incomprehensible.

## Part III — Feed Back: Learning from Experience

### 23. Diagnose the learning environment before trusting experience
**When** — deciding how much weight to give anyone's gut, including your own (Ch 9).
**How** — apply the Klein–Kahneman test: are cues adequately valid, and has there been an opportunity to learn them with fast feedback? Poker passes; political forecasting and clinical judgment fail.
**Trade-offs** — the test is qualitative and easy to self-servingly pass. The tell that you have failed it is confidence uncorrelated with accuracy.

### 24. Replace judgment with a crude model
**When** — prediction depends on summing many individually weak cues (Ch 9).
**How** — count factors for and against, or fit a weighted sum in a spreadsheet. Burgess's unweighted 21-factor tally beat three psychiatrists at predicting parole failure.
**Trade-offs** — models miss genuine broken-leg cues, so use the model's output as a starting point to adjust rather than as an oracle where you truly hold rare, highly informative information.

### 25. Upgrade the feedback the environment supplies
**When** — outcome feedback alone is not moving you (Ch 9, Conclusion).
**How** — keep score and track performance; log decisions so memory cannot revise them; conduct after-action reports on a record of the attempt; score calibration, not just direction. Online poker's hand histories are the model case.
**Trade-offs** — ego-bruising, and logging costs time during performance. But without it, working memory spent performing leaves no capacity for evaluating.

### 26. Decompose the question and anchor on base rates
**When** — forecasting anything (Ch 9).
**How** — break a large judgment into sub-questions to block the bait-and-swap; establish the reference-class frequency before fine adjustment.
**Trade-offs** — slow, and reference classes are sometimes genuinely absent. Superforecasters still cannot predict much a decade out.

### 27. Form a brain trust for constructive disagreement
**When** — you have cognitive blind spots and no external corrective (Ch 9, Ch 12, Conclusion).
**How** — assemble practitioners from different perspectives to dissect each other's work. Groups took the Wason task from 9% to 75%, and some groups reached the right answer no member had proposed.
**Trade-offs** — ideology, groupthink, and domineering members derail debate; the group is only *more likely* than solo thinking to converge on truth.

### 28. Put the learner in the seat, with a safety net
**When** — realistic practice is dangerous, costly, or scarce (Ch 10).
**How** — Smith-Barry's package: dual controls, deliberate adversity to recover from, in-performance communication, theory cut in favour of doing. Crash rate fell from ~1 in 10 to ~3 percent *while* maneuver difficulty rose.
**Trade-offs** — requires instructor capacity and equipment that classroom teaching does not; the resource constraint is real, which is why the classroom substitution keeps happening.

### 29. Simulate early, graduate deliberately
**When** — building any training program with a proxy for reality (Ch 10).
**How** — use simulation heaviest at the start, optimize for functional not visual fidelity, simplify away real-world noise for beginners (the crosswind study), and set an explicit exit point.
**Trade-offs** — past the knee of the curve, more simulator time is at best neutral and at worst teaches artifacts absent from the real task.

### 30. Map the legitimate path and the transparency of practice
**When** — trying to enter a field (Ch 10).
**How** — research how entry actually works, however unwelcome the answer. Brennan on academia: programme rank trumps fit, stipend and location; "you can't get any job your advisor couldn't get." Then check whether you will actually be able to *see* real practice, or be stationed at the wrapping station.
**Trade-offs** — the honest answer is often demoralizing, and optimizing for legitimacy can crowd out genuine skill. "You can't work around an obstacle you can't even see."

### 31. Budget skill and signal separately
**When** — choosing between two development opportunities (Ch 10).
**How** — ask what each deposits into the skill account and into the visibility account. The certificate and the informal study may teach the same thing; only one goes on a résumé.
**Trade-offs** — pure signalling is waste, and pure skill-building can go unrewarded. Most real opportunities are a mixture; the pattern is to notice which, not to pick a side.

### 32. Learn the lore of the tribe
**When** — you can follow the formal content but not the informal conversation (Ch 10, Ch 4).
**How** — with texts, read slowly and look up every unfamiliar word or phrase until you can read fluently. In person, be the one who asks what a term means.
**Trade-offs** — painstaking at first, and socially costly in the moment — a short-term price for understanding the work. Brodie's *serotonin* pronunciation test shows how sharply lore sorts insiders from outsiders.

### 33. Diagnose the procedure before prescribing more practice
**When** — errors are consistent rather than random (Ch 11).
**How** — treat them as buggy algorithms: reconstruct the rule the learner is actually running (e.g. always subtract the smaller digit from the larger). Stop, explain, restart on correct footing.
**Trade-offs** — requires diagnostic skill and interrupts flow. But extra practice on a buggy procedure ingrains it.

### 34. Renovate rather than rebuild
**When** — an established skill or worldview needs to change (Ch 11).
**How** — change one plank at a time (the ship of Theseus); seek the ridge between peaks rather than descending into the valley.
**Trade-offs** — some changes genuinely require a new motor program and cannot be reached by tweaking (hunt-and-peck will never become touch typing). Woods rebuilt and won; Gossett, Beck, Duval, Baker-Finch, and Ballesteros rebuilt and did not recover.

### 35. Break a habit with constraints, not self-monitoring
**When** — old patterns keep reasserting themselves during performance (Ch 11).
**How** — change the task so the old way is impossible: write without adverbs, paint without colour, hit with an undersized racquet face. Constraints push you off a habit without directing attention onto it.
**Trade-offs** — badly chosen constraints train an artifact. Direct instruction on your own mechanics during performance risks the yips.

### 36. Hire eyes
**When** — you cannot simultaneously perform and observe yourself, and "feel is not real" (Ch 11).
**How** — get a coach, tutor, or reviewer. They need not be better at the skill than you — Woods worked with coaches far worse at golf — because their advantage is free observational bandwidth.
**Trade-offs** — feedback carries social and motivational freight; a good feedback environment is one used to assist learning rather than to reward or punish, targeting the task rather than the person, on a base of mutual trust.

### 37. Run exposure as an experiment you don't sabotage
**When** — fear, not ability, is the binding constraint (Ch 12).
**How** — build a fear hierarchy matched precisely to the situational, cognitive and physiological content of the fear; enter it; drop the safety behaviours; refuse reassurance ("under no circumstances should the therapist reassure the patient that everything will be okay"). Vary contexts and refresh periodically, because learned safety is narrower and more fragile than fear.
**Trade-offs** — requires real, acceptable risk; the aim is not to prove humiliation impossible but that it is less likely than feared and more survivable. Too large a first step simply doesn't get taken, especially self-guided.

### 38. Face fears in company
**When** — exposure is available but you keep not doing it (Ch 12).
**How** — join a group built around the feared activity: Toastmasters, a study group, a conversation club. Cohesive small groups control fear; people who lived alone fared worst in the Blitz.
**Trade-offs** — a companion can become a safety behaviour if you learn "I'm safe *with them*"; the group must supply exposure, not shelter from it.

## Integration patterns (Conclusion)

### 39. Loop between drills and the whole task
**When** — designing any ongoing practice regime.
**How** — run part-task drills to iron out tricky spots and reduce load, and whole-task practice so the components integrate and stay meaningful — working back and forth, layered on top of the see/do/feedback loop.
**Trade-offs** — drills compress volume (more layups in practice than in a game) but automaticity in a drill does not guarantee fluency in the full task; whole-task practice is meaningful but load-heavy.

### 40. Ask the three questions
**When** — auditing any pursuit you care about.
**How** — (1) How can I improve the way I learn from others? (2) How can I make my practice more effective? (3) How can I enhance the quality of my feedback? Cowen's version of the same audit: *"What is it you do to train that is comparable to a pianist practicing scales?"*
**Trade-offs** — sharpening the axe takes time away from chopping. The woodcutter spent two and a half of his three hours on the axe.
