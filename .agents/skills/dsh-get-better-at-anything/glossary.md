# Glossary — Get Better at Anything: 12 Maxims for Mastery

Alphabetical. Chapter references point to `chapters/chNN-*.md`.

**ACT-R** — John Anderson's cognitive architecture in which skills are built from production rules and facts live in a separate declarative memory system (Ch 6)
**Actuarial method** — Meehl's term for prediction by a simple formula fed basic data; the rival to clinical judgment (Ch 9)
**Adaptive expertise** — the ability to produce coherent answers to novel combinations of constraints, explained by the construction–integration account of intuition (Ch 4)
**After-action report** — a structured post-performance review; essential to Top Gun pilots learning from simulated sorties, and one of the Conclusion's three feedback enhancers (Ch 9, Conclusion)
**Associative phase** — Fitts & Posner's second phase: the performer tries variations, major errors are eliminated, performance smooths and calibrates across conditions (Ch 11)
**Autonomous phase** — Fitts & Posner's third phase: skill becomes effortless and reflex-like; the original explicit instructions may be forgotten (Ch 11)
**Avoidance** — any behavior that neutralizes a perceived threat; sustains anxiety by blocking disconfirming evidence and by delivering reinforcing relief (Ch 12)
**Base rate** — the overall probability of events of a given type; Tetlock's superforecasters anchor on reference classes before adjusting (Ch 9)
**Behavioral task analysis** — the predecessor of cognitive task analysis, cataloguing physical movements (Ch 4)
**Blind variation with selective retention** — Donald Campbell's (1960) account of creativity by analogy to natural selection (Ch 8)
**Blocked practice** — practising one item or skill repeatedly before moving to the next; better during practice, worse for transfer (Ch 7)
**Bloom's two-sigma problem** — one-on-one tutoring raises achievement roughly two standard deviations over conventional instruction; the challenge is finding classroom-feasible methods approaching it (Ch 3)
**Bootstrapping skill** — a foundational skill that unlocks disproportionate downstream learning; reading is the archetype (Ch 3)
**Brain trust** — a group of practitioners who dissect each other's work; strategy #3 for learning under uncertainty (Ch 9, Conclusion)
**Buggy algorithm** — Brown & VanLehn's term for a consistently applied but incorrect procedure, e.g. always subtracting the smaller digit from the larger (Ch 11)
**Calibration** — whether stated confidence matches observed frequency (do your 99% predictions occur 99% of the time?); in poker, the balance of value bets and bluffs that leaves no exploitable pattern (Ch 9)
**Chunking** — reorganizing many items into fewer meaningful units; Miller's escape from the working-memory limit (Ch 2, Ch 4)
**Clinical method** — Meehl's term for prediction by an expert's subjective impression (Ch 9)
**Cognitive load theory** — John Sweller's theory distinguishing intrinsic from extrinsic mental effort and prescribing the elimination of the latter (Ch 2, Ch 5)
**Cognitive phase** — Fitts & Posner's first phase: conscious, deliberate control while the learner works out the correct technique (Ch 11)
**Cognitive task analysis (CTA)** — a family of 60+ techniques for extracting knowledge from experts; most valuable when work is conceptual rather than physical (Ch 4)
**Community of practice** — the group whose ongoing activity defines and transmits a craft; Lave and Wenger's unit of analysis (Ch 10)
**Conjunction fallacy** — judging a conjunction more probable than one of its conjuncts; the Linda problem (Ch 9)
**Construction–integration model** — Kintsch's account in which multiple conflicting representations are unconsciously built and then stabilize into the most likely interpretation (Ch 4)
**Contextual interference** — Battig's principle that training conditions producing interference (and worse practice performance) often accelerate learning of new tasks (Ch 7)
**Curse of knowledge** — the speaker omits what they believe is obvious, overestimating the listener's knowledge (Ch 4, Ch 10)
**Deliberate practice** — Ericsson's model; the "deliberate" part means shifting back from the autonomous to the cognitive phase, under immediate feedback (Ch 5, Ch 11)
**Desirable difficulties** — Robert and Elizabeth Bjork's term for practice conditions that hurt immediate performance and help long-term retention (Ch 5)
**Desk-drawer syndrome** — filing work away unseen to avoid rejection; ego-saving and stagnation-guaranteeing (Ch 5)
**Direct Instruction (DI)** — Engelmann and Becker's method of decomposing skills into components and empirically testing scripted example→practice→feedback sequences (Ch 3, Ch 7)
**DISTAR** — the Direct Instruction reading program (systematic phonics); the most successful program studied in a large-scale reading experiment (Ch 3)
**Einstellung** — Luchins's term for perseveration of a habitual problem-solving mode; "the mindset which predisposes an organism to one type of motor or conscious act" (Ch 11)
**Equal-odds baseline** — Simonton's finding that the ratio of hits to total works stays roughly constant across a career, so quality is a function of quantity (Ch 8)
**Expertise-reversal effect** — instructional supports that help novices lose value and eventually turn negative as expertise develops (Ch 2, Ch 5)
**Exposure** — encountering a feared stimulus without harm; the active ingredient in anxiety treatment (Ch 12)
**Extinction** — the fading of a learned association when the signal appears without the outcome (Ch 12)
**Extrinsic load** — mental effort not directed at learning; the target for elimination (Ch 2)
**Fear hierarchy** — Wolpe's ordered list from mild discomfort to extreme terror, used to structure exposure (Ch 12)
**"Feel is not real"** — golf-improvement lore capturing systematic misperception of one's own movement (Ch 11)
**Fitts & Posner phases** — cognitive → associative → autonomous, the 1967 model of motor skill acquisition (Ch 11)
**Flooding** — exposure that presents the most-feared situation from the outset and prevents escape (Ch 12)
**Formal discipline** — the refuted doctrine that studying a subject generally strengthens mental faculties (Latin improves memory, geometry improves reasoning) (Ch 6)
**Functional fixedness** — Duncker's term for the difficulty of seeing alternative uses for an object perceived in one role; the candle problem (Ch 11)
**Functional fidelity** — correspondence between the information used and actions taken in a simulation and in the real task; matters more than physical realism (Ch 10)
**Game-theory optimal (GTO)** — poker strategy calibrated so no opponent can exploit a pattern in your play (Ch 9)
**Generate-and-test** — the weak method of trying something and seeing whether it works (Ch 1)
**Goal-free effect** — removing the specific goal ("find as many unknowns as you can") suppresses means-ends analysis and frees capacity for pattern learning (Ch 2)
**Gosport tube** — Smith-Barry's hose from a funnel at the instructor's mouth to the student's headphones, enabling instruction during flight (Ch 10)
**Habituation** — the diminishing impact of a stimulus that naturally evokes a response, with repeated experience (Ch 12)
**Heuristic** — a method with no guarantee of success that works decently often (Ch 1)
**Hill-climbing** — the weak method of making whichever small adjustment most improves a tentative solution; fails when things must get worse first (Ch 1)
**Identical elements** — Thorndike & Woodworth's theory that transfer occurs to the extent skills share overlapping components (Ch 6)
**Impetus theory** — the intuitive folk theory of motion; poor physics but a useful approximation in high-friction environments (Ch 11)
**Implosive therapy** — flooding conducted through vivid therapist-guided fantasy rather than real-life exposure (Ch 12)
**Inert knowledge** — Whitehead's term for ideas and methods that could apply broadly but lie dormant and unused (Ch 6)
**Innovation** — in Edison's distinction, a creative work measured by social impact (contrast: invention) (Ch 8)
**Intermediate effect** — Vimla Patel's finding that intermediate students elaborate far more than either novices or experts (Ch 11)
**Intrinsic load** — the unavoidable mental effort of the learning itself (Ch 2)
**Intuition as recognition** — Simon's claim that intuition "is nothing more and nothing less than recognition" (Ch 4)
**Invention** — in Edison's distinction, a creative work judged on technical merits (contrast: innovation) (Ch 8)
**Investment theory of creativity** — Sternberg & Lubart's model of creators as stock pickers; risk appetite separates routine from creative experts (Ch 8)
**Kind vs. wicked learning environment** — whether cues are valid, feedback fast, and repetitions plentiful (poker) or not (political forecasting) (Ch 9)
**Klein–Kahneman conditions** — intuitive judgment is genuinely skilled only if the environment provides adequately valid cues and the person has had an opportunity to learn them (Ch 9)
**Knowledge-telling strategy** — Scardamalia and Bereiter's term for writing things as they come to mind; why children write more fluently than experts (Ch 5)
**Learned helplessness** — the avoidance and passivity produced by repeated early failure (Ch 3)
**Learning styles** — the poorly supported idea that instruction should match a preferred visual/auditory/kinesthetic modality; a shallow misconception needing unlearning (Ch 11)
**Legitimacy** — the community's acceptance of a given path to full participation in a practice (Ch 10)
**Legitimate, peripheral participation** — Lave and Wenger's theory of how newcomers become encultured in a community of practice (Ch 10)
**Long-term working memory** — Ericsson & Kintsch's retrieval structures, by which experience lets long-term memory extend short-term capacity (Ch 2)
**Lore** — knowledge alive in a community but not written down; dominant at a field's frontier, and a membership signal (Ch 4, Ch 10)
**Mastery learning** — Bloom's cycle of teach → practice → diagnostic test → corrective feedback, assuming ~95% of students can master the material (Ch 3)
**Means-ends analysis** — the weak method of identifying the gap to the goal and reducing it; heavy on working memory, and an obstacle to learning (Ch 1, Ch 2)
**Modal model** — Atkinson & Shiffrin's 1968 architecture: sensory input squeezed through a short-term bottleneck into a long-term store (Ch 2)
**Moderate experience hypothesis** — Siegler's claim that strategy variety (good and bad) peaks at intermediate experience (Ch 11)
**Moneymaker effect** — the post-2003 explosion in online poker after amateur Chris Moneymaker won the WSOP from a $39 satellite (Ch 9)
**Multiple discovery** — the same idea arrived at independently by several people (Darwin/Wallace, Newton/Leibniz, nine claimants to the telescope) (Ch 8)
**Multiple representations** — holding several psychologically distinct but formally equivalent framings of the same material (Ch 7)
**Near miss / remote miss** — Blitz terminology: direct exposure to harm resurges fear; distant exposure without harm diminishes it (Ch 12)
**Negative reinforcement** — strengthening a behavior by removing a potential pain; the mechanism by which relief entrenches avoidance (Ch 12)
**Non-monotonic learning** — performance that gets worse before it gets better as a normal part of acquisition (Ch 11)
**Number scrabble** — Simon and Newell's game, structurally identical to tic-tac-toe, demonstrating that formal equivalence is not psychological equivalence (Ch 7)
**Outcome feedback** — knowing only whether the result was good or bad; insufficient for calibration, and in one experiment made people worse (Ch 9)
**Overlearning** — practising past the point of correctness to automaticity, freeing working memory for higher-level work (Ch 3)
**Overregularization** — the developmental stage where children apply the -ed rule to irregular verbs ("I goed there"), becoming temporarily less grammatical (Ch 11)
**PARI** — an expert-extraction method built on watching practitioners solve real problems while thinking aloud (Ch 4)
**Part-task vs. whole-task practice** — drilling components vs. performing the complete skill; the Conclusion recommends looping between them (Conclusion)
**Phonological awareness** — the ability to consciously segment speech into manipulable sounds; among the strongest predictors of early reading (Ch 3)
**Planning (weak method)** — reformulating in a simpler problem space, solving there, and generalizing back (Ch 1)
**Price's law** — half a field's output comes from roughly the square root of the number of contributors (Ch 8)
**Problem space** — a start state, a goal state, and the legal moves connecting them (Ch 1)
**Production rule** — an abstract IF–THEN unit combining a condition with an action, including mental actions; the currency of transfer (Ch 6)
**Progressive problem solving** — experts continually raising the difficulty of the problems they set themselves, so mastery never feels easy (Ch 5, Ch 8)
**Pseudoword test** — pronouncing nonwords that conform to English spelling (*bluck*, *squimper*); a tell for fluent decoding (Ch 3)
**Reciprocal inhibition** — Wolpe's theory that relaxation and anxiety inhibit each other; the protocol survived, the theory did not (Ch 12)
**Recognition-primed decision** — Klein's model in which experts match a situation to a typical case and act on the first option, deliberating only when the situation is atypical (Ch 4, Ch 9)
**Redundancy effect** — duplicating the same content across two channels worsens learning, as capacity is spent filtering (Ch 2)
**Retrieval practice** — recalling material from memory rather than re-reading it; a desirable difficulty (Ch 5)
**Satisficing** — Simon's strategy of taking the first good-enough option rather than optimizing (Ch 1)
**Self-efficacy** — Bandura's task-specific belief that you can perform an action; sourced from mastery > vicarious experience > persuasion > physiological state (Ch 3)
**Sheepskin effect** — the sharp jump in returns to schooling in the graduating year; evidence for signaling over human capital (Ch 10)
**Signaling theory of education** — schooling largely filters applicants rather than building productive skill (Ch 10)
**Situated cognition** — the study of how thinking is constrained and facilitated by the surrounding physical and social world (Ch 10)
**Spacing** — distributing practice over time rather than massing it; a desirable difficulty (Ch 5)
**Split-attention effect** — extrinsic load created by materials that force shuttling between separated pieces of information (Ch 2)
**Strong method** — an algorithm guaranteed to work, or a domain-specific heuristic that drastically prunes the search (Ch 1)
**Superforecaster** — a Tetlock-identified high-accuracy forecaster; his IARPA team beat controls by 60–78% (Ch 9)
**Systematic desensitization** — Wolpe's 1950s exposure protocol combining a fear hierarchy with relaxation (Ch 12)
**Systematic phonics** — explicit teaching of sound-spelling correspondences; wins "by an overwhelming margin" in Adams's review (Ch 3)
**Tacit knowledge** — Polanyi's term for what we know but cannot say how we know (Ch 4)
**Transfer** — the carry-over of learning from one task to another; scales with shared production rules (Ch 6)
**Transparency** — the ability to observe and understand a community's actual practice; the meat-cutters' back room (Ch 10)
**Two-factor theory** — Mowrer's account of why irrational anxieties persist through avoidance (Ch 12)
**Two-memory view** — learned safety is a new inhibitory memory, not an erasure of fear; hence relapse and the need for varied, refreshed exposure (Ch 12)
**Undecidability** — the proven nonexistence of an algorithm for a class of problems; Hilbert's tenth problem (Ch 1)
**Weak method** — a general fallback used when no strong method is available: generate-and-test, means-ends analysis, planning, hill-climbing (Ch 1)
**Wason four-card task** — a logic problem solved by 9% of individuals and 75% of small discussion groups (Ch 6, Ch 9)
**Woodshedding** — retreating for repetitive solitary practice; standard among skilled jazz performers (Ch 7)
**Worked-example effect** — for novices, studying a fully solved problem beats attempting to solve it (Ch 2, Ch 5)
**"Yips"** — the ruin of a golf swing by excessive attention to one's own movement; the hazard of self-monitoring during performance (Ch 11)
**Zone of learnability** — Kintsch's finding that low-prior-knowledge students learn best from well-organized texts and high-knowledge students from poorly organized ones (Ch 5)
**Zone of proximal development** — Vygotsky's gap between what a learner can do with assistance and alone (Ch 5)
