# Chapter 5 — Interviewing, choosing, and onboarding

## Core Idea

Interviewing is a skill most CTOs are weak at while believing they are good at it. The
governing mantra of the whole chapter: **treat every candidate exactly as you would treat a
potential client** — at the fetch, in the seating, during the exercise, on the exit walk, and
in the rejection. The outcome could be life changing for them and their family, and they are
assessing you as much as you are assessing them.

The second theme is that a decision this consequential must be **data led and comparable**,
which means a **scorecard** with defined criteria, defined score ranges, and shared
understanding among every interviewer — so personal bias comes out of the room and candidates
can be compared on a level playing field.

The third: an accepted offer is not the end. "You may be surprised at the number of times a
candidate goes through the acceptance process and signs the papers, yet never shows up on the
start date."

## Frameworks Introduced

- **The interview funnel** — phone/pre-interview (30 min max) → in-person (or video as last
  resort) → exercise → exit walk.
- **The three interview goals** — Can this candidate do the job I need them to do? Will they fit
  the team dynamic? Can you sell them on why they should be working with you and your team?
- **The scorecard** — 10 to 20 criteria split soft/hard, each rated 1–10 (some formulations 0–5),
  each with a written score-range scale, plus a Notes column.
- **Two rejection camps** — "definitely not" and "definitely maybe."
- **Three-part onboarding** — bringing them up to speed / your expectations / getting to work.

## Key Concepts

- **Who should be primary interviewer.** If the candidate would be a direct report, you are
  primary, with at least one other person from your team or company for a second pair of eyes and
  ears. If they'd report to one of your managers, you should *not* be primary — "if you have given
  the responsibility of managing people to someone, then you should feel comfortable letting them
  choose the people for their team." Take a 15–30 minute slot at the end instead, to meet them and
  do the last sell.
- **Buzzword test.** If you suspect the candidate is name-dropping the buzzwords of the day, ask
  them to explain their answer *without using that buzzword.* "Those who know can; those who cannot
  stumble."
- **Professionalism tells on the phone screen.** Did they find a quiet area, or are they on a
  crowded train or in a supermarket checkout line? "Are they treating you like you would like them
  to treat your clients?"
- **Interview about a specific task** (Lou Adler). Ask about a past project similar to what you
  need — it puts them in their comfort zone and they speak freely about problems encountered and
  the solutions they found. Or outline your actual project and ask how they'd solve it, then
  challenge the answer to test confidence.
- **Test whether they will point out an error.** "You don't want to surround yourself with 'yes'
  people and be the smartest person in the room." The subtle method: invert the value proposition
  of two competing technologies the candidate knows — for example, ask *"Why is JSON harder and
  slower to parse than XML?"* That is about the limit trick questions should go to.
- **Why are they leaving?** Twofold reason: (a) will they speak poorly of a previous or current
  employer — a red flag, because one day that may be you; (b) so you can confirm you can offer an
  environment that keeps them happy and productive.
- **Salary questions.** Candidates are usually well briefed not to ask; if they do, defer to a
  post-interview.
- **The self-start exercise.** Some organizations leave a candidate an hour with a bare machine —
  no tools installed — to produce something trivial. It separates those who forage for themselves
  from those who need a well-defined process. Asking for help is fine: "it shows they know their
  own limits."
- **The exit walk.** Escort them off the property, ideally with someone who was *not* part of the
  interview, who can ask how it went as polite small talk. "The more astute candidates will know
  the interview never really ends until they leave the building."
- **Prevalidated future pool.** The "definitely maybe" group cost next to zero to re-acquire — you
  have already marketed, sorted, and interviewed them. It is not uncommon to go out for one
  position and hire two, because onboarding and training can be done in one swoop (a case you must
  make to the CEO and CFO).

## Mental Models

- **The hotel with no room ready.** Arriving to find no one at the front desk; then no room; then
  a room with no fresh sheets and the previous guest's litter on the floor. "Yet this is how many
  companies welcome new recruits into their team... they knew they were coming many days, or even
  weeks, in advance."
- **The interview as two-way engagement.** It seldom happens in reality because the interviewee
  lacks confidence to even the playing field — "you should not take advantage of this."
- **The killer intense stare.** Video makes candidates look extremely intense because they are
  always looking at the screen. Forgive them for it.
- **Your own interview history as data.** Think back: how did it make you feel? What question
  caught you out? What did you enjoy answering? What was the atmosphere like?

## Anti-patterns

- **Ghosting rejected candidates** — not answering emails or returning calls hoping they realize.
  "Such behavior is cowardly and unacceptable." You have no clue where they end up; they may work
  for one of your clients, or a competitor.
- **Interrogation** — running down a list of prepared questions in a cold, robotic manner instead
  of steering a conversation.
- **Google-style lateral puzzles** — "How many manhole covers are there in a given city?" They
  "don't really serve any purpose but to make you sound far more intelligent than you probably are."
- **Power seating** — a lower seat, too far away, or the desk between you. "That instinctively acts
  as a barrier." Sit at the corner of the desk if you have no meeting table.
- **Showing off.** "This is about them, not you."
- **Over-detailed rejection feedback.** Stay high level and on hard skills; soft skills are
  subjective and hard to argue. Too much detail opens you to challenge you may not recall enough
  to defend.
- **Letting rejection turn into a negotiation.** If they plead they can improve and fix it, do not
  address it — reaffirm they were not strong enough in a field of candidates and thank them again.
- **Day one at a desk alone with a binder of company documents.** "If you are the new recruit, you
  might be getting up and walking out and making a call to your second choice."
- **Access not provisioned before day one** — everything they try fails and they have to keep
  telling you. "This wears down both you and them."
- **Video as first choice.** "This should be used only as a last resort." Even for a remote
  position, pay to bring them to your offices or meet in a public place.
- **Hubris.** "In our industry, good people never have a problem finding work. Do not let hubris
  convince you that you are their only option."

## Reference Tables

### Scorecard criteria — the example split

| Class | Criteria |
|---|---|
| Soft skills | General appearance; general articulation; meeting etiquette/professionalism |
| Hard skills | Software development lifecycle; design patterns; coding knowledge (of a specific language) |

Aim for **10 to 20 items**, rated **1 to 10** (higher is better). Criteria are written as
objective-style points, never as interview questions — they are "the results that you are
looking for from your conversation."

### Table 5.1 — Candidate scorecard (as printed)

| Criteria | Score/10 | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| General appearance | | |
| General articulation | | |
| AWS knowledge | | |

The Notes column holds only a quick few words on *why* you gave that score.

### Four preconditions for the scorecard to work

| # | Precondition |
|---|---|
| 1 | Accurately identifying the criteria that are important for this role |
| 2 | Setting the score range of each criterion |
| 3 | Communicating the scoring to each person interviewing |
| 4 | Filling in the scorecard as soon as possible and not leaving it too long |

### Video interview setup

| Item | Guidance |
|---|---|
| Platform | Google Meet, Microsoft Teams, Zoom, Apple FaceTime, Skype for multiplatform; GoTo Meeting, WebEx, and interview-focused BlueJeans for chat, screen sharing, collaboration and recording |
| Preparation | Give plenty of notice of the technology; offer a quick test with someone on your team; confirm bandwidth for at least a one-hour interview |
| Two-way video | Make sure the candidate can see you and your interviewers. If your video fails, reschedule or move both sides to voice only |
| Pace | Allow more time and speak slower and more clearly — technology in the middle slows communication |
| Candidate tells to read | Did they dress? Did they find a quiet place, or are people walking behind them? Do they stay onscreen? Are they engaged, or doing something else such as searching for the answer? |
| Don't penalize | Pixelated video or non-Hollywood lighting |
| Joint exercises | Shared code review; a shared text editor in place of a whiteboard; pen and paper held to the camera for noncoding roles; Google Docs or VS Code Live Coding for real-time joint editing |

### Onboarding — bring them up to speed (the day-one curriculum)

| Topic |
|---|
| In-depth review of either the service or product that you produce |
| Overview of the major terms and vocabulary you all use and the context in which they are used |
| Who are the top and oldest clients? Which have a particular place in the heart of the company (often the first few clients a company ever had)? |
| Other departments within the company, particularly the ones that interact with yours the most |
| Any specific unwritten policies your company or department has |
| Quick overview of all major meetings they are likely to be part of |

Should take no more than a day; task team members with sections and **put it on their calendars
so no one gets blindsided.** Then show them restrooms, kitchen, coffee; meet them at reception
and give a tour with introductions.

## Worked Example

### The AWS criterion, scaled (as printed)

Scaling the criteria is the tricky part — for each item you must state what you have to discover
to place a candidate in a range. The book's AWS example:

| Score | What it means |
|---|---|
| 0 | No previous experience of AWS |
| 1–3 | Used only a handful of services, such as S3 or EC2 |
| 4–6 | Has configured/set up their own account, used IAM to create multiple users |
| 7–10 | Created subnets within a VPC with security groups and NAT gateways, as well as experience with role-based IAM accounts |

Crucially: "Getting a 10 means this candidate has answered every question you had about AWS and
has more than enough knowledge to be successful in their role. It doesn't mean they know
everything there is about AWS — that isn't the purpose of this exercise. The purpose is to find
out whether they know everything that will allow them to work in your environment."

**How the scores are used:** not as an automatic hire threshold. "It doesn't mean the
highest-scoring candidate should automatically be offered a role." The value shows up when
interviewers disagree by a large margin — that is the conversation where people talk through and
maybe reevaluate their scores.

### The tollbooth exercise

The best exercise the author has seen presented: **have the candidate architect a backend system
that would operate the automatic tollbooth seen on most highways.**

Why it works — it is something everyone is acutely familiar with, yet it forces the candidate to
think about:

- latency
- efficiency
- user experience (not waiting seconds for the toll to go up or stay down)
- when to indicate that the user was nearly out of credits

"To this day, every time I come to one of these, I am reminded of this problem. By the way, that
candidate was me, and, yes, I did get the role."

### Left or right — signalling the exit walk

A technique the author has used successfully: **how he routed the candidate out of his office.**
Handing them to the escort on the **left** meant he did not feel this candidate had an
opportunity; handing them to the **right** signalled his support. That gave the escort the cues
to tailor their exit-walk questions — including upselling harder when they really wanted the
candidate to get a good feel for the environment.

### Cutting a phone screen short, respectfully

The author terminates a pre-interview call politely **after the first 10 minutes** when the person
is clearly not the right fit, giving feedback that they either don't know the basics or, more
annoyingly, have not researched the role they applied for. "This is neither rude nor rash; you are
being respectful of their time and yours, not wasting it chasing something that isn't going to
happen." (He has had candidates search in real time and read answers out of Wikipedia — "though I
applaud their ingenuity, this wasn't the skill set I was necessarily looking for.")

### Saying no, in both camps

**Definitely not:** keep it simple and to the point. Thank them for their interest; say they were
not successful. If they ask where they fell short — do not be scared of the question — be honest
but at a high level, naming the areas where they didn't meet expectations and suggesting the role
may not be what they are looking for. If their Java experience isn't strong enough, leave it at
that.

**Definitely maybe:** let them know they were close, but there was a stronger candidate in a
number of areas. Then ask whether it would be alright to **keep their details on file**, because
although there is no position now you don't want to lose contact and would like to reach out
should things change. Ask permission to **connect on LinkedIn** so they feel the genuine sentiment
in the statement. Common for candidates who overstretched their skills and weren't quite ready for
the senior role they applied for — "but one day, they will be."

## Key Takeaways

- Practice interviewing; never treat it as an hour to get through.
- The interview is more than the hour — the fetch, the office tour, the seating, and the exit walk
  all carry information a list of pre-canned questions never will.
- Build the scorecard before the first interview, scale every criterion, brief every interviewer,
  and fill it in immediately.
- An accepted offer is not a done deal — assume nothing until they turn up for their first day,
  and keep the "definitely maybe" pool warm.
- How you say no "says more about you than celebrating the successful candidates."
- Onboarding: tell them explicitly how to be successful in your team — how you like to be kept
  informed, when to ask for assistance, when to escalate. "Every leader has their own style of
  management, and the successful ones remove all of the guesswork." Then give simple tasks with
  quick wins, such as small copy changes in code that expose the full lifecycle without stress.
