# Chapter 7 — Annual reviews

## Core Idea

An annual review is a car's yearly inspection and service: a check-in that keeps things running
optimally and gives you a heads-up on issues before they become too problematic to resolve. The
chapter's central move is to replace the vague, dreaded review questionnaire with a **skill
matrix** — a grid of competencies against seniority levels, with written expectations in every
cell — so the review becomes data-led rather than gut- or emotion-led, and so a person seeking
promotion can *see* what the next level requires.

The same discipline extends to the two hardest events in people management: **handling failure**
(never blame, find the root, shrink the blast radius) and **termination** (a data-led decision,
never taken in isolation, never taken in anger).

Cadence guidance: quarterly or six-monthly reviews are used by some organizations, but "as long
as your team has regular check-ins with their managers (or you), then anything more than annual
is too much overhead."

## Frameworks Introduced

- **The skill matrix** — competencies (x) × seniority levels (y), each cell holding one or two
  sentences describing what success looks like.
- **Four review outcomes** — no improvement, overconfidence, underperforming, outperforming.
- **Failure investigation** — two camps (mistake/incompetence vs. lack of foresight/planning),
  the **five whys**, and a five-question learning log.
- **Three termination categories** — downsizing the department, skills no longer required, poor
  performance (itself split into "death by a thousand cuts" and "the event").
- **Termination logistics** — notice period, timing, communication, delivery, remove access.
- **The structured demotion.**
- **WFH management** — setup, managing expectations, preventing abuse.

## Key Concepts

- **Why the classic format fails.** "List three areas that the employee is exceeding in, and
  identify three areas that need more attention" is too vague to develop anyone, tedious for the
  manager, hard for the employee to interpret, and it presumes improvement is needed — "But I have
  to find something; otherwise, HR comes after me." And for someone seeking promotion it offers
  nothing: "Just work a little harder, and you will be promoted" implies more hours for no extra
  pay, not smarter work.
- **The matrix doubles as an interview instrument.** "A well-created skills matrix is not only for
  annual reviews but can also make your interviewing process easier because you will have something
  solid to rate each candidate against."
- **Every role gets its own matrix**, tailored to the key attributes that role needs to succeed;
  competencies should cover both hard (technical) and soft (interpersonal) skills and align with
  the core values of the company.
- **Self-assessment first.** Give at least a week's notice plus a copy of the matrix, and ask them
  to self-assess where they believe they are currently operating, thinking of examples where they
  operated at a higher level. You assess separately, referring to the previous year's matrix. In
  the meeting, **lay the two on top of each other and focus the entire conversation on the
  differences.**
- **Consistency, not incidents.** "Do not let one particular incident (successful or otherwise)
  influence you into thinking they deserve a promotion/demotion." And "one swallow does not make a
  summer."
- **The six-month re-evaluation.** If someone is close but not quite at promotion level, say what
  they need to do and set a date roughly six months out, "so you are not having them wait another
  year for a chance at a promotion."
- **Never skip levels.** "No matter how well they are doing, they will be lacking the experience in
  the levels they are jumping that may prove key for consistency at their new promoted level."
- **The matrix is a guideline, not a rule book** — someone excelling in six of eight competencies
  might be enough to promote.
- **Celebrate promotions publicly**, citing the reasons — "this cements your transparency and
  commitment to data-led decisions that show no favoritism or bias."
- **The buck stops here** (Harry S. Truman). "No matter the issue, at the end of the day, their
  failure/mistake is yours to own... Many managers claim that they hold themselves up to this
  standard, but few actually do."
- **Blast radius.** "Your role is to come up with a plan to prevent such a thing from happening
  again, and if you can't truly prevent it, then determine how can you minimize the 'blast radius'
  should it happen again."
- **Check whether the failure was yours.** "If the Security Manager failed to install the firewall
  because you didn't authorize the purchase order, then they are not at fault."
- **Termination is not punishment.** "Termination should not be seen as a punishment or retribution
  but instead a recognition that this person is not qualified for the role they were hired for."
- **Exit interviews.** Some favor them; others believe no good comes of them beyond a final venting.
  Many companies don't keep the notes. "If you are running your group well, nothing new can be
  learned in an exit interview that shouldn't already be known if you have been regularly meeting
  with your team and properly conducted one-on-one meetings."

## Mental Models

- **A CTO who has never experienced failure hasn't yet led.**
- **Death by a thousand cuts** — no single wound is fatal; the sum of the smaller inflictions
  overwhelms. The performance failure that shows up across several fronts at once, hardest to spot
  in new hires who interviewed really well.
- **Out of sight, out of mind** — the empirically observed penalty on remote workers (see the
  Worked Example).
- **The car service** — the framing metaphor for the review itself.

## Anti-patterns

- **The three reactions you must never have to human error:** shouting and using expletives or body
  language that suggests intimidation; using threats or direct insults; carrying out instant
  terminations. "No matter how stressed you are, how angry you become, or how disappointed, no
  justification can excuse any of these reactions."
- **Blaming the person, especially outside your group.** "Being quick to blame others and dismiss
  an issue as someone's lack of focus or incompetence says more about your leadership than the
  individual in question."
- **Terminating because you don't like someone.** "I would bet the problem is more with you than
  them, and you are allowing your emotions to spill into your professional decisions." Seek counsel
  with HR and your CEO.
- **Terminating in isolation.** Always get the support of your CEO/CFO and involve HR at every step.
  "If you can't convince them that you are making the right decision, you are probably being a bit
  impulsive and need to have a rethink."
- **Terminating out of frustration, anger, revenge, or to be seen as doing something.**
- **Never terminate someone without someone else present** — usually an HR representative, failing
  that the CFO or CEO. If one of your managers is terminating, you and HR should be in the room.
  "Say as little as needed while keeping on message."
- **Selling a performance improvement plan as a trap or set of tripwires.** It must show you want
  to set them up for success and will offer whatever assistance helps.
- **Treating the remote team like a call center** instantly available to any query at any time.
- **Hounding remote staff every 15 minutes** — but equally, failing to reach out at all, because you
  have lost the visible clue that someone is struggling.
- **Assuming remote people are chained to their home desks** — you don't expect office staff to be
  at their desks all day; let the chat status feature communicate.

## Reference Tables

### Skill matrix competency areas (developer/engineer example)

| Competency | Definition |
|---|---|
| Core discipline knowledge | Technical information pertaining to their discipline/area |
| Business knowledge | Knowledge of not only the company products but the marketplace and trends |
| Communication | General communication, including emails/chats, talking, and presentations |
| Planning and work | Managing tasks, identifying, scheduling, and execution |
| Output and ownership | Quality of output/end result and how they manage the support of that output |
| Problem-solving and innovation | Ability to solve problems and innovate where necessary |
| Mentoring and leadership | Interacting and knowledge sharing with others |

### The seven-level seniority ladder for a typical developer role (as printed)

| # | Level |
|---|---|
| 1 | Associate, Junior/Graduate |
| 2 | Associate |
| 3 | Associate, Senior |
| 4 | Lead |
| 5 | Manager |
| 6 | Director |
| 7 | Fellow |

"The goal here is to show a career growth path, illustrating the levels that your organization
recognizes at both a compensation and responsibility metric." Some roles will have fewer rungs —
that is fine, but determine it up front to remove ambiguity.

### Four review outcomes and their handling

| Outcome | What it means | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| No improvement | Perfectly acceptable — a person stays consistent at a level you are both happy with; some don't want extra responsibility depending on where they are in life | Pay raises should be related **only to inflation** |
| Overconfidence | Most people rate themselves one or two levels above where they are, citing one example where they truly excelled | Level the conversation back to consistency. Sometimes you lose someone who believes strongly they deserve more — "you are never going to truly satisfy them, and it is best to let them continue their career elsewhere" |
| Underperforming | Can point to being overstressed, overworked, or losing interest in the role | Probe for the reason and work together to address it. **Do not immediately demote**; review in six months to see whether a realignment into a role that gives them success and enjoyment is required |
| Outperforming | Consistently across the board | Promotion is definitely in the cards — but do not jump levels |

### Failure investigation

| Camp | Examples of preventable failures |
|---|---|
| Mistake/incompetence | Was the person properly trained? Was this a basic error or a sophisticated edge case no training would have prepared them for? Were they distracted or stressed? Were steps missed? Can you add software guard rails, checks and balances, retraining, or additional reviews? |
| Lack of foresight/planning | A security breach because the person responsible for security didn't install or update the firewall; data loss because the person in charge of databases didn't properly test and confirm backups; loss of service because the person missed an email for a problem in payment; hardware failure because the person responsible never had an upgrade or continuity plan |

Method: the **five whys** iterative narrowing approach, devised by the founder of Toyota. "It
doesn't always work, because sometimes there are many reasons for something to fail, but what it
does do is to start the questioning at a high level and, at each answer, try to narrow it down."

### The learning log — five questions to ask across recorded failures

| Question | What it tells you |
|---|---|
| Does a problem keep coming up repeatedly? | The processes you put in place to prevent it aren't working |
| Are problems isolated to a handful of clients? | For client-centric organizations, something unique to a client makes it hard to replicate |
| Is there any seasonality to the problem? | Find the external factor — does the switch to daylight savings cause downtime? |
| Are the same actors involved? | A lack of knowledge requiring more training, or changing out these employees |
| What is the impact of the problem/failure? | Rating severity tells you how much effort to deploy — "sometimes problems are just easier to live with, especially if they are short term or rare" |

### Termination logistics

| Step | Guidance |
|---|---|
| Notice period | Is knowledge transfer required? If not, "most of the time, it is advisable to not have the person be physically present for their notice period" |
| Timing | **First thing in the morning, never the last working day of the week** — give them the ability to act on their next opportunity the next working day instead of waiting for Monday |
| Communication | Decide the points and reasons to be communicated to the person; then the messaging for the group — usually "expressing a skill mismatch and wishing them the best for the future is good enough" |
| Delivery | Never terminate alone; have HR, or failing that the CFO or CEO. A witness confirms all steps were taken and talking points communicated and understood |
| Remove access | Execute your offboarding procedure |

### Immediate-dismissal conduct (HR involved as quickly as possible)

Being rude or abusive; using drugs or alcohol; theft; dishonesty; sexual/racist overtones.

### Remote working setup

| Item | Guidance |
|---|---|
| Basics | Good reliable internet line, office furniture, a laptop powerful enough for all their work. Companies commonly help with setup, furniture, and cofunding a faster line |
| Highest-payback purchases | **A high-quality webcam and a headset — plus training on how to use them effectively.** "The payback will be instant" |
| Video platform | Screen sharing, guest access, recording, ideally single sign-on into your backend IT — Google Meet, Microsoft Teams, Zoom, Ring |
| Chat | Slack, Mattermost, Cliq, Teams — has quickly come to replace email as the primary channel; encourage use of the status feature |
| Offices too | Set up conference rooms to join remote people into a meeting quickly |
| The bar | "If you can bring any of your team into a (virtual) meeting as quickly as you would have them huddle around a whiteboard, then you have the foundation to make WFH work" |
| Expectations | Establish **core hours** when the team is available online as if in the office; agree how people signal breaks and lunch. Remote working does not automatically mean flexible hours — that is a separate discussion and agreement outside the WFH plan |
| Check-in cadence | Depending on the work, "once or twice a day via chat isn't unreasonable" |

## Worked Example

### Table 7.1 — Basic matrix structure: Developer (as printed)

Three of the seniority levels, filled in across seven competencies:

| Competency | Junior Associate | Associate | Senior Associate |
|---|---|---|---|
| Core discipline | Basic understanding | Shares discipline knowledge with others | Follows industry trends, knows advanced techniques/patterns |
| Business knowledge | Understanding of what business does at a high level | Knows at least one area of the business at a high level | Knows all the major units of the business at a high level |
| Communication | Clearly articulates their own status | Within the team, clearly communicates their technical detail and status | Clearly translates technical detail/status to nontechnical people |
| Planning and work | Clearly articulated to fine detail by lead | Basic task provided by lead but must further define the finer details | Clearly articulated by lead but must further define details through research and questions |
| Output/ownership | Verified by seniors | Verified by senior, but at a high level | Output trusted to be high quality; designs are reviewed by leads |
| Problem solving | Offers solutions narrow in their scope | Proposes solutions for a wider scope | Proactive in identifying and solving problems |
| Mentoring/leadership | Accepts direction and guidance from others | Mentored by seniors; may mentor juniors | Mentors associates and juniors in their discipline |

Building the full grid means **well over 30 descriptions** — "an exercise that will repay you with
every review and interview you do from here on in." The definitions evolve over time as the team
grows and matures. The goal: "to be crisp and clear enough so the reasoning why someone is at the
level they are is not subjective but that they are demonstrably performing at the expectation level."

### "Wrong window" — a human-error postmortem and its fix

One morning at a client, every alarm went off, alerting the team to something big in production.
Attempts to log on to the servers (early days, when you still SSHed in) yielded nothing — it was as
if they had disappeared.

They had. **The System Manager had accidentally deleted a cluster of web servers in production.**
He had too many terminal windows open and thought he was working in the dev environment, cleaning
up resources that were no longer used.

Several safeguards followed, but the most obvious one: **any terminal window open to a production
server was rendered in a horribly obnoxious color so it stood out.** The record of the incident and
the rectification steps are documented.

Note the pattern the chapter prescribes: this is a *system* failure as much as a human one, so the
fix is a guard rail rather than a reprimand — and the incident becomes material for your interview
process, "so you may gauge how others would cope in the future."

### The UK government remote-working study (2011–2020)

Four findings the book cites (http://mng.bz/PoQg):

| Finding |
|---|
| People who worked remotely were **50% less likely to be promoted** |
| Homeworkers were **more likely to work into the evening** |
| Remote workers had a lower absence rate due to sickness than office workers: **0.9% versus 2.2%** |
| Bonuses were **38% less likely** for remote workers than those in the office |

"What this pointed to was a distinct 'out of sight, out of mind' mentality. People who were not
visibly around were more likely to be overlooked for promotions and bonuses, yet ironically, were
expected (and did) work longer hours than their office counterparts." The disparity should shrink
post-COVID given adoption rates, but you must "put in the necessary checks to make sure your remote
workforce is treated as fairly as your in-office teams" — and guard against subconsciously harboring
prejudice or holding remote staff to higher expectations.

### The structured demotion

An alternative the author has seen work well: if someone is supposed to operate at a senior level
but is really coming in at a junior level, **adjust expectations and set them at the level at which
they can achieve success.** This may involve an adjustment in compensation, "but it works well for
both parties." Seen when someone is given a chance to lead engineers, then realizes they don't like
it and just want to code.

## Key Takeaways

- With a skill matrix, annual reviews stop being an HR box-tick and become something both sides can
  look forward to and complete quickly.
- Build the matrix once per role; it pays back at every review and every interview.
- When something breaks, get in front of it with the stakeholders — "how you get in front of them,
  communicating with the necessary stakeholders, will define your leadership."
- Downsizing: exhaust every alternative first (retraining, another department), communicate openly
  so it is no surprise, help people find new roles — and consider that another company may want the
  whole intact team.
- Skills-no-longer-required: give plenty of runway to retrain, and if you risk everyone leaving
  before the project completes, offer a **stay-on bonus**.
- Termination decisions must be documented, data-led, agreed with CEO/CFO/HR, and delivered with a
  witness.
- Remote management is not different in kind — it just demands firmer stated expectations, deliberate
  check-ins, and active guarding against the out-of-sight penalty. Virtual team events (happy hours,
  online gaming, quizzes via Jackbox.tv and YouTube), thirty minutes every few weeks at the end of
  the week, pay team-building dividends.
