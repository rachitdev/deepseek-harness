# Chapter 4 — Building a team

## Core Idea

"You are only as strong as your team — tend to its future growth now." The chapter's single
piece of standing advice: **never see recruiting as a one-time event.** Make it part of your
routine, with the mindset that you are always hiring. Bench strength is a wonderful tool to
have in your back pocket; the most successful CTOs bring people on nearly immediately because
they have built up a great pipeline of candidates.

The second half is a claim most CTOs underrate: **the job description is the most important
document in the entire hiring process.** "If this doesn't work, then everything downstream is
moot." When teams complain that hiring is hard, the job description usually explains why —
"you catch more bees with honey."

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Five resource types** — FTE, contracting individuals, outsourcing to vendor, onshore
  resourcing, offshore resourcing (with a comparison table).
- **Three hiring events** — project development, attrition, team exhaustion/back support.
- **The discipline matrix** — a human-capital audit scoring each team member 0–10 per
  discipline, with Current / Total / Avg / Need / Delta columns.
- **Five sourcing channels** — referrals, recruitment agency, headhunting, graduate
  fairs/internships, self-serve online.
- **The five-section job description** — Title / Description / Minimum requirements /
  Required skills / About-legal — adapted from **Lou Adler's** approach (*Hire with Your
  Head*, Wiley, 2007): sell the opportunity, not the tasks; be goal oriented, not activity
  oriented.

## Key Concepts

- **Hire for two to three core strengths plus a suite of secondary skills**, not a narrow skill
  set. Example given: strong in cloud architecture and Java, with database, messaging, and
  security as secondary. "It is rare (and, in my view, unhealthy) for an organization to hire
  someone for a very narrow skill set."
- **Overlapping disciplines are encouraged.** They give bench strength and stimulate healthy
  collaboration. "An idea is good only if it can stand up to scrutiny."
- **Key person risk** — the one person who is the only one who knows how to restart a piece of
  software, handled that account, or has their own toolbox of scripts. PE firms in particular
  hate single resources propping up the business.
- **Contract-to-hire** — "try before you buy"; convertible to FTE for a small fee, useful when
  you haven't yet figured out whether a position is full time.
- **Contractors must still be interviewed** — the common misconception is that whatever the
  agency sends is top-notch. Focus less on soft skills and more on hard technical skills; they
  should produce from day one. Check references for independents. Some countries prevent hiring
  a single contractor in an extended role for too long.
- **Advertise the pay scale.** Management may push back, fearing current employees discover they
  are underpaid. "Don't let this situation happen; it will never end well." A pay scale creates
  trust and weeds out those looking for a specific range.
- **The stretch goal** (Adler). Most people move to better themselves professionally, and it
  usually takes a new job to get that promotion. Make the job sound a little harder than what
  they're doing now — but not out of reach. "This is my time": the most successful CTO hires the
  author has made were not the ones already doing the job but the ones ready for the next step
  who wanted to prove themselves.
- **Years of experience is not a requirement.** "You want someone who can fill the role, so does
  it matter how long it took them to figure it out?" Career history already shows how long
  they've been in the industry.
- **The 75% rule.** Most people reading a required-skills list will apply if they meet at least
  75% of the items — as they should.
- **Offshore expectation levelling.** Don't expect the same thoroughness or attention to detail
  as your local team — not because the work is shoddy but because "they don't have the context
  for the daily happenings that can shape a product." Start with small, quantifiable tasks to
  gauge quality and time.

## Mental Models

- **Don't replace, evolve.** After attrition: "The last thing you want to do is to try to find a
  replacement. No matter how good (or poor) the person you are replacing, you will never find
  their exact equal, and to measure a new hire against that is not setting up the future for
  success."
- **This too shall pass** (Lincoln). You may believe you have the dream team; forces outside your
  control will force a change, and you mold a new one.
- **The pedigreed racehorse.** "Even the most pedigreed racehorse needs training — they don't win
  races straight out of the gate." Don't treat graduates as seasoned resources and then despair.
- **A happy home life is a happy work life.** Partners and spouses see stress and absence first;
  don't dismiss concerns that reach you.
- **If I ask you to work late, I will be there with you** — the author spent the full 24 hours of
  a sponsored VCU hackathon on-site with students on a company rotation schedule.

## Anti-patterns

- **Environmental Community Liaison Associate.** A fancy way of saying Refuse Collector — it
  won't attract people who can do the job because they won't recognize what you're selling.
- **Opening a description by talking about your company.** The first sentence must describe what
  the candidate will be doing; there is plenty of room for the company later.
- **Marketing filler** — *exciting, fun, fast-paced, energetic*. Most such oversell words are implied.
- **Bloated minimum requirements**, especially "minimum experience of x years."
- **Insulting required skills** — listing Microsoft Office proficiency for a developer role. "Do
  not treat your candidates like idiots."
- **Turning About/legal into unread terms and conditions.** "Why waste this valuable real estate?"
- **Keyword-matcher recruitment agencies** — the résumé pushers who send whatever matches.
- **Assuming your people can just absorb the new project.** "Can't you simply do more with who you
  have?" is a fair CEO question you must be prepared to answer with data.
- **Treating a referral differently** from any other candidate — no shortcuts, no special
  considerations, or you inherit social guilt if it doesn't work out.
- **Under-managing offshore.** "Many engagements fail because of a lack of proper oversight and
  management from the domestic side of the relationship."

## Reference Tables

### Table 4.1 — Resources comparison review (as printed)

| Type | Strength | Weakness | Compensation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Full-time employee | Long term; client facing; aligns to company vision; promotes loyalty; in person | Costly over time; hard to remove; can lead to too much reliance on an individual | Fixed salary; health care + pension; stock options/bonus |
| Contracting individuals | Short-term projects with specific deliverables; terminate fast; great for short-term expertise requirements; simple billing model; in person | No loyalty or vested interest in the company vision; no long-term view, focused on the project at hand | Fixed hourly/daily rate |
| Outsourcing to vendor | Long-term commodity type of servicing; continuity of service while resources are rotated | No team building; narrow in what they can do for you; no in-house expertise on the subject area; no on-the-job learning | Fixed price per contract |
| Onshore resourcing | Long-term, defined projects; engaging a team, not an individual; good when a project requires a lot of short-term expertise; complete outsourced management | No learning or expertise, only the resulting project; only for larger projects; requires greater definition of the project; harder to find/vet the right company; loss of control of individual resources | Project price or monthly retainer |
| Offshore resourcing | Low cost; a different time zone can increase your time availability; once found, the right company can scale resources up and down quickly | Time zone issues; very hard to find/vet the right company; much harder for dispute resolution; cultural differences may impact project quality | Fixed price per contract/resource |

Tender rule for onshore: get **three to five bids**; if there is a **larger than 20% price
difference**, remove those outliers instantly from the contenders. Ask for (usually anonymized)
résumés of the people who will work on your team.

### Hiring events and their questions

| Event | What to ask |
|---|---|
| Project development | Which disciplines are required and how do they map to an individual? How will this individual be used after the project — still required, or not? Will the role benefit more than just this project? |
| Attrition | What were the top two things you were counting on this person for? What skills and expertise levels are lost? What in-house knowledge about internal or client systems has gone, and was it documented? Was this really a full-time role, or had it run its course? What was the person lacking that could have made them more productive? What is the impact on overall team morale? |
| Team exhaustion / back support | See the tells below |

### Tells of team exhaustion

| Signal | What it means |
|---|---|
| Project/sprint deadlines missed more than hit | In the retrospective, watch the *reason* — if "resources pulled away" recurs, projects are doomed before they start |
| Overall PTO is not being used | Do not assume they love the job too much to leave; they may feel they can't take prolonged time off |
| Panic/stress when key individuals take PTO | Heightened stress because that person's knowledge and skills sit nowhere else |
| Overreliance on a single individual | Key person risk |
| Prolonged late-night and weekend work | Occasional late nights are the nature of the business; continual ones mean "you are effectively asking them to work two or more jobs" |

Leader's job: decide whether what you are seeing is **a blip or a pattern.** If it's a pattern,
you have the data for the case to the CEO/CFO. Enlist the team to watch for these traits too —
"we seem to see it in other teams more quickly than we can in our own."

### Discipline matrix scoring scale

| Score | Meaning |
|---|---|
| 10 to 8 | Primary discipline with a senior/advanced knowledge |
| 7 to 5 | Primary discipline, but considered junior; requires guidance or mentoring from a senior |
| 4 to 3 | Secondary skill that complements primary discipline |
| 3 to 0 | Knows enough about the skill to assist and provide backup, but not a core competency |

Mark any skill exclusive to one individual with **brackets `[ ]`** so single-point reliance is
visible at a glance.

### Sourcing channels

| Channel | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|
| Referrals (typical bonus $500–$1,000) | Timing — can be quick to find someone; inexpensive | No guarantee of any candidates; can make things awkward for the referring employee if mishandled |
| Recruitment agency | Quick to present a number of qualified candidates; prequalified before you review | Takes effort to find an agency that understands your needs; rarely do any technical validation; expensive at 10%–25% of first year's salary |
| Headhunting (vendors, open source projects, book authors, article/blog writers) | Already prequalified — they know their trade to a high degree; if you've worked with them you know the personality fits | More opportunistic than strategic; search is very narrow |
| Graduate fairs/internships (project sponsorship, internships, sponsored hackathons) | You get to know students over a long period; a great way to give back even if you hire no one | Very long term — think at least six months; requires mentoring and patience |
| Self-serve online (LinkedIn, ZipRecruiter, Indeed, Stack Overflow) | Complete control over wording and presentation; very cost effective | Not guaranteed; generates a lot of noise from agencies and window shoppers |

Channel fit: lean to **Stack Overflow for developers**, **LinkedIn for noncoding roles**.
Cost is typically a few hundred dollars a month with upsell packages.

### Job description — the five-section structure

| # | Section | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Title** | The most important piece. First thing read — over the phone, in an email, a tweet, a search result. Don't be too clever; keep it simple. Stick to standard industry titles no matter your internal titling. Build in as much metadata as possible without going overboard |
| 2 | **Description** | Grab interest in the first sentence, second at a push; by the third you're adding detail and color. Then talk directly to the person you want to hire, in their language. Fringe benefits (international travel, working with clients) belong here |
| 3 | **Minimum requirements** | Keep to a bare minimum. Real, demonstrable, non-negotiable items only — legally permitted to work in your jurisdiction, or a required certification such as security clearance |
| 4 | **Required skills** | Only what is relevant; as a rule of thumb, anything you would discuss in an interview. Load requirements with words like *demonstrable* or *working knowledge* to signal depth. Resist years of experience |
| 5 | **About/legal information** | Describe the company, products/clients, and why it is a good place to entrust a career. Equal opportunity statement, vacation, pensions, health care, work-from-home policy |

Opening-sentence dos and don'ts:

| Do | Don't |
|---|---|
| Describe what it is the candidate will be doing | Talk about your company |
| — | Repeat what your title has conveyed |
| — | Waste time with unnecessary surplus words |

## Worked Example

### The discipline matrix for a five-person team (as printed)

| Discipline | Cormac | Noah | Joseph | Dillon | Preston | Avg current skill | Total current skill | Need | Delta |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| AWS | [7] | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 | [7] | [7] | 10 | -3 |
| JavaScript | 2 | 5 | 5 | 2 | 1 | 3 | 15 | 20 | -5 |
| Java | 2 | 0 | 0 | 8 | 0 | 2 | 10 | 5 | +5 |
| HTML/CSS | 0 | 8 | 8 | 0 | 0 | 3 | 16 | 15 | +1 |
| MySQL | 0 | 0 | 0 | 8 | 8 | 3 | 16 | 10 | +6 |

**The two concerns that immediately stand out**, exactly as the book reads them:

1. **AWS** — the skill is meeting the requirement, but it is **reliant on a single resource**.
   "If Cormac decides to move on, we really have a problem." (Hence the bracket notation.)
2. **JavaScript** — the largest negative delta (-5), and the people who do have it are juniors,
   so **a senior hire here is appropriate**.

The Need column is subjective and changes with projects; it can be split into multiple columns —
per project, next six months, six months and beyond. Keeping the matrix current must be a
collaborative effort: it surfaces where strengths and desires lie and who would benefit from
training.

**The human capital audit process:** when the author starts with a new CTO or team, he asks for
everyone's résumés and builds the matrix, plus previous reviews to see how people evolved. He
then sits with the principals and asks them to review his findings, "which usually results in
them completely readjusting every figure I create — that is okay. It's so much easier to edit
than to create." The consistent result is surprise: the team is usually not as strong in a given
area as they thought.

### Titles, rewritten

Seven titles the book puts up for inspection:

| Title | Verdict |
|---|---|
| VP of Engineering | Reads clearly |
| Senior Web Developer | Reads clearly |
| Head of Digital Transformation | **Too vague** — could mean anything, including roles outside the technical space |
| Cloud Architect | **Not bad, could be better** — specify the provider and level: *Senior AWS Architect* says so much more |
| React Mobile Developer (Jnr) | Good — speaks intelligently and directly to the intended audience; states the framework up front so people don't have to read further |
| Project Manager | Known, but if you already work with Scrum, say it in the title |
| Scrum Master | States the method up front |

### Three opening sentences that get straight to the point (as printed)

- **Cloud Architect** — "Lead the team transforming our traditional data center to run exclusively
  in AWS to provide resilience and self-scalability for our web infrastructure."
- **React Mobile Developer (Junior)** — "Join and learn from our seasoned mobile developers as they
  transform our digital offering through our signature Android and iOS apps."
- **Project Manager** — "Organize, educate, and manage our engineering team projects as they embark
  on our continued growth."

### The mystery shopper test for a recruitment agency

Have someone register their interest with the agency for a particular discipline and observe the
quality of roles offered back. The author has used this repeatedly "to weed out the charlatan
résumé pushers from the ones who know their trade" — some roles offered were so far from the
core competency of his résumé he wondered whether it had been read at all. Justified because
you may be paying a one-off agency fee of **up to 20% of the salary**.

Lori Jennings (founder, Jennings ProSearch) on qualifying an agency: know the standard metrics —
**time to fill, completion rates, retention times** — then ask: "Are they constantly talking to
people in your role across companies understanding your challenges as a CTO? Can they help advise
you on compensation? Do they understand what motivates candidates at your level? Do they have a
strong network of candidates who will take a call from them?"

## Key Takeaways

- There are more options than FTEs; match resource type to time frame, budget, and whether the
  skill persists past the project.
- Build the case for a hire from data — the project plan, the attrition analysis, or the
  exhaustion tells — and be ready to state the **impact of not hiring** as reality, not threat
  or ultimatum. Ask for help communicating the resulting delay to the waiting client or department.
- Keep the discipline matrix current; brackets expose key person risk before it bites.
- Cultivate at least two sourcing channels, always on.
- Write the job description yourself, sell the opportunity and the stretch, and publish the pay scale.
- In a candidate-short market, if a great resource appears without an open position, ask the CFO
  to squeeze something out of the budget. "If you don't ask, you will never know."
