# Chapter 15 — You, Inc.

## Core Idea

"While you are doing all this great team building, who is looking after you? Who is keeping you
focused on what you are looking for from your professional life?"

The final chapter turns the book's own management practices back on the CTO: run yourself as a
business — **You, Inc.** — with your own review process, your own mentor, your own way of keeping
current, your own succession plan, and a career plan you keep "updated and measured."

The warning that motivates it: "The last thing anyone wants is to suddenly realize 5 or 10 years have
slipped by and you really haven't advanced as far as you dreamed. That sounds like a long time, but it
can whip by very quickly, especially if you are head-down, making great changes."

And the closing claim of responsibility: **"There is only one person responsible for your success, and
that is you."**

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Three directions of feedback** — your CEO, your team, yourself.
- **Four areas to ask your CEO to review you on.**
- **The mentor definition** and how to use one.
- **Managing imposter syndrome** with the résumé-as-journal.
- **Keeping pace** — three sources, let themes emerge, leading edge not bleeding edge.
- **Succession planning** as quiet, multi-year preparation.
- **Stepping up** — how to apply for a first CTO role.

## Key Concepts

- **Executive reviews get skipped.** "It is common for organizations to have a formal review process,
  yet skip the executive team. Do not use that as an excuse. Run toward feedback and set up a small
  structure so you can get formal input on your progress." The mechanism: take one of your regular CEO
  meetings, ask how you are doing, and **ask point blank what else you can do to be more successful.**
  "If no critique can be had, then invite your CEO to set some goals for you, or define areas in which
  they would like to see more from you."
- **Your success is your boss's success.** "All good CEOs are interested in developing their executive
  team and will be forthcoming with their feedback. If yours is not, help them get there with a little
  nudge and guidance in the right direction."
- **Silence from your team is itself feedback.** "If people feel they cannot talk openly and honestly to
  you, then that is feedback in and of itself that you can do something with. You don't want to rule
  with an iron fist through fear and intimidation. You want to lead with confidence, compassion,
  firmness, and fairness."
- **What a review is for.** "Reviews are not character assassinations. They are there to help the person
  be better by understanding how their actions and communications are being received. In our heads, we
  always assume we are clear and articulate. We may be blind to the fact we are just shy of detail that
  would make a huge difference for the listener."
- **Mentor, defined.** "Someone who has your best interests at heart but does not have a direct influence
  over your career" — typically **not your boss**. "A boss can be a guiding influence and someone you
  learn from, but they are not a mentor in the purest form." A mentor is someone you confide in and seek
  counsel from, offering an outside perspective without a vested interest in the outcome. "Sometimes the
  mentor doesn't even need to know they are serving you in a 'mentor' role."
- **Multiple mentors are encouraged.** "It is perfectly acceptable (and encouraged) to have a number of
  mentors and advisors, each influencing a different area of your life." But be conscious of their
  sphere of expertise — someone unfamiliar with your environment "may not be able to properly appreciate
  the area you are seeking assistance or input on."
- **A mentor gives perspective, not answers.** "They are not there to give you answers — you make all
  your own decisions. They help you with perspective and ask you the sorts of questions that will help
  you see with clarity."
- **Imposter syndrome, defined.** "That feeling you are somewhere you shouldn't be — a place you do not
  belong. You live in constant fear that at some point you will be discovered and asked to leave. Some
  mix-up in HR hired the wrong person, and you have been winging it in their place." The reassurance:
  "at some point in their career, most people feel this, including well-known celebrities."
- **Managing it:** recognize it; remind yourself of your achievements and journey so far; breathe; draw
  strength from your experience. "Never feel you are an island. We live in one of the most connected
  times of human civilization — somewhere, someone will be able to help."
- **You cannot keep up with everything — period.** "Let me ease your stress here and now and offer you an
  answer — you can't." Focus on what is relevant to your personal interests and to your enterprise.
- **Leading edge, not bleeding edge.** "The bleeding edge is too early — it is called bleeding because it
  is sharp and can hurt if it goes wrong. There is no need for you to take unnecessary risks and bets on
  technology that may not make it in the long run."
- **Succession begins before you plan it.** "As your organization grows, your hands-on work will decrease.
  As you focus more on management, you will have others pick up the areas you used to manage directly.
  This natural evolution, of you rotating out of day-to-day chores, is a form of succession."
- **Prepare a successor quietly, over years.** "They don't even need to be aware of your overall plan;
  they will just see it as — which it is — your continued trust and confidence in them as you expand
  their field of responsibility." It takes confidence, "because we always want to feel special and needed."
- **You won't pick your replacement.** "You probably won't have much control over who is actually hired
  in your place. If you leave the right way, then your input, guidance, and wisdom will be welcomed and
  sought."
- **Career planning is not a salary target.** "Planning your future is a concentrated effort of identifying
  your wants and desires and making the appropriate plan to achieve them. One thing is for sure: sitting
  doing nothing, waiting for the phone to ring, doesn't work."
- **Success, defined.** "Success is making the right decision at the right time for the right reason."
- **Hiring a CTO is not hiring a senior developer.** "You will be judged on your soft skills and how you
  are going to manage a team to provide successful outcomes. This is where you need to focus your strengths
  when it comes to the interview stage."
- **The cover letter carries the application.** "The number of CTO roles is small in comparison to
  managerial or senior roles. Therefore, there will be a lot of competition from folks like you... Although
  your résumé is important, your cover letter is extremely valuable. This is your opportunity to lay out how
  you could operate at this level and why you are a good fit for this company. **Your résumé should be
  focused more on your management skills than your technical skills.**"

## Mental Models

- **You, Inc.** — "keep an eye on 'You, Inc.,' and keep that business plan updated and measured."
- **Reading the label from inside the bottle.** "It is hard to read the label of the bottle from the inside.
  You need those around you to give you honest feedback."
- **The Office.** David Brent / Michael Scott believe they are super popular, loved, and count their team as
  friends; the side looks and horrific expressions say otherwise. "It is easy to recognize this on the screen
  but much harder to see it in real life... we've all had a *The Office* situation in our careers at one point."
- **The automotive mentor.** You consult them "only when you are looking to purchase a new car, not when you
  need to fill it up with gas. Even then, your automotive mentor isn't going to tell you what model to
  purchase. They will listen to your requirements, offer you other things to maybe consider, and help you
  think through your process."
- **The lottery ticket, revisited.** "Oh God, why do I never win the lottery?" — to which God answers, "Meet
  me halfway and at least buy a lottery ticket!"
- **The industry's blessing and curse.** "One of the most exciting things about our industry is its changing
  landscape, how it seems to reinvent itself every five years. One of the hardest things about our industry
  is its changing landscape, how it seems to reinvent itself every five years!"

## Anti-patterns

- **Skipping your own review** because the company's formal process skips executives.
- **Ruling by fear and intimidation**, then wondering why no feedback comes back up.
- **Beating yourself up.** "A good leader has the self-introspection to be honest with themselves and know
  when something could have been done better or differently. Do not be too harsh on yourself... just be
  self-aware and make corrections accordingly."
- **Treating a mentor as a decision-maker** or as a new best friend to consult on every choice.
- **Chasing the bleeding edge** — and, on the competitor-watching side, mistaking hype-wave riding for signal
  ("note how many block chain solutions are being touted?").
- **Bluffing your way into a CTO role.** "Be up front and note that although you have never had the CTO title
  formally bestowed on you, you have the relevant experience to take the step up."
- **Waiting for an entrenched CTO to leave.** "Outside of waiting for them to move on or retire, you can't.
  So, you have to look elsewhere when you are feeling you are ready to take that next step."
- **Compromising on your own worth.** "Know your own worth, know your own wants, and do not compromise."

## Reference Tables

### Ask your CEO to review you on

| Area | The question |
|---|---|
| Communication | Are you keeping the business adequately informed of projects, directions, and issues? |
| Level of detail | Is the level of detail being communicated too little or too much? |
| Business intelligence | What is your contribution to the overall business intelligence? Are trends or industry shifts being properly tracked and talked about internally? |
| Initiative/vision | Is confidence high that the technology is tracking to cope with projected future growth and opportunities? |

### Three directions of feedback

| Direction | How to get it |
|---|---|
| Your CEO / immediate boss | Use an existing regular meeting; ask how you're doing and what more you can do to be successful; if no critique comes, ask them to set goals |
| Your team | Requires a management style where people believe feedback won't come back to haunt them. "If your tone is right, your direct reports should have no problems in preparing a small review for you to act on" |
| Yourself | Self-introspection, honestly, without excessive self-criticism |

The self-audit the table is for: **"How many of the big shifts did you actively track from their inception,
versus the ones you were forced to look at, and, finally, which ones did you miss so completely you still
don't know what they really mean?"** And the perspective: "If you think back 10 years, much of the list was
still an academic/R&D project yet to break into the mainstream. Certain items now have become so ingrained
in our modern enterprise, it's hard to remember how we coped earlier."

### Keeping pace — the method

| Step |
|---|
| Choose **at least three sources of industry news** that fall within your area |
| Sign up to their email newsletter or feed — "a way for information to come to you" |
| Wait and let it come in organically. Read, digest, continue |
| When a theme starts popping up repeatedly **from different sources**, give it more attention — "maybe task someone in your team to explore it a little further" |
| Watch competitors and how they position their products — even if they're riding the hype wave, it gives you a reason to explore an area so you have an answer if asked |
| If affordable, subscribe to an analyst or research firm — **Gartner and Forrester**, "a few thousand a year," giving research and product comparison reports plus discounted conferences. "They keep a finger on the pulse so you don't have to" |
| Empower and encourage your team to watch verticals — a framework or language — and present a proposal for research time |

"Learning is fun, but it can feel like you don't have time to keep updated. You can't afford not to; however,
you can share the burden and let it bubble up naturally."

## Worked Example

### The résumé as a slow journal

The chapter's concrete practice against imposter syndrome:

> **Keep your résumé updated every six months.**

"It serves as a very slow journal, letting you catalog major milestones."

Two benefits, as printed:

1. **A reminder of your accomplishments.**
2. **Your résumé is ready for any opportunity that may come along at a moment's notice.**

The reason it works: "In the heat of a self-doubt moment, you can forget all the good things. Keep your
résumé updated, and keep it accessible. All the greats (and those heading for greatness) have moments of
self-doubt and reflection. It is natural."

### Applying for your first CTO role

The chicken-and-egg problem stated plainly: "You will most likely be put off with some of the requirements,
particularly experience. This is the classic chicken-and-egg situation: how do you get experience if you
don't get a chance to earn it?"

The sequence the chapter prescribes:

1. **Start looking at job openings** — before you are ready to apply, "look around, and get a feel for the
   language of each job description. Get a feel for how they are positioning the need for a CTO, especially
   companies for whom this is their first CTO hire."
2. **Look at the role as a whole** and determine whether the experience requirement is a soft or hard limit.
   "Sometimes an organization is looking to firm up their technology leadership, and therefore, they are
   recruiting their first CTO."
3. **If you have relevant team-leadership experience, throw your hat in the ring** — and be up front that
   the title has never formally been bestowed on you.
4. **Lead with the cover letter**, laying out how you could operate at this level and why you fit this
   company; point the résumé at management skills rather than technical ones.
5. **Prepare for a soft-skills interview** — the company is looking for "a reliable, steady hand to take on
   the responsibility of the office."

### The transatlantic language lesson

When the author first started working in the United States, coming from the United Kingdom, some of his
phrases and language "while innocent at home, were not landing the same on the other side of the Atlantic."

"I got to know HR very well, because they had many a word with me to explain how certain phrases would offend
instead of offer support or mirth (the Urban Dictionary was my friend to prove there was no malice).
Therefore, I had to learn to **read faces and body language at a whole different level and check the way I
phrased things before I vocalized them.**"

### The author's own career arc, as a model for the plan

"When I stepped out from university as a fresh-faced graduate, I had no idea the role of CTO even existed. My
only goal was to learn as much as possible and be the best at what I do. What that thing was, was not defined,
but within my first few years in industry, I had a much greater view of the landscape and could focus my
vision and plan for my career. Since then, as I have achieved each goal, I have been opened up to a new world,
where I have adopted and refocused my next set of goals."

The questions the chapter puts to the reader directly: *Why now? What steps have led you to this stage? For
the role you are in now, what does success look like?* "Knowing what success is will make it easier to rate
your happiness and satisfaction and track your progress."

## Key Takeaways

- Seek input from your boss to identify areas to improve.
- Take regular pulse checks from your team to make sure you are delivering what they need.
- A mentor helps you think through the larger life decisions that come your way.
- Recognize your achievements by keeping a journal or résumé — it is the antidote to imposter syndrome.
- Don't stress about keeping up with everything; take steps so you don't miss big moves.
- While lifting and building up others, keep an eye on who could step in for you.
- Define what you want from your career so you can recognize success and achievements when they come.
- "Take stock of your own career. Celebrate your journey so far, and think hard about all the major decisions
  you have had to make to get there."
