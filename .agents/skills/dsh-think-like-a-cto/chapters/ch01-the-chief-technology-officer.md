# Chapter 1 — The Chief Technology Officer (CTO)

## Core Idea

The CTO role is the least universally defined seat at the executive table. The CEO
"leads the charge" and the CFO "writes the checks," but poll a room of readers on what
a CTO is and you get as many answers as there are people — and each is probably right.
Williamson resolves the ambiguity by declaring an operating assumption for the whole
book: **the CTO owns both the technology vision and its execution.** The CEO and board
own the *what*; the CTO owns the *how*.

The scope line the book draws: the CTO owns the **front office** — the servers and
software that make up the client-facing product, platform, or enterprise. The CTO does
*not* normally own the **back office** — desktops, mail servers, phones, printers — which
sits with an IT director or manager typically reporting to the CFO. In smaller companies
that line is deliberately blurry; startup CTOs order laptops, haggle with the ISP, replace
printer ink, and cut code.

## Frameworks Introduced

**The Four CTO Archetypes.** Each comes with a litmus test of "you may be here if you
answer yes to one or more."

1. **Prestartup, in name only** — two people, a visionary and a technologist; the title
   was granted for gravitas (partly compensating for little or no pay).
2. **Funded startup: the technology expert with money** — investors are in, the budget
   feels like a win, but it is really credit that must be repaid.
3. **Established company: their first CTO** — founder-led, successful, systems aging
   quietly; the CTO must be strategic and transformational.
4. **Established company with CTO** — a defined engineering org, existing roadmaps, a
   handover rather than a founding.

**The First 100 Days** — a four-phase onboarding arc (0–7 / 7–30 / 30–70 / 70–100 days).

**The "Do We Need a CTO?" test** — a 14-question data-led case for management.

## Key Concepts

- **What a good CTO provides the company** (six items): a stable, scalable, manageable
  platform to grow on; informed leadership keeping the platform relevant and updated;
  opportunities to explore new areas by unleashing the platform's true power; operational
  confidence giving customers reliability and predictability; creative uses of technology
  for company and customer benefit; and the ability to recruit, retain, and manage a
  highly efficient team.
- **Decision paralysis** — introduced here in the funded-startup archetype: the fear of
  making the wrong decision prevents any decision being made. Revisited at length in Ch 3.
- **VP of Engineering vs. CTO** — sometimes just a naming scheme; sometimes a real split
  where the VP of Engineering reports to the CTO and takes the logistics of running the
  engineering team. The book assumes the VP of Engineering is on the road to CTO.
- **Larger and longer-term thinking** — "larger" is not bigger platforms, it is how the
  systems live in the context of the company and end client. "Longer term" means
  **five-year blocks**, a natural cycle of the business.
- **The engineer's advantage** — the biggest tool an engineer-turned-CTO has is the
  *knowledge of expectations*: what will work, what will not, how long it should take or last.

## Mental Models

- **The chess board.** From the preface: the CEO is the king, the CFO the queen (most
  powerful piece, involved in all money in and out), and the **CTO is the rook/castle** —
  the next most powerful piece, providing the support that lets the other pieces move and
  clearing paths into the distance.
- **The house with aging AC.** The established-company-without-a-CTO problem: you live in
  the house for years and forget how crucial the AC and hot water are, not realizing the
  systems age each year, waiting to fail spectacularly.
- **The restaurant kitchen.** "A restaurant cannot run without a chef to look after and
  run the kitchen... A CTO is the chef in the company, keeping an eye on everything that
  is needed to serve the food."
- **Going to lose for an hour** (Maverick, 1994). Mel Gibson's character loses deliberately
  for the first hour at the poker table to watch the other players' tells. The CTO role is
  a marathon, not a sprint — you have time to make your presence felt.
- **Old dull before new shiny.** It is tempting to focus on the new shiny objects, but the
  old dull ones are "the ones paying everyone's salary."

## Anti-patterns

- **Treating CTO as an extension of the senior-engineer job.** "Most will look at the role,
  make some assumptions, and presume it to be just an extension to their current daily
  tasks. This type of attitude, though common, is doomed to failure."
- **Founding-CTO territoriality.** Not wanting to give up control, feeling threatened when
  hiring people who may be more qualified.
- **Freewheeling as a virtue.** Believing a complete lack of structure is modern and trendy.
  "Structure and process are there for a reason: to maintain a consistent and predictable
  customer experience."
- **Gut-only business cases.** Cases that win are data led, not emotionally led. Gut instinct
  plays into decision making but "should never be the only reason."
- **Optimizing for a number instead of the consequence.** See the Worked Example.
- **Judging out loud in week one.** Engineers see a problem and want to address it; fight
  that urge.

## Reference Tables

### The four CTO archetypes and their litmus tests

| Archetype | Litmus test (yes to one or more) |
|---|---|
| Prestartup, in name only | No one to challenge or validate technical decisions; you are the only person developing; this is your first real job, even unpaid; very little process or structure in the business; only you know how to maintain the systems |
| Funded startup (expert with money) | Company is less than three years old; no one in your department has been there more than 18 months; you are on your second or third platform evolution; the company spends more than it brings in; product features change weekly/monthly |
| Established company, first CTO | Established with paying clients and mature product line; majority of the platform is over 10 years old; only a handful know how to keep things up and don't know *why* it works; most of the company doesn't realize its reliance on the technology or data; no standard policies, no real security or compliance focus |
| Established company with CTO | CTO position has existed at least three years; engineering department established with roles and hierarchy; process and standard practices instituted and mostly adhered to; roadmaps, strategic visions and project plans exist; technology stable and understood by most |

### The first 100 days

| Phase | Focus | Activity |
|---|---|---|
| 0–7 days | The client's perspective | Learn what the company does, its terminology, pricing model, top and most important clients. Not too much detail — get a sense of how the company runs, what each department does, who the key individuals are |
| 7–30 days | Listen and observe | Get to know the engineering department and every other department in depth. Engineering should run on autopilot while you come up to speed. Listen and observe: how people perceive engineering, the personalities, individuals' backgrounds, contributions, frustrations, goals; the current project pipeline |
| 30–70 days | Lift the lid | Get system access and poke around yourself: read source code for quality, review data stores, review servers and infrastructure. Note easy wins and quick fixes. Meet established clients over lunch or a visit for an outside perspective. Start forming short-term plans and the shape of the long-term vision |
| 70–100 days | Begin execution | Put in place missing processes, structure the fluffy areas. Identify the components required to keep the lights on and what it takes to shore them up, so large initiatives don't stop the company functioning. Multiple meetings with the CEO on their vision. Get project wins under your belt |

### Top 10 qualities for a CTO

| # | Quality |
|---|---|
| 1 | Can lead and inspire through lifting others up |
| 2 | Looks far down the road before making any decisions |
| 3 | Communicates in a way that is confident yet nonpatronizing |
| 4 | Listens to their CEO and board to understand their desires and goals |
| 5 | Keeps themselves updated with the technologies most relevant to their company's industry |
| 6 | Is not scared to fail and, should they fail, refuses to point the finger |
| 7 | Doesn't try to do everything themselves and instead delegates to their team |
| 8 | Knows they are not the smartest person in their team just because they are the boss |
| 9 | Is confident to acknowledge and credit a good idea, irrespective of its source |
| 10 | Has a vision that can be communicated and actioned |

## Worked Example

### The "Do we need a CTO?" checklist, as the book gives it

The business case for the role must be data led. Williamson supplies the questions;
answering yes points to the need for a dedicated CTO. The framing test first: *is there
custom software that has been developed in-house without which clients wouldn't be able
to engage what they pay the company for?*

Then the fourteen questions:

1. Is there custom software being developed for clients or in support of clients?
2. Has there been significant customization of a third-party platform (such as Salesforce
   or Microsoft Dynamics) to support clients?
3. Do you lack usage data on how clients are interacting with your systems?
4. Are you in an industry that has potential data compliance or laws to be adhered to
   (e.g., are you storing medical or financial information about or for your clients)?
5. Is security treated as a necessary evil inside the company, with much sharing of passwords?
6. Are you running and maintaining your own client-facing servers?
7. Do you lack a sandbox or safe environment for testing and trying new software or
   configurations?
8. Do you have a small team of junior or inexperienced technical staff with no technical
   leadership?
9. Is the notion of software patching or upgrading foreign and hasn't been done in years?
10. Do you lack documentation or asset/version management for the systems powering client
    production?
11. Do you have no backup or real-time disaster recovery policy?
12. Do only a handful of key personnel know how the systems operate, meaning that without
    them, the company would be in trouble in a catastrophic failure?
13. Does support handle problems related to poor or outdated software?
14. Are current systems beginning to show their age and not able to service the features
    clients are demanding?

### The $10,000 lesson (Words of wisdom: "They won't thank you")

Williamson, then an architect redesigning a platform for a portfolio company, made "the
rookie mistake of thinking about today and forgetting about tomorrow." One of his selling
points was that the redesign **saved the company $10,000 a year** — a lot of money at the
time. There was a small risk the component might fail.

His mentor Jim Milbery, founding partner of ParkerGale Capital, replied that although the
saving was laudable, when things go wrong, try explaining to the board "but I saved the
company $10,000" while they are bleeding customer goodwill.

The lesson: a CTO's decisions have farther-reaching consequences than simply running a
platform. Weigh a saving against the goodwill it puts at risk, not against the budget line.

## Key Takeaways

- A CTO looks after the current *and* long-term future of the platform; assume both vision
  and execution are yours.
- Early-lifecycle CTO = hands-on, do-everything. Growth CTO = management/architecture,
  delegating and looking outward. Established-company CTO = shoring up technology for growth
  and scalability while fixing legacy problems.
- The first 100 days define your reputation for leadership and thoughtfulness — spend them
  on discovery and your own in-depth due diligence, not on rash changes.
- The top-10 qualities are almost all about making best use of the team around you: "many
  hands can make light work."
- This should not be the only book on your shelf — keep one or two general leadership books
  alongside it.
