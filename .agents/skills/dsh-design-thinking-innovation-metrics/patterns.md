# Patterns — Design Thinking and Innovation Metrics

The book's reusable moves, each as **When / How / Trade-offs**. Grouped by the book's own parts:
the 101s (design thinking and measuring), the 201 (performance measurements), and the 301
(data-driven innovation).

---

## Part I — Foundational Moves

### Split the metric question by EXPLORE vs EXPLOIT before anything else
**When** — at the very start of any metric conversation, and again whenever a portfolio, team, or
initiative changes phase.
**How** — classify the activity: is it creativity, experimentation, and new capability building
(EXPLORE, high uncertainty, the jungle), or efficiency, cost optimization, and output (EXPLOIT,
low uncertainty, the superhighway)? Then pull from the matching metric family — minimum viable
exploration metrics or the appropriate mix of exploitation metrics.
**Trade-offs** — running two metric worlds costs governance and creates translation friction at
the handover. The alternative costs more: "metrics based on the one-size-fits-all pattern for
EXPLORE and EXPLOIT often do not achieve the desired goal."

### Choose the measurement level deliberately
**When** — before selecting any metric.
**How** — pick from individual, team, organization/culture, design challenge/project, portfolio,
business ecosystem, transition to scale. The team-and-culture levels measure human behavior,
mindsets, and capabilities; the project-and-business levels measure from tool-level activity up
to portfolio balance.
**Trade-offs** — the book explicitly rejects a full stakeholder × function matrix as
over-engineering; but choosing loosely means measuring the wrong thing well.

### Call it X
**When** — the object of measurement is ill-defined: purpose, creativity, employee experience,
mindset, future skills adoption, brand image.
**How** — refuse the loaded word. Substitute "X" as a placeholder and force the team to define
what real quantity X stands for, with no room for interpretation. Interrogate: what real-world
behavior is it associated with? What does that behavior look like and when does it happen? Who is
involved?
**Trade-offs** — slows the conversation and can feel pedantic; prevents inheriting assumptions
baked into an existing term.

### Run the sanity check on every metric
**When** — reviewing any inherited or proposed metric.
**How** — ask what the team would do differently if the figure went up, went down, or stayed the
same. If there is no answer, set the metric aside and look for a better one.
**Trade-offs** — kills metrics that stakeholders are attached to; that is the point.

### Classify the evidence behind a metric
**When** — auditing a historically grown measurement system.
**How** — sort each metric into no evidence / irrelevant evidence / unreliable evidence /
evidence-based. Keep only the last category, or upgrade the others.
**Trade-offs** — several long-standing measures will not survive; expect political resistance.

### Pair a lagging outcome with leading steps
**When** — the outcome you care about only reveals itself at the end of a long cycle.
**How** — name the lagging indicator (e.g. design thinking macro cycle length), then decompose it
into leading indicators you can move now (e.g. average design thinking process step length,
customer interview velocity). Look across the micro steps for bottlenecks.
**Trade-offs** — more instruments to maintain; the payoff is corrective action inside the cycle
rather than after it.

## Part II — Design Thinking Practice

### Apply the Double Diamond asymmetrically
**When** — planning any design challenge.
**How** — draw and staff the problem space wider than the solution space. Target the 80% of time
proficient teams spend understanding the customer problem, against the observed reality of up to
70% on ideation and sometimes under 5% on the first two quarters.
**Trade-offs** — the organization will read this as slowness. Counter with the cost-of-change
table: `$` in understanding versus `$$$+` in implementation.

### Declare the behavior
**When** — continuously, in workshops and standups.
**How** — say out loud whether the team is hunting (divergent, seeking the opportunity) or
transporting (converging, bringing it home). Facilitators support the declared behavior rather
than pushing their own.
**Trade-offs** — feels ritualistic at first; prevents the most common team dysfunction — half the
room diverging while the other half converges.

### Write the point of view before ideating
**When** — at the hinge between problem and solution space.
**How** — fill `[persona] needs [what] in order to [job-to-be-done] because [insight]`, then
reframe as an HMW question. Prioritize PoVs by impact and urgency.
**Trade-offs** — a badly written PoV locks in a bad frame for the whole solution space; write
several and prioritize.

### Score every artifact on desirability, feasibility, viability
**When** — at each iteration of the micro and macro cycle.
**How** — plot ideas, experiments, and solutions on the three dimensions. Early on, weight
desirability; as the solution matures, weight feasibility and viability. If working in only two
dimensions, use the *size* of the idea as the desirability indicator.
**Trade-offs** — three-dimensional scoring is slower than dot-voting; it is the only defensible
basis for the later investment gate.

### Track experiments with I × E confidence
**When** — after every experiment or prototype test.
**How** — record critical assumptions, then per assumption: confirmed or rejected, insights (I,
1–3), strength of evidence (E, 1–3). Compute C = I × E (1–9). Decide **love it / change it /
leave it**. Compare the computed confidence with the team's gut feel — the gap is itself signal.
**Trade-offs** — Likert scoring is subjective; consistency across a team matters more than
absolute accuracy.

### Gate investment on a probability-of-success threshold
**When** — before releasing budget for final prototypes, MVPs, or major investment.
**How** — express expected results as an XYZ hypothesis ("At least X% of target market Y will
Z"). An experiment succeeds when actual results at least match expected. Update the probability
of success via Bayes after each experiment across comfort, stretch, risk, and dark zones. Require
a pre-defined minimum probability before funds are released.
**Trade-offs** — requires discipline in defining the threshold *before* results are known;
otherwise the threshold moves to fit the desired answer.

### Build capability with pilot teams, then coaches
**When** — starting a design thinking capability programme.
**How** — begin with ≤5 pilot teams on 4–12 month design challenges. Convert some of the workforce
into internal coaches early in their own training. Scale to 20–30 teams. Accompany with town halls
and leadership boards. Keep training plus project-based learning at or under 40% of total project
cost.
**Trade-offs** — internal coaches are less expert than external specialists; they multiply reach
far faster and cost far less.

## Part III — Building the Measurement System

### Design the measurement system with design thinking
**When** — building or overhauling an IMS.
**How** — treat the system itself as a wicked problem. Iterate build → measure → learn on the
metrics. Move through learning zone → action zone → impact zone. Let S-curve inflection points
trigger the next design cycle.
**Trade-offs** — slower than adopting a template; templates rarely fit culture, maturity, or
ambition.

### Start with four to five minimum viable exploration metrics
**When** — first measurement of any EXPLORE activity.
**How** — name them (or call them X), state the problem they tackle, who they help, and what
activities could follow. Express each as a simple, understandable question the team answers on a
short cadence. Measure, evaluate, adapt, then operationalize the survivors.
**Trade-offs** — some will have short lifespans and some will prove useless; that attrition is
designed in, not a failure.

### Express metrics as team questions, not dashboards
**When** — introducing a new exploration metric.
**How** — "Did the team conduct at least five needfinding interviews this week? Yes or no? How
many?" / "Did the customer interviews generate any insights this week? How useful on a scale of
1 to 10?"
**Trade-offs** — questions feel less rigorous than numbers; they get answered, and answers get
acted on.

### Aggregate weekly signal into sprint-rolling metrics
**When** — a weekly metric swings because different Double Diamond phases have different profiles.
**How** — roll week-over-week data into a two-week-sprint rolling metric so phase change does not
read as performance change.
**Trade-offs** — loses week-level responsiveness; buys interpretability.

### Define kill metrics at the start of the challenge
**When** — launching any Moonshot-ambition design challenge or ecosystem initiative.
**How** — write measurable termination criteria in the canonical form "If X hasn't happened by
[date], we'll shut it down." Cover early customer feedback, willingness to pay, prototype cost
against budget, quality/quantity of critical functions, and technology readiness. Give the
persevere/pivot/kill decision to the team.
**Trade-offs** — a badly chosen threshold kills a good initiative early; the counterfactual is a
year and millions spent proving what a small experiment could have shown.

### Separate idea ROI from team success
**When** — a promising initiative is failing.
**How** — use metrics like insight velocity that measure the *team's* learning independently of
the *idea's* outcome. Reward the team for proving an initiative will not work. Do not link OKR
achievement directly to bonuses.
**Trade-offs** — requires a reward system that genuinely tolerates stopping; without it the
pattern is theatre.

### Decompose the North Star into two layers of input metrics
**When** — the top-line metric is a lagging indicator (which it usually is).
**How** — layer A = output/North Star metric. Layer B = directly related input metrics based on
type of business (breadth, depth, or frequency of engagement). Layer C = a second layer of input
metrics from exploration and exploitation that can be optimized now. Trace the causal chain right
to left.
**Trade-offs** — three layers is more model than many organizations will maintain; two is the
minimum that permits action.

### Shift EXPLOIT KPIs toward health metrics
**When** — reviewing an existing KPI set, especially in turbulent conditions.
**How** — for each KPI ask whether a health metric would drive better action. Keep only 5–7. Make
them weekly. Ensure the owner can directly influence the number. Link each to the North Star. Set
a *threshold of concern* rather than a target, baselined on the last 12 months. Review them in
weekly team prioritization sessions; automate the critical ones.
**Trade-offs** — thresholds invite "we're fine" complacency between reviews; targets invite
gaming. The book prefers thresholds.

### Pre-wire downturn triggers
**When** — building the exploitation mix, before a downturn.
**How** — fill three scenarios — forecast versus monthly average, cash runway in months, and named
trigger events (e.g. revenue within X% of OPEX) — each with the actions that follow and the
estimated annual savings and additional revenues.
**Trade-offs** — scenario work ages; revisit at least annually.

## Part IV — Objectives and Alignment

### Align for impact instead of cascading
**When** — setting objectives across levels.
**How** — publish the North Star and strategic guardrails from the top; let teams determine where
*they* can have the greatest impact on priorities and write their own objectives. Target at least
50% bottom-up. Make every objective publicly visible.
**Trade-offs** — slower than cutting and pasting goals downward; the pasted version produces
compliance, not impact.

### Mix ambition levels one to two
**When** — composing a quarter's objective set.
**How** — roughly one Moonshot to two Roofshots. In fast-changing environments substitute an
Everest for the Moonshot. Score them differently: Moonshot success at 60–70%, Roofshot at 100%,
Everest at something new to customer and market.
**Trade-offs** — all-Moonshot sets destroy credibility; all-Roofshot sets remove stretch.

### Calibrate ambition with a shared metaphor
**When** — a team cannot feel the difference between ambition levels.
**How** — use a concrete competition (the book uses the America's Cup): participating = 100%
achievable, top three = a range, world record = very aspirational. Attach a rough time horizon to
each (one year / two years / more than three years) and name the milestones that sustain the
journey.
**Trade-offs** — metaphors carry cultural baggage; pick one the whole team recognizes.

### Write key results with the four-part sentence
**When** — drafting any key result.
**How** — "At [end date], we know the objective is achieved because [outcome]. The underlying
metrics are based on [what happens regarding progress]. The key result makes the objective
measurable based on [how]." Two to five per objective. Measurable and specific. Never a task, a
to-do, or a classic KPI.
**Trade-offs** — verbose compared with a bare number; the verbosity is what stops task lists
masquerading as key results.

### Run envision → focus → create → define → mobilize → (re)define
**When** — facilitating an OKR cycle for innovation teams.
**How** — envision with strategic foresight ("What if…" for Moonshot, "Imagine that…" for Everest,
"How might we…" for Roofshot); focus on three to five objectives; create by breaking into three or
four missions for the next 12 months; define success criteria (brainstorm → cluster → select top
three per mission) then key results; mobilize skills and communicate; redefine at check-in.
**Trade-offs** — experienced teams compress this into one workshop; first-timers need several.

### Spend check-in time on low confidence
**When** — the regular OKR check-in.
**How** — reflect briefly on all OKRs; go from rough to detailed as in a retreat; concentrate time
on the OKRs with the lowest confidence level; end with an action plan. Work the four steps:
current progress → predicted score → roadblocks/constraints → concrete adjustments.
**Trade-offs** — high-confidence OKRs get less air time and can drift; the trade favours the
initiatives actually at risk.

### Prioritize by urgent × important and name what stops
**When** — new objectives arrive without new capacity.
**How** — place missions in *do it now*, strategy and objectives in *plan it*, and explicitly fill
*delegate it* ("What can be delegated to free up time and resources?") and *drop it* ("What is not
important anymore?").
**Trade-offs** — forces uncomfortable conversations about work people are attached to; "innovation
work also means stopping things."

## Part V — Data, AI, and Neuro

### Use design thinking to ask the data question, and data to answer the human one
**When** — any hybrid-model engagement.
**How** — design thinking finds and frames the WHY and supplies the powerful questions
(Question-Analyze-Builder before model building); data analytics grounds WHAT customers actually
do. Map data-mining techniques onto the Double Diamond: options/processes/physiological conditions
in the problem space, clustering and visualization at PoV, generative models in the solution
space, feedback and dynamics at test.
**Trade-offs** — mixed teams are harder to run — "collaboration between design thinkers and data
scientists is not only profitable, but often very challenging for both groups" — but "mixed teams
ultimately achieve better results."

### Let metrics emerge from the data
**When** — the IMS has reached IMS 2 and predictive analytics is available.
**How** — identify the problem first, then create *new metrics from the data* rather than defining
or adapting metrics from assumptions. Feed the data-originated metrics back into analysis for the
EXPLORE or EXPLOIT effect you want.
**Trade-offs** — data-originated metrics can be uninterpretable to the teams who must act on them;
pair with the "easy to understand" principle.

### Democratize the analytics tools
**When** — rolling out any predictive measurement.
**How** — transparency and remove silos → allow self-service access to analytic tools → make data
simple and accessible → create data-originated metrics.
**Trade-offs** — governance and data-quality risk rise with access; unequal access risks
misalignment, dissatisfaction, and cultural division, which the book treats as the larger danger.

### Run the Data Ethics Canvas across the whole cycle
**When** — from problem identification through scaling, not at the end.
**How** — principles (purpose, values, laws, core principles) → assign cycle (sources and rights,
limitations, sharing, minimizing negative impact) → implementation (actions, engagement,
enhancement, impact of data). Cover privacy, accountability, security & safety, explainability &
transparency, non-discrimination & fairness, human control of technology, professional
responsibility.
**Trade-offs** — adds process overhead; it is the precondition for evidence-based innovation work
and for customer trust.

### Design the AI experience, not the AI features
**When** — ideating AI/ML solutions.
**How** — start from persona, JTBD, and the AS-IS customer journey; map where AI adds real value;
formulate "If [data] is integrated/analyzed, it can be predicted/indicated that [hypothesis]";
prototype first *without* real data by acting out the experience, then with data and tools.
Prioritize on feasibility × value to the customer/company.
**Trade-offs** — acted-out prototypes cannot measure model performance; they surface experience
problems far cheaper than a trained model would.

### Replace the annual engagement survey with active ONA
**When** — measuring culture, engagement, and change readiness.
**How** — move from the passive annual survey to AI-enabled network analysis that identifies
influential change agents, high-performing team behaviors, real subject matter experts, and
burnout risk continuously.
**Trade-offs** — continuous behavioral analysis raises privacy and consent questions; run it
through the Data Ethics Canvas.

### Add neuro measures where declarative answers fail
**When** — testing a high-resolution prototype, or measuring empathy, creativity, and team
collaboration.
**How** — run the Neurodesign Project Canvas: state what you know from memory and what you expect,
name the critical schema/interaction/touchpoint, select tools (hyperscanning, heart rate, eye
tracking; add EEG, biometrics, face coding, self-report), set up the experiment, capture and
visualize, then state limitations honestly and report how the patterns feed onward into
classification and AI.
**Trade-offs** — lab complexity limits ecological validity; single tools each add modest
resolution and only the combined toolbox produces the step change. Neuroscience cannot read minds.

### Protect incubation time
**When** — a team is stuck in the Groan Zone or on a hard challenge.
**How** — deliberately relieve the prefrontal cortex with a low-concentration activity — a nap,
walk, or jog — so the brain continues divergent thinking subconsciously and changes activation
patterns.
**Trade-offs** — reads as slack in efficiency-driven cultures; it is the cheapest divergence
intervention available.
