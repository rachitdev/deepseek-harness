# Chapter 6 — The Design Thinking Toolkit

*(printed pp. 103–144)*

## Core Idea

A curated tool set — "the entire design thinking toolbox comprises more than 500 methods and
tools" — organised along the **problem-to-scale-and-growth framework**, with *examples and ideas
for quantification and measurement at the tool level included throughout*. The chapter's
governing claim: **design thinking cannot be measured as a single concept.** Tool-level measures
"are to be understood as supporting components for reducing risks" and "are very often the
starting point for creating a larger innovation measurement system."

The most quoted statistic in the chapter:

> Proficient design thinking and innovation teams ideally spend **80 percent of their time**
> trying to better understand the customer problem (problem space). But observations across all
> industries and sizes show many teams spend the lion's share (**up to 70 percent**) of
> activities and time on ideation without having understood the customer's problem, and
> "sometimes spend **less than 5%** of their available time on the first two quarters of the
> double diamond."

Hence: the Double Diamond "should be presented and applied in an **asymmetric form**" —
*solving the right problem* wider than *solving the problem right*.

## Frameworks Introduced

### The five fits (the toolkit's spine)

| # | Lens | Application of tools | Central question | Fit achieved |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Design Thinking (problem space)** | Understanding customer/user needs | What is the customer/user problem? | **Problem/Customer Fit** |
| 2 | **Design Thinking (solution space)** | Developing prototypes for potential solutions | Which solution fits best? | **Problem/Solution Fit** |
| 3 | **Lean Start-Up** | Build, measure, and learn with MVP(s); documentation of the business model and related experiments | How much is the customer willing to pay for it? What are the features, benefits, and value? | **Product/Market Fit** |
| 4 | **Ecosystem Design** | Design and validation of value proposition, actors, value streams, business model to realize MVE (if required) | Where to play and how to win? How can the ecosystem best be configured? | **Systems/Actors Fit** |
| 5 | **Scale** | Transition from EXPLORE to EXPLOIT to realize growth; process efficiency, scalability, network effects | How can efficiency be increased and business expansion realized? | **Ecosystem or Product/Co-Evolution Fit** |

### Tools by fit

1. **Problem/Customer Fit** — Define Success · Problem Statement · Empathy Map · Jobs to Be Done ·
   Personas/User Profiles · Critical Items Diagram · Point of View
2. **Problem/Solution Fit** — Scoring Ideas · Tracking Experiments / Prototypes Tracking Canvas ·
   Probability of Innovation Success · Prototype Question Checklist · Design Thinking Canvas
3. **Product/Market Fit** — Innovation Accounting · Lean Canvas
4. **Systems/Actors Fit** — Ecosystem Capital · Ecosystem Design Canvas
5. **Scale** — Scale & Growth Canvas

## Key Concepts (tool by tool, with the book's own metrics)

### Define Success
Defines success at various levels; may be anchored in the design brief, derived from design
principles, or defined by the team. Timing varies — "most teams start before the project," but
it can be done after the first observation of the problem space or close to implementation.
**Procedure:** (1) ideate all elements that may define success, then narrow to the critical ones;
(2) ask what success means for the potential customer, for the company, and what the value of
the project may be for the organization; (3) converge — cluster key areas, then vote;
(4) **review the criteria for success several times as the project progresses**, since internal
or external requirements may change.

### Problem Statement
Template: `How might we (re)design ______ (WHAT) for ______ (WHOM), so that ______ (THEIR NEED)
is satisfied?`
Formulated from the **WH questions**: WHO is involved? · WHAT do we know already about the
problem? · WHEN did the problem start? · WHERE does the problem occur? · WHY is the problem
important? · HOW could the problem be an opportunity?
It is "a baseline for measuring — for example, confidence level — and outlining critical
assumptions."

### Measuring Confidence Level and Critical Assumptions
Two complementary indicators for the relevance of a problem statement.
**Confidence-level check-in questions** (each on a Low / Not sure / High scale):
1. How confident are we with the targeted user profile (persona)?
2. How confident are we about the deeper needs of the user/customer addressed in the problem statement?
3. How confident are we about the estimated size of the market (total and addressable)?
4. How confident are we with any limiting factors from the design principles?

**Critical assumptions** are categorized on a `CRITICAL / NOT CRITICAL` × `KNOWN / UNKNOWN` grid,
asking "what is the team assuming with regard to customer behavior (desirability), monetization
(viability), and technology (feasibility)?"

Metrics stated:
- **Initial confidence level of team versus confidence level in every micro design thinking
  cycle** — indicates ambiguity at the start; compares confidence with prototypes and experiments
  performed until the final solution. "The metrics also help the team to overcome the *groan
  zone*, switching from diverging to converging."
- **# of critical and unknown assumptions identified** — indicates how much uncertainty is
  related at project start and "helps to estimate the time and resources needed to validate
  assumptions."
- **% of change from unknown to known in each iteration** — "indicates the continued process of
  knowledge gain along the activities."

### Empathy Map
Four questions: **SAY** (quotes and defining words) · **DO** (actions and behaviors) ·
**THINK** (what might they be thinking; what does this tell you about their beliefs) ·
**FEEL** (what emotions might they be feeling). Flows into Persona and JTBD.

**Measuring activities related to customer/user interactions:**
1. **Time between respective observations and deep interviews of customers/users** — collect for
   typical innovation projects, then take action to reduce the time between interactions.
2. **Number of customer/user interactions over the entire macro or micro cycle** — "the ultimate
   goal is to keep the number of interactions constant throughout the entire design cycle (i.e.,
   from need-finding to solution testing)."
3. **A lagging indicator on the variety of interactions with customers/users** — compares the
   persona used against the actual customers/users; "allows you to analyze in retrospect to which
   kinds of customers and needs an interaction has taken place over the whole design cycle."

**How to measure empathy** — the book is explicit that this is hard: "measuring empathy is
problematic because meaningful comparisons about how to define and measure empathy are lacking."
Research in social cognitive neuroscience suggests four neural networks must be activated for a
complete experience of empathy, and these "should be the basis for future measurement":
1. **Effective sharing** — subjective reflection by the team on another person's observable experience
2. **Self-awareness** — differentiation between the team's experiences and the customer's
3. **Mental flexibility** — the ability to imagine what it would be like to experience the world from the customer's position
4. **Emotion regulation** — the team's ability to regulate the volume of their own feelings as they arise from mirroring the customer experience

### Jobs to Be Done (JTBD)
Template: `When ______ (Situation), I want to ______ (Motivation), so I can ______ (Expected result).`
JTBDs can contain both emotional and functional aspects.
**Two-dimensional measurement:** (a) how *important* is the task to the customer/user;
(b) how *satisfied* is the customer with existing solutions. "It has proven useful to survey the
importance for the customer/user on a **Likert scale**." Satisfaction with existing solutions is
surveyed the same way.
**Desired-outcome statements** give predictability to a market opportunity; characteristics:
solution-free · stable over time · measurable · controllable · tied to a process or task the
customer is trying to complete.

### Personas / User Profiles
**Typical elements:** location · gender · age · education level · hobbies/interests · income ·
marital status · occupation · channels · motivation · pains · gains · JTBD.
**Typical criteria for comparing personas:**
- *Motivational* — achievements, personal growth, efficiency, convenience
- *Personality* — introvert vs extrovert; thinking vs feeling; sensing vs intuition; judging vs perceiving; loyal vs fickle; passive vs active
- *Customized, based on the problem statement* — e.g. confidence in digital, level of independence, planning horizon, buying behavior, preferred channels (multi-, opti-, omnichannel)

**Measuring the perceived value of potential solutions** — three questions:
- **Feature value:** what features and aspects do potential customers care most about? least about?
- **Quantified value propositions:** what value messaging drives them to sticking to a specific proposition?
- **Price sensitivity and willingness to pay:** how much are customers willing to pay to solve their problem?

### Critical Items Diagram
Procedure: (1) ask "what is crucial for a successful solution for the customer/user in the
context of the problem?"; (2) sketch the diagram; (3) brainstorm critical elements on Post-its;
(4) **select and name four experiences and four functions, one of which focuses on completely
new or future expectations**; (5) agree the final **eight critical items**. Items are placed
"by applying the concept of the daisy flower. Each petal has the same importance as the others,"
with "happiness" at the centre.
**Metric:** *Importance of future functions and experiences for user* — the extent to which
individual critical items develop over time in relevance and time-related importance, plotted
`Year 0` vs `Year 0 + 5`.

### Point of View
Reframing of the design challenge into an actionable problem statement. Four steps:
`1. Insights → 2. Clustering → 3. Drill down into the design challenge (build HMW questions on
different PoVs) → 4. Prioritization (which PoVs and HMW questions have biggest impact and
highest urgency)`.
Extended template: `How might we redesign ______ (WHAT) for ______ (WHOM), so that ______ (HIS
NEED) is satisfied, by considering ______ (INSIGHT / REQUIREMENT / LIMITATION)?`
**How to create powerful HMW questions:** adjust the original problem statement with focused HMW
questions · present the identified challenge as a problem statement · use the HMW question to
evaluate competing ideas · phrase the question so it is actionable and tangible · remember HMW
questions guide the innovation effort and inspire the team · expand into *"What would happen
if…"* or *"What is stopping us from…"* once the team is comfortable.

### Scoring Ideas
Score every idea, experiment, and solution on the three design thinking lens dimensions. "In the
early design phases the team usually concentrates mainly on desirability; the question of
feasibility and viability becomes more important as the solution matures."
**Tip from the book:** "If you operate in only two dimensions, it is recommended to use the
**size of the idea as the indicator for desirability**." And: "a good starting point is to
compare new approaches with already existing solutions."
Explicit prohibition: **"it is not advisable to measure the number of ideas in this context, as
it is not a useful measure."**

### Probability of Innovation Success (Bayes' theorem)
Bayes' theorem — "a classic of probability theory … a calculation rule used to calculate the
(conditional) probability of an event" — determines a potential solution's likely success from
the data of previously conducted experiments on the exploration map.
- **An experiment is a success if the actual results at least correspond to the expected results.**
- Expected results are formulated as the **XYZ hypothesis**: *"At least X% of target market Y
  will Z"*, where Z describes how that percentage of the target market will react to the
  solution idea.
- Experiments run in the **comfort, stretch, risk, and dark zones**. After each experiment, the
  learning progress is evaluated by an updated calculation of the probability of success.
- **The gate:** "The objective is to achieve a predefined minimum value for the probability of
  success … **This threshold must be reached before investments for the realization of the final
  prototypes, MVPs, or any other relevant investments can be released** to the design thinking
  and innovation team."

### Exploration Grid for Market Opportunities
Axes: **expected return** (financial potential) × **degree of evidence**. Four quadrants:

| Quadrant | Financial potential | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Big market opportunity** | High | Strong evidence of success |
| **Promising market opportunity** | High | Weak evidence of success |
| **Small market opportunity without risks** | Low | Strong evidence for success |
| **Niche market opportunity** | Low | Weak evidence of success |

Rule: **"do not include any potential solution with no evidence."**
Indicators: *ratio of big, promising, niche, and small market opportunities in the exploration
portfolio* (indicates innovation risk of the exploration portfolio); *budgets for teams gathering
evidence over the entire design cycle* (input for the expected return of investments in teams to
provide evidence on desirability, feasibility, viability).
Alternative framing: core / adjacent / transformational, used with the "where to play" and "how
to win" framework.

### Prototype Question Checklist
Example questions: What immediate or short-range gains or results can be anticipated? · How
simple or complex will the idea's execution or implementation be? · What might be potential
roadblocks from key stakeholders in the organization? · How soon could the solution be put into
operation? · Is the solution legal? · What is its environmental impact? · Will it have any
negative effect? · Who might be a current or potential competitor? · Who will buy it first
(early adopters)? · How much will it cost to get the solution to market? · How can ESG and
impact be balanced in the best possible way?

> "Design thinking includes exploring the problem space, prototyping an idea, and implementing
> the solution to achieve innovation success. But **without impact, there is no innovation.**"

**ESG Prioritization Risk & Opportunity Matrix** — axes `ESG priority (low→high)` ×
`motivation to benefit all stakeholders`, moving from *internal operational focus and harm
reduction* to *external market opportunities focus*:
- Top-right: dual goals with core focus to design products and services to solve a systemic
  societal challenge, and to optimize EXPLOIT/EXPLORE
- High ESG priority, internal focus: primary goal to avoid direct harm or minimize risk from
  business and innovation activities; financial materiality is the core focus
- Low ESG priority: products and services designed to meet market need, but without any
  consideration of systemic societal challenge

Narrow the metric list through a **double materiality assessment** — soliciting feedback on the
issues and metrics with both the greatest business value and the greatest relevance to
stakeholders, and then taking action.

### Innovation Accounting and AARRR
**AARRR (Dave McClure):** Acquisition · Activation · Retention · Revenue · Referral.
Sample questions: (1) How do customers/users find us? (2) Do the customers have a first positive
experience? (3) Are the customers/users coming back? (4) How does the offer or function make
money? (5) Do the customers/users recommend the offer to other people?

| Stage | Typical AARRR measurements |
|---|---|
| Acquisition | Number of unique visitors per channel |
| Activation | Time to value; customer aha moments; conversion rates |
| Retention | Retention versus churn rate; customer churn; retention length |
| Referral | Referred customers; viral coefficient; viral cycle time |
| Revenue | Revenue churn; lifetime value; acquisition costs; average order value; conversion from free to paying customers |

**Footnote the book prints:** *"AARRR metrics depend on business model, industry, and business
ecosystem configuration."* Related simplified frameworks: **RARRA** and **ATAR** — all "applying
a very simplified set of possible metrics that should be extended with minimum viable exploration
metrics depending on the business environment and the business model."
Early revenue simulations are performed and measured with **driver trees**.

### Willingness-to-Pay (WTP) Analysis
Pricing models named: cost-plus pricing · comparison pricing · willingness-to-pay analysis.
Method: **discrete choice analysis**, either from actual purchase data or by asking customers to
choose among alternatives with different attribute sets. "In most cases, the potential customers
are not able to articulate the value they place on a feature directly; what they can do is
compare two alternatives in an A/B test and express their preference. Given a sufficiently large
number of votes, this method (**conjoint analysis**) can be an implicit evaluation of each
feature by the customers."
Then: "create a **market demand curve** that shows how many customers will buy at a given price;
from this, the **market price elasticity** can be calculated."

### MVP Portfolio Planning (Ansoff Matrix, adapted)

| | EXISTING OFFERS | NEW OFFERS |
|---|---|---|
| **Existing markets** | Usually no MVP needed for the realization of a unique value proposition | Innovative offer development (diversification) |
| **New markets** | New customer development (first-mover advantage) | Innovative customer development |

"The focus in design thinking and lean start-up is on **innovative customer development (new
markets / new offers)**." The book's caution: "In the mindset of integrated and rather
traditionally managed companies, innovative customer development is seen as risky because there
is often little room for the exploitation of existing know-how or for achieving economies of
scale."
**Portfolio grid mechanics:** projects entered on the grid, progress reflected **every four
weeks or at shorter intervals**; **bubble size per initiative indicates the value added** (NPV,
IRR, revenue, or other figures); four phases `DISCOVERY → PROTOTYPE/DEVELOP → DELIVERY → MVP`
(roughly: discovery and delivery); arrows indicate shifts to the next phase or a return to
discovery "since the first usability test yielded new insights."

### Ecosystem Capital
"An important metric for business ecosystem initiators and orchestrators … used to quantify the
value of network connections, configuration, and value streams." Traditional companies "have a
high proportion of physical and human capital, which is sufficient for linear growth but limits
the achievement of greater margins."
**Typical elements to consider in measuring ecosystem capital:**
- Formal established and orchestrated relationships between actors in the system
- Established value streams and benefits for all actors in the system
- Exploitation of network effects and scale effects of actors in the system
- Adaptability of actors in the system to cope with the dynamic environment
- Alignment of the frequency of customer interaction, share of wallet, and retention to the system
- Configuration of the ecosystem with regard to openness, transparency, and market roles
- Application of new and game-changing technology and use of big data analytics for agile customer development and innovations

### Scale
Two focal points beyond feasibility: **rhythmicability** (network effects, repetitions) and
**captivateability** (number, intensity) of interactions in the business ecosystem. "These both
must increase and at the same time reduce the unit cost of each interaction/transaction in the
system." Exponential thinking means levers "greater than 5, 8, and 10 of the current value" —
the term **10x** is often used.

## Anti-patterns

- **Ideation without problem understanding** — up to 70% of time on ideation, sometimes <5% on
  the first two quarters of the double diamond. "Design thinking generates only output but no
  results and impact."
- **Counting ideas** in the solution space.
- **Shortcutting design thinking straight into lean start-up.** "Teams try to take a shortcut
  and omit the important design thinking activities before the lean start-up phase."
- **Absolute revenue numbers too early.** "Attempts are often made in the lean start-up phase to
  deal with potential revenue results in absolute numbers, which are based on predictions that
  cannot be verified at the start of the product/market fit." Instead: indicate a **range** of
  possible revenues.
- **Traditional metrics for ecosystem initialization.** "Traditional metrics such as return on
  investment, profitability, cash burn rate, and revenue are of little help in terms of
  initialization, as these metrics are all **lagging indicators**."
- **Solutions with no evidence** placed on the exploration grid.

## Reference Tables

### Analysis vs Synthesis
| | Analysis | Synthesis |
|---|---|---|
| **Definition** | Fragment a complex customer problem into micro sections to acquire more comprehension | Collect the micro sections and connect them together to acquire a broader view of the problem |
| **Correlation** | Without synthesis, analysis will not get justified | Without analysis, synthesis will not take place |
| **Key feature** | Research, observation, theories of customer behavior and needs | Practical experiments and outcomes that are new and freshly available |
| **Impact** | Easy to understand and execute | Complex to understand and execute |
| **Dependency** | An independent process | Dependent on analysis |
| **Process steps** | Thinking, observing, then trying | Applying, experimenting, then studying and reflecting on outcomes |
| **Benefits** | Detailed investigation of specific topics | Abstract overview of the whole topic |
| **Types** | Qualitative, quantitative, predictive, descriptive, prescriptive, diagnostic | Sample-based, vector, frequency modulation, granular, wave table, additive, subtractive, physical modeling, spectral, West Coast |

### Examples of indicators related to design thinking (problem space)
Confidence level of the team regarding problem worth solving · overlapping characteristics,
needs, behaviors in a set of personas · number of new insights collected per deep interview ·
scoring of ideas on desirability, feasibility, viability · number of critical and unknown
assumptions identified · percentage of change from unknown to known in each iteration ·
number of interactions across the organization to collaborate · reduced cost from start to
implementation · design thinking maturity level of team/organization · hard financial facts,
outcomes, and impact on brand value and customer satisfaction.

### Examples of indicators related to lean start-up
Determining if a new product, service, or experience is creating value for the customer/user ·
measuring if customers/users are returning · quantifying how much a customer/user is willing to
pay · potential **range** of revenues of a new offering · total investment from problem
identification to product/market fit.

### Examples of indicators related to business ecosystem design
Volume of real options in market opportunities versus relevant opportunities · number and
engagement level of marquee business ecosystem actors contributing to the core value proposition ·
actual number realization of ecosystem capital in X-time · satisfaction of ecosystem
partners/actors with expansion of the core value proposition over time · customer satisfaction
with the value proposition delivered jointly by the actors in the system.

### Examples of indicators related to growth, scale, and EXPLOIT
Number of customer adoption in X-time versus market size · customer stickiness to an offering
versus all customers in X-time · solutions sold to new customer segments versus all segments ·
unit cost per transaction versus IT investments to scale.

## Worked Example

### The Experiments / Prototypes Tracking Canvas — with the book's confidence-level arithmetic

The canvas columns as printed: `NAME OF EXPERIMENT/PROTOTYPE TESTED` · `MAIN OBJECTIVE OF THE
EXPERIMENT/PROTOTYPE TESTING` · then per row: **CRITICAL ASSUMPTIONS** → **LEARNINGS**
(Confirmed or rejected? · Insights *(I)* · Strength of the evidence *(E)*) → **SCORE**
(Calculated Confidence Level) → **ACTIONS** (Love it, leave it, or change it?).

**The calculation the book specifies:**

```
Insights (I)            Likert 1 = weak … 3 = strong
Strength of evidence (E) Likert 1 = weak … 3 = strong
Confidence level  C = I × E      max. 9,  min. 1
```

**The three phases of running it:**
- *Before the experiment:* reflect on critical assumptions
- *Planning the experiment:* decide what to test and how
- *After the experiment:* validate insights, check the strength of evidence, calculate the
  confidence level

Then **compare the calculated confidence level with the general feeling of the team** about the
prototypes tested / experiments conducted — the gap between the number and the gut feel is
itself the signal.

**The three actions:**
| Action | When the book says to take it |
|---|---|
| **Love it** | "Typically when the hypothesis is confirmed, and the design thinking team is very confident about the strength of the evidence." |
| **Change it** | "A typical pivot when the design thinking team has gathered enough evidence to change parts of a prototype and to test it again afterwards." |
| **Leave it** | "This radical move is not an easy decision for design thinking teams. However, if there are multiple pieces of supporting evidence that disprove the hypothesis, the prototype will not make sense in most cases." |

**Two metrics on top of the canvas:**
- # of experiments/prototypes which contribute to significant learning outcomes related to
  "love it, change it, or leave it"
- # of experiments/prototypes that were independently initialized, planned, and executed by the
  design thinking team

### The TikTok case — a game-changing JTBD detected by combining design thinking and data analytics

The chapter's named case study for the power of JTBD, timeline **2015 → 2018**:

| Step | What happened |
|---|---|
| Starting point (2015) | **Musical.ly** is an app that focuses on adding background music and videos |
| External trigger | The TV show **Lip Sync Battle** becomes famous on Thursday nights |
| Observed signal | **Downloads of the Musical.ly app increase after the TV show** — evidence based |
| The "why more downloads?" analysis | Fans search in App Stores for **"lip sync app"** after the show. Musical.ly shows up in the top search result, which generates massive downloads |
| New customer needs identified | Customer/user needs for **lip sync** and the JTBD for **cool video features** |
| Outcome (2018) | **TikTok** — "a new value proposition based on user motivation, advanced features, and JTBD leads to better ad hoc videos." TikTok "has become the number-one downloaded app by understanding new customers' needs, motivations, and behavior." |

This is the book's illustration of the **hybrid model** (design thinking + data analytics, see
Ch. 17): the download spike is the *data insight*; the lip-sync JTBD is the *human insight*; the
new value proposition is what the combination produces.

| Band | Fields |
|---|---|

| **Problem space** | **Problem statement** (Where is the problem?) · **Empathy map** (What are the deeper needs of the customers? What drives them? | **Solution space** | **Ideation** (How could the problem be solved? How do others solve it? What if…?) · **Prototype & test** (What critical functions and experiences can be built and tested? How can the solution be implemented and tested in a lo-fi prototype? How can the prototype be improved with the lessons learned?) · **Final prototype** (Is there a problem/solution fit? The Lean Canvas fields as printed: `WHO (customers) WANTS (product, service…what) FOR

Revenue Stream.

## Key Takeaways

1. Apply the Double Diamond **asymmetrically** — the problem space deserves 80% of the effort,
   not 5%.
2. Every tool carries its own tool-level measurement; these accumulate into the IMS.
3. Confidence level = Insights × Evidence, max 9 — the single most reusable calculation in the
   toolkit.
4. Use love it / change it / leave it as the standing action set after every experiment.
5. Set a probability-of-success threshold (Bayes + XYZ hypothesis) as the investment gate before
   final prototypes and MVPs.
6. Never place a solution with no evidence on the exploration grid; never quote absolute early
   revenues — quote a range.
7. Traditional lagging metrics (ROI, profitability, cash burn, revenue) are useless for ecosystem
   initialization; use ecosystem capital instead.
