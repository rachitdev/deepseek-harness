# Chapter 1 — Why Design Thinking & Innovation Metrics Are Important

*(Motivation, The Roots of Design Thinking, The History of Innovation Metrics, Why/How/What — printed pp. 18–44)*

## Core Idea

Measurement of creativity, innovation, and business success is still done badly: organizations
invent "nice-sounding KPIs" to justify exploration budgets, while executives on the other side
repeat "What you can't measure, you can't manage." Lewrick's position is that both camps fail
for the same reason — they apply **one measurement logic across two fundamentally different
activities**. EXPLORE (creativity, experimentation, new capabilities, new organizational
structures) and EXPLOIT (efficiency, cost optimization, output, ROI against business plans)
need different metrics, different mindsets, and different cadences.

> "There is a need for a sensible approach toward metrics so that measurements do not take on
> a life of their own and become instruments of justification, defense, and power, rather than
> fulfilling their intended value of self-reflective direction setting."

The second claim is behavioral: people optimize for whatever they are measured on. "If the
measurement doesn't make sense, you can't expect the behaviors of each individual and the team
to be logical, either." When targets are missed, leaders blame the employees rather than the
measurement system that produced the behavior. Most such failures happen because
**the organization measures efficiency but actually wants to know about effectiveness.**

## Frameworks Introduced

### The EXPLORE / EXPLOIT split
The book's organizing division. Every metric decision starts by asking which side you are on.

| | EXPLORE | EXPLOIT |
|---|---|---|
| Focus | Creativity, experimentation, new capabilities and structures | Best possible efficiency, cost optimization, increased output |
| Uncertainty | High | Low |
| Budget purpose | Developing disruption | Developing the core |
| Business model | Reinventing | Sustaining |
| Objective ambition | Moonshot / Everest OKRs | Roofshot OKRs |
| Metric family | Minimum viable set of **exploration metrics** | Appropriate mix of relevant **exploitation metrics** |

> "Metrics based on the one-size-fits-all pattern for EXPLORE and EXPLOIT often do not achieve
> the desired goal."

### Building Blocks of the Innovation Measurement System (IMS)
The book splits the metrics subject into four building blocks:

1. **North Star metric(s)**
2. **Minimum viable set of exploration metrics**
3. **Appropriate set of exploitation metrics**
4. **Performance measurements (OKRs)**

### Levels for Defining Metrics
Metrics are defined at six ascending levels — the illustration that anchors the whole book:

| Level | EXPLORE side examples | EXPLOIT side examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Individual** | Aptitude for collaboration and entrepreneurship; openness for change and mindset-shifts | Individual contribution to the team; aptitude for efficiency and automation; openness for exponential growth |
| **Team** | Diversity and interdisciplinarity; progress of capability and skill building; progress and team-of-teams collaboration | Standardization and specialization; efficiency gains and reorganization |
| **Organization / Culture** | Diversity and tolerance to failure; balance between readiness for change and fear; trainings for future skills; Moonshot and Everest OKRs | Roofshot OKRs; culture of continuous improvement; balance between agility and discipline |
| **Design Challenge / Project (Alignment)** | Problem/solution fit and product/market fit; test and validation of assumptions; viability of product, service, offering | Systematic improvement; forecasts and detailed plans; growth targets or efficiency gains |
| **Portfolio** | High uncertainty; budget/resource for developing disruption; reinventing business models | Low uncertainty; budget for developing the core; sustaining business models |
| **Business Ecosystems / Scale** | System/actors fit; minimum viability of ecosystem (MVE); activities of customers and ecosystem actors; ecosystem/co-evolution fit; optimized value streams; multidimensional business models | Network effects and exponential growth; digital/physical/hybrid touchpoints; scalable processes, IT, data analytics; ecosystem capital |

### The Problem to Growth and Scale Framework
The book's spine (front-matter illustration). Level of uncertainty falls left to right:

`PROBLEM → SOLUTION → TEST & VALIDATE → LAUNCH & EXECUTE → GROWTH`
with `PROBLEM/SOLUTION FIT` and `PRODUCT/MARKET FIT` as the two gates,
and `BUILD → MEASURE → LEARN` (lean start-up, MVP) running between them.

- **High uncertainty** (design thinking, research) → *Apply minimum viable set of exploration metrics*
- **Medium uncertainty** (co-creation, lean start-up) → *Apply appropriate mix of relevant exploitation metrics*
- **Low uncertainty** (business ecosystem design & agile customer development) → *Create, adjust, and apply overall innovation measurement system*

MVP = minimum viable product; MVE = minimum viable ecosystem.

## Key Concepts

- **Vanity metrics** — indicators that "feel good, are great for marketing, and are easy to
  visualize." In large organizations they are often called *activity metrics*, used "to
  demonstrate how busy the teams are and to justify the innovation theater." They indicate
  current impact and neglect future benefit, and give no clear indication of concrete actions.
- **The four typical vanity metrics to avoid**: number of ideas; number of patents; R&D
  spending; number of employees in R&D.
- **Health metrics** — an EXPLOIT alternative to classic KPIs. "As the name implies, health
  metrics describe a business's vital signs, much like physical vital signs." Especially useful
  in turbulent times because they show how resources and time are being used.
- **OMTM (one metric that matters)** — typically applied by start-ups in the early incubation
  phase, and by intrapreneur units inside established companies.
- **AKIs (Aspirations and Key Insights)** — a newer naming for measurement systems that
  emphasises the aspiration side of OKRs for innovation teams, grounded in customer insights.
- **Value creation / value delivery / value capture** — "become more important than optimizing
  numbers." Which one matters depends on growth phase and maturity of the initiative.
- **Lagging vs leading, financial vs nonfinancial** — "not all metrics are the same, and their
  nature can range from lagging to leading, and financial to nonfinancial."

## Mental Models

- **The jungle and the superhighway** (developed in Ch. 2): EXPLORE is jungle, EXPLOIT is a
  well-lit superhighway with cruise control. You need both; be mindful which metrics you apply
  and when.
- **Measurement as direction setting, not justification.** The test of a metric is whether it
  supports "decisions that result in guidance, actions, and transparency."
- **Prudent risk-taking.** "A state-of-the-art innovation measurement system should allow
  prudent risk-taking, embedded in a culture and mindset that is tolerant of risk and failure."
- **Two objectives in every engagement** (from 20 years of practice): building new capabilities
  (learning) *and* delivering high-impact outcomes — i.e. project-based learning.

## Anti-patterns

- **Inventing KPIs to defend budgets.** Nice-sounding KPIs invented to justify exploration
  spend become instruments of power, not direction.
- **Applying traditional KPIs before exploration starts.** "Applying traditional KPIs in every
  step, before even starting to explore and test new market opportunities, is destroying"
  the exploration work.
- **Counting ideas.** The book's recurring joke: *"How many new ideas did we come up with this
  year?" → "ONE. A new way of counting ideas."*
- **Agile teams given solutions, not problems.** Teams required to develop solutions from
  supplied ideas, with no chance to explore the problem space first, lose sight of the ultimate
  purpose for customers.
- **Blaming people instead of the measurement system** when badly designed objectives are missed.
- **Vague OKRs with no alignment to a North Star** — they lead to wrong actions.

## Reference Tables

### Recommended Procedure (the book's five-step opening move)
| # | Step | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Explain the purpose and goals | Is the focus of defining and measuring EXPLORE or EXPLOIT? |
| 2 | Be clear about what is measured | Measure at the appropriate level: individual, team, culture, project, portfolio, ecosystem/scale |
| 3 | Work with minimum viable exploration metrics before fixing metrics in a rigid system | Apply the cycle of measuring, evaluating, operationalizing |
| 4 | Find alignment between business and teams | Very vague OKRs without alignment to a North Star lead to wrong actions |
| 5 | Measure at different levels | Even an initial measurement at the *tool* level yields first insights and actions |

### Evolution of Design Thinking (timeline, 1960 → 2040)
| Era | Focus |
|---|---|
| Early days | (Re)design of products, services, processes, business models, experiences |
| ~2000–2020 | Enable digital platforms, business ecosystems, new organizational behavior, user experiences |
| Future | Immersive experiences, convergence with AI, automation, mass customization; mindset for the most wicked problems of mankind |

### Evolution of Innovation Metrics (timeline, 1940 → 2040)
| Era | Focus |
|---|---|
| Early days | R&D expenditures, capital, tech intensity, patents, number of publications, number of new products |
| Today | Outcomes, processes, portfolios, risk/return, clusters, network effects, design thinking and systems thinking capabilities, team performance |
| Future | System dynamics, collaboration, future capabilities, supported by AI, big data analytics, emerging measurements about communities and ecosystem capital |

### Common EXPLOIT quantifiable metrics named in this chapter
- % of successfully met financial ambitions (success ratio)
- % of sales from new products in the past *n* years
- Return on investment in design thinking and innovation activities
- % of profits from new users, customers, or categories
- Customer satisfaction / net promoter score
- Metrics on IT scalability and real-time data analytics performance

## Worked Example

### The Symbiosis of Design Thinking and Measurements — the four diagnostic questions

The chapter presents a diagnostic (printed p. 34) that a practitioner runs against their own
organization before selecting a single metric. Reproduced from the illustration:

1. **Mindset:** *Does the mindset of your organization consider the pains, gains, and jobs to be
   done for your customers?*
2. **Organizational setup:** *Does the organizational setup allow radical collaboration and a
   balanced portfolio of EXPLORE and EXPLOIT?*
3. **Metric/system fit:** *Does the metric and the measurement system of your organization fit
   your customer?*
4. **Action:** *Does it support deriving actions and making better decisions?*

If the answer to (1) or (2) is no, the book's guidance is to fix capability and structure
before metric design — this is Ch. 2's tip #1, "It is hard to measure what hasn't happened."
If (3) or (4) is no, you are looking at a vanity metric and should apply the "love it, change
it, or leave it" rule from Ch. 2.

### The history in one line
Innovation measurement originated in the **1960s** with international standards for measuring
company R&D. **1970s–1980s**: attempts to overcome linear innovation models; new theories of
economics of industrial innovation. Then a revival of **Schumpeter's** theories founded a new
economics of innovation, and innovation *output* measurement (a new business model, product,
or process) became the priority. With digital transformation, frameworks treating innovation
and investment in intangible assets as two highly correlated phenomena became established —
covering technology, digitization, environmental and social sustainability, customer experience
and branding, internal innovation networks, purpose, and external innovation ecosystems.

### The design thinking lineage (as the book states it)
Stanford's design-oriented programs began in the **1950s**. **Prof. Larry Leifer** — one of the
first postgraduate students — founded the **Center for Design Research (CDR)** in **1984** and
has led the ME310 course since the late 1980s (**55th year in 2022**), taught for almost 20
years in partnership with universities and clients worldwide. **David Kelley** (founder of IDEO
and a former PhD student of Leifer who took ME310 in the 1970s) founded the **d.school in
2005**. CDR research focuses on effectiveness of collaboration, teamwork, and lately
neurodesign. Frameworks derived from this lineage include the Business Model Canvas and Value
Proposition Design.

## Key Takeaways

1. Split the metric question by EXPLORE vs EXPLOIT before anything else; one-size-fits-all
   fails both.
2. Four building blocks: North Star metric(s), minimum viable exploration metrics, appropriate
   exploitation metrics, performance measurements (OKRs).
3. Choose the *level* deliberately — individual, team, organization/culture, project, portfolio,
   ecosystem — because the metric differs at each.
4. Kill the four classic vanity metrics: number of ideas, number of patents, R&D spending,
   headcount in R&D.
5. Consider health metrics instead of classic KPIs on the EXPLOIT side, especially in turbulent
   times.
6. Measuring efficiency when you want effectiveness is the most common root cause of failure.
