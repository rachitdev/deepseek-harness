# Chapter 16 — The OKR Toolkit

*(printed pp. 285–311)*

## Core Idea

Six phases — **Envision → Focus → Create → Define → Mobilize → (Re)define** — each with its own
canvases and techniques, run "via divergent and convergent thinking, which are particularly
suitable for innovation."

The workshop reality check: **"Qualified teams with experience in defining objectives can usually
realize OKR definition in a single workshop. Teams working with objectives for the first time
usually need several interactions and workshops until the team objectives with the respective
first missions to be worked on are ready for concrete actions."**

The guiding purpose: use design thinking methods "in order to work on the appropriate problems and
**not to jump directly into the implementation of predefined solutions and existing ideas**."

## Frameworks Introduced

### The Six Phases

| Phase | What it does | Key questions |
|---|---|---|
| **Envision** | "Synthesize a shared view of the long term directed to the organization and the teams" | What are the plausible futures of your industry, business model, or domain? How does the future look in 5, 10, or 15 years? What are the new, next, and future social, technological, economic, environmental, and political trends? |
| **Focus** | "Specify strategic priorities for which a potential team has the opportunity for the greatest impact" | What kind of objectives are needed to achieve the priorities (Moonshot, Roofshot, or Everest)? Which objectives at the corporate, team, or individual level bring the greatest benefit? |
| **Create** | "Break initiatives down into missions to accelerate the path toward that long-term destination over the next **12 months**" | Which of these objectives align with the organization's key priorities? Which mission must be started now to secure the envisioned future? |
| **Define** | "Define key results for objectives or cluster and define success for each mission" | How is success defined for each mission? What are the design principles for the missions? Who should work on which mission? How much time and effort are needed? Which kind of capabilities are needed? What are potential constraints? |
| **Mobilize** | "Ensure there is a critical mass of resources aligned with the key missions and the objectives are communicated transparently" | Is there an action plan with clear milestones and key results defined? Are all stakeholders, team members, and supporting entities informed about the mission, objectives, and key results? |
| **(Re)define** | "Adapt or redefine objectives to new factors, key results performance, and/or other conditions, if necessary" | — |

### Tool inventory by phase
1. **Envision the future: strategic foresight** — What If? · Imagine That · How Might We? · Envision OKR Canvas
2. **Focus: be mindful about purpose, priorities, and the greatest benefits** — Objectives Styles: Moonshot versus Everest · Team Collaboration Canvas
3. **Create: team and individual objectives** — Moonshot/Everest Canvas for Innovation Teams · Prioritization of Assignments and Activities
4. **Define: cluster and success** — How to Draft Key Results · OKRs Documentation Template
5. **Mobilize: reflect on skills & capabilities** — Cultivate Curiosity & Experimentation · Mobilize & Communication Canvas
6. **(Re)define: measure, discuss, & (re)prioritize** — (Re)define OKRs Canvas · Ways of Working & Cultural Reflection

### Strategic Foresight
"Strategic foresight is looking **10+ years** into the future and asking, *'What will our
customers value? What kind of products and services will our customers need, and how can we best
serve them?'* These questions are different from the ones we usually ask in the context of **two
to three years** of strategic planning: *'How can we do this a little better, faster, or more
efficiently? How can we improve or expand upon what we're currently providing?'*"

*Strategic foresight helps the team:* gain a better understanding of future customer behavior ·
make the right technology and architecture decisions · create future scenarios that are plausible
and relevant · explore different types of disruption (positive and negative with respect to the
status quo) · scan for signals of change, identify patterns · **look back to see forward**.

**Guidance to talk about the future** (the seven prompts): *What you (for)see is what you get* ·
*Take a different perspective* (analyze the past to be able to shape the future actively) ·
*Think in opportunities* · *Find solutions that matter* · *Create powerful interdisciplinary
teams* · *Communicate the vision (North Star)* · *Actively shape the future*.

### The Three Envisioning Techniques mapped to ambition

| Technique | Character | Ambition it envisions |
|---|---|---|
| **"What if…"** | Game changing · future focused · big impact | **Moonshot** |
| **"Imagine that…"** | Based on current and future customer needs · explorative thinking | **Everest** |
| **"How might we…"** | Innovative completion of mission · forward thinking · incremental impact | **Roofshot** |

**"What if…" helps the team:** to provoke bigger and out-of-the-box thinking · to anticipate
possible opportunities, risks, and bets on the future · to span the world and open up all
conceivable possibilities · to explore options that lie away from day-to-day operations.
*Procedure:* create an initial list of "What ifs" to bring to the workshop · ask "What are three
'what if' questions we could ask in the current business situation?" · establish criteria for how
outcomes will be prioritized (e.g. impact on customer/user, time to fix) · collect additional
questions or **aim for boldness by asking "What would we do next if we had no limitations?"** ·
constantly reflect which questions will deliver the most value · determine what it will take to
execute the envisioned future.

**"Imagine that…" helps the team:** to focus on core aspects of the desired future · to converge
from the status quo · to create actions for the first initiatives · to feel real with the
envisioned future · to provoke bigger thinking · to share an image of the future with the wider
team. *Procedure:* imagine the world from multiple perspectives · apply reframing exercises or
take the perspectives of customers, colleagues, competitors, stakeholders · apply symbolic and
visual communications · allow creative, subjective, and emotional alternatives to the analytical
logic. The chapter quotes Einstein: *"Imagination is more important than knowledge. Knowledge is
limited. Imagination encircles the world."*

**"How might we…" helps the team:** to set aside prescriptive beliefs · to explore a variety of
endeavors · **not to jump directly into solution** · to team up with members with different
thinking preferences and T-shaped profiles. Used together with **5 × WHY and 5 × HOW**.

### Objectives Styles: Moonshot versus Everest

| | **A Moonshot objective motivates teams…** | **An Everest objective motivates teams…** |
|---|---|---|
| | to complete first missions of longer transition (e.g. more than three years) with big impact on the world | to work better and more closely together, as an Everest objective is rarely achieved alone |
| | to get inspired to create extraordinary solutions | to develop a deeper inner commitment to an initiative |
| | to work on a future with a reasonable chance of success | to make a high commitment and go the extra mile; to embark on a new path of learning |
| | to be part of a meaningful break from the past | to pioneer with a new product or service in the market (analogous to a first ascent of a mountain) |
| | to tap into new and emerging technologies | to redefine the deeper purpose of work and values |

**Named Moonshot examples:** self-driving cars · the realization of the Internet from space · CO2
neutrality · cell-gene therapy with new success-based business models in medicine.
Moonshot objectives "are mostly based on the **future-back approach**… So it is not scenario
planning in the narrow sense, but as designed for big impact."
On Everest: "the goal is closer and already tangible, yet not easy to achieve. It takes courage
for an Everest objective in **'expedition style.'**"

> "**Failure is a part of success**, for both Moonshot and Everest objectives, if the team strives
> to achieve remarkable results."

### Key results vs KPIs — the distinction the toolkit insists on
> "Key results have a purpose and answer the question of whether the team/individual made
> **measurable progress on a specific objective by a defined date**. A KPI, on the other hand, can
> be anything and any metric is often mistakenly considered a KPI. In summary, key results are
> defined in the context of an objective, **originated by the team or individuals**, are visible
> to all employees, aim for alignment across teams and toward the North Star, and inform
> predominantly **near and mid-term** focus and prioritization."

### The Key Result mental model (the four-question sentence)

```
At ______________________ (What end date?),
we know that the objective is achieved because ______________________ (What outcome?).
The underlying metrics are based on ______________________ (What happens regarding the progress?).
The key result makes the objective measurable based on ______________________
                                                       (How to make the objective measurable?).
```

"Most of the key results should be **leading indicators to measure progress** and **lagging
indicators to guide the broader execution**." And: "for teams new to design thinking, adding the
**declared behavior** provides additional guidance to the broader team."

### The Alignment / Autonomy Matrix
Axes: **high ↔ low common alignment** (defined North Star and communicated vision ↔ fragmented
vision and no North Star) × **high ↔ low autonomy of teams and individuals**.
- Low autonomy + high alignment → **micro management**
- High autonomy + low alignment → **chaos**

> "If the organization tends to act chaotically, it is often due to the **lack of a North Star**."
> "Good strategies create a path for action based on a high common alignment. **A strategy without
> a plan of action is inherently incomplete.**"

**The two regular reflection questions:**
- How do individual team members and management feel in the environment of the adapted performance
  management system?
- Have ingrained behaviors been discarded?

## Key Concepts

- **The objective count rule.** "As a general rule of thumb, **each team should have no more than
  three to five objectives at any one time.**"
- **Quarterly review as the switch mechanism.** "The quarterly review of each OKR set enables a
  switch to more valuable goals when changes occur, such as those caused by crises, new insights
  from interaction with potential customers/users, due to measurements and applied minimum viable
  exploration metrics, or just market shifts."
- **All objectives public.** "All objectives should be public to all peers — everyone has access
  to everyone else's objectives."
- **No individual objectives for innovation work.** The Create-phase diagram carries the explicit
  footnote: *"Concerning the actual innovation work, it is recommended not to agree on individual
  objectives."*
- **Skills are somewhere.** "Skills are usually somewhere in the organization or with external
  service providers. For example, if it is needed to understand the customer better, ethnographic
  research… or insights based on neuroscience must be included."
- **The learning maxim printed in the Mobilize section:** *"There is no such thing as a mistake.
  There is no such thing as winning or losing. There is only doing it!"*
- **Strategy-problem link.** "Strategies that are directly linked to solving a problem have the
  best chance of performing."

## Mental Models

- **Thinking long term is a state of mind.** "When the entire organization is focused on a North
  Star, it has a long-term mindset, **thinking in terms of years, not project weeks or shareholder
  quarters**… everyone in the organization can accept that change will take time."
- **"What if…" and "Imagine that…" aim at what could be, rather than using today's perceptions as
  roadblocks.**
- **Innovation work also means stopping things or delegating things.**
- **OKRs are not set in stone.** "They can change during finalization, dissemination, and regular
  check-ins with the teams. Experienced teams usually deal with this uncertainty and convergence
  gracefully."
- **Sign it together.** "It helps if all team members sign their agreed team OKR template together
  at the end. **Set it, sign it, and start collaborating!**"

## Anti-patterns

- **Objectives that are operational task lists** — the Redefine phase exists partly "to reflect if
  the key result will really inspire team to take the appropriate actions, or whether it
  represents an operational task list."
- **Chaos from missing alignment**, or micro-management from missing autonomy.
- **Year-end rating framing.** "The cultural change needs to bring a new understanding of the fact
  that the objectives were **not introduced to perform a rating at the end of the year**, and that
  a universal KPI is the measure for this."

## Reference Tables

### Six guiding principles for envisioning
| # | Principle |
|---|---|
| 1 | Shift the approach from improving the current offering to **solving problems that matter** for the customer/user |
| 2 | Apply **strategic foresight (> 10 years)** and try to anticipate the new and future customer behaviors and needs |
| 3 | Use **re-framing** to redefine the nature of competition using "What if…" and "Imagine that…" |
| 4 | **Change the perspective and questions of all critical assumptions** with regard to potential solutions and customer needs |
| 5 | Focus on a **North Star that is ambitious, achievable, measurable, and within the limits** of the organization or different teams |
| 6 | Introduce a new worldview to ensure that **myopic beliefs and expert opinions do not interfere with judgment** |

### Team & personal checklist for objectives and key results
| **Objectives** | **Key results** |
|---|---|
| Aligned to North Star and strategic choice | The objectives are achievable |
| Directional to a defined objective | Measurable |
| Reachable, ambitious, or very bold | **Not a classic KPI** |
| Time bound | **Not a task or to-do** |
| Do not have too many | Do not have too many |

### The Prioritization Matrix (urgent × important)
| | **Urgent** | **Not urgent** |
|---|---|---|
| **Important** | **DO IT NOW** — 1st mission, 2nd mission, 3rd mission (results based on set cycle) | **PLAN IT** — strategy, team objectives, individual objectives (start to align OKRs) |
| **Unimportant** | **DELEGATE IT** — "What can be delegated to free up time and resources?" | **DROP IT** — "What is not important anymore?" |

### The "What if…" question bank (as printed)
- What if ______ legislation is sanctioned?
- What if ______ customer/user flaws?
- What if ______ happens to our customers/users?
- What if ______ technology emerges?
- What if we do or don't (grow / enter / leave) in a market?
- What if we do or don't make ______ investment in ______?
- What if a competitor does ______?
- What if we miss ______?
- What if the demand for (product/service) exceeds supply?
- What if we lose (supplier(s) / partner(s) / ecosystem actor(s))?
- What if our customers / the market adopt(s) / choose(s) ______?

### "How might we…" templates
```
Example 1:
How might we (re)design ______________ (context: what?)
for ______________ (for whom?)
so that ______________ (need of customer/user) is satisfied.

Example 2:
How might we accomplish ______________ (objective / mission)
by utilizing ______________ (e.g., existing capabilities)
to accomplish ______________ (What?) by ______________ (Date?)
```

### Defining Success — the five-step procedure
1. Decide if success should be defined **on the objective or on a concrete mission**
2. **Brainstorm** success criteria with the team
3. **Cluster** the success criteria into major topics or points of view based on the customer,
   team, or project perspective
4. **Select the top three** success criteria, for example, per mission
5. Apply the criteria as basis for further development of short-term key results

### Team Collaboration Agreement — the roles to assign
- Who is the **OKR representative** in our team?
- What is the team's **check-in / review cadence**?
- Who is the team's **key results maintainer** (responsible for maintaining the progress
  measurement)?
- Who from the team **attends the check-in**?
- How will the team address the daily business and ways of collaborating?
- How will the team **celebrate achievements and lessons learned** after the check-ins?

### The check-in procedure — four steps
| Step | Question |
|---|---|
| **1. Current progress of the team** | How did the team do on recent activities to drive the key result? |
| **2. Predicted score based on agreed scoring system** | What is the current score, and what is the confidence level of the team to achieve the result? |
| **3. Potential roadblocks / constraints** | What is preventing progress? Which problems are solvable, and which facts have to be accepted? |
| **4. Concrete adjustments, measurements, and action plans** | What needs to be adjusted? What are the next steps? Who can help? |

Facilitation advice: "go from the rough to the detailed, as in a retreat, and create an action
plan at the end… all OKRs should be reflected on briefly. In general, it is recommended to
**spend more time on those with a low confidence level.**"

## Worked Example

### The Alphabet / Google OKR case with the book's own figures

> "No organization has more collective experience implementing OKRs than Alphabet. Since **1999**,
> the company has impressively demonstrated how exponential growth can be realized through the
> appropriate culture and measurement systems."

**The timeline the chapter prints:**

| Year | State |
|---|---|
| **1999** | **40 employees applied OKRs at Google for the first time** |
| **2022** | **140,000 employees of Alphabet still applying OKRs in combination with other innovation metrics** |

**The four practices, as the book states them:**

| Practice | What Alphabet does |
|---|---|
| **Inspiring** | "Every OKR is drafted as a sophisticated stretch goal for **Everest and Moonshot objectives**" |
| **Providing feedback** | "OKRs are drafted by considering **feedback from peers acting as OKR facilitators** and who are **visible to the entire workforce**" |
| **Creating impact** | "The successful achievement of an objective must provide **clear value**" |
| **Collaborating** | "Goals are created **60 percent from the bottom up**, and the autonomous teams are **synced vertically and horizontally**" |
| **Focusing** | "Each quarter, **a maximum of five objectives with four verifiable key results each** is practical to stay focused" |

Larry Page, quoted: *"OKRs have helped lead us to 10x growth, many times over. They've kept me and
the rest of the company on time and on track when it mattered the most."*

### The OKRs Documentation Template — every field

| Field | Question |
|---|---|
| **Vision / North Star** | What is the long-term vision (**three to five years**)? How will you define the one metric that matters most to the North Star? |
| **Name of the team** | — |
| **Type & scoring** | For example: Moonshot, Roofshot, Everest |
| **Strategic goals / prioritized missions for the initiative** | Why are we doing what we do as a team? What is the overall purpose and potential impact? |
| **Objectives** (Objective 1, 2, 3) | What does the team want to do/achieve? |
| **Key results** (three per objective) | How would the team like to measure progress? Each carries: **Owner · Start value · End value** |
| **Note** | Descriptions, labels, questions, important stakeholders, benefiting (parent) objective, resources, skills needed |

### The Envision OKR Canvas — filled-in structure

| Field | Content |
|---|---|
| **Envision North Star** | "State and describe the long-term vision or high-level company-wide North Star (for example based on one metric that matters most)" |
| **Strategic guidelines and scope for action for the team** | "Describe any boundaries and defined scope of actions for the organization" |
| **Row: "What if…" questions** | → Long-term objectives (aspirational qualitative statement) → **Why? / How?** (ask why/how multiple times to explore and adjust the problem space) → **Leading/lagging measures** (brainstorm and describe first high-level activities/milestones and/or outcomes/impact in the context of the objective and purpose) |
| **Row: "Imagine that…" statements** | → Long-term objectives → Why?/How? → Leading/lagging measures |
| **Row: "How might we…" questions** | → **Long-/mid-term** objectives → Why?/How? → Leading/lagging measures |
| **Envision (footer)** | "What is, for example, the bold and ambitious **more-than-three-year** Moonshot or Everest objective? How is it linked to the overall vision and North Star of the company or organization?" |

### The Moonshot/Everest Canvas for Innovation Teams

| Column | Instruction |
|---|---|
| **Prioritized missions** | "Describe and prioritize **three or four missions** the team is aiming to complete in the **first 12 months**" |
| **Tasks related to missions** | "Describe potential tasks which relate to missions, and **question whether the skills and capabilities exist within the team/organization**" |
| **Other missions to be completed** | "Describe other missions to be completed over the timeline and discuss **dependencies and importance**" |

The cascade it feeds, drawn from *now* to *long term*: `STRATEGIC CHOICE → OBJECTIVES → TEAMS
(individual*) → KEY RESULTS → MISSION → TASKS`, with the footnote reminding that individual
objectives are not recommended for the actual innovation work.

### The Mobilize & Communication Canvas

| Field | Instruction |
|---|---|
| **Share and communicate the short-, mid-, and long-term objectives** | What is the team expecting to achieve? |
| **Share and communicate the expected outcomes** | What outcome is expected from the team? |
| **Explain the problem statement / aspirational statement** | "Explain WHY it is worth working as a team on the objectives" |
| **Outline critical assumptions** | "Which assumptions are critical and have the highest priority to complete in the mission? Make transparent how frequent the progress of verifying measured assumptions is" |
| **Make key results transparent** | "Define with the team and communicate after each check-in which leading and lagging indicators will be used to determine if the mission is progressing toward the outcome" |
| **Show value for customer/user** | "Connect the dots and show the link between customers/users, the key value measures, and the overall outcome" |
| **Explain costs and availability of budgets** | "Calculate the expected investment to deliver the outcome. **Divide the cost between CAPEX and OPEX**" |
| **Highlight funding mechanism** | "Make explicit who the sponsor of the initiative is and where the funding is coming from. **Make clear from the beginning if the funding provided is based on the achievement of missions or is for achieving the long-term and bold objective**" |

### The Team Collaboration Canvas — six numbered blocks
1. **T-shaped participants & thinking preferences** — What T-shaped profiles are available? How
   are thinking preferences distributed? What additional skills are needed?
2. **Team goals & objectives** — What do we want to achieve as a high-performing team? What are
   our mid- and long-term team objectives? What are good time-bound measurements and metrics?
3. **Purpose** (centre) — Why are we doing what we do as a team? What is the overall purpose and
   potential impact?
4. **Individual goals & objectives** *(if applicable)* — How are the individual objectives aligned
   to the team objectives? Which individual goals are in conflict with the team objectives?
5. **Principles and action points** — What are the design principles? How to communicate? How are
   decisions created? How are execution and measurement organized? How are lessons learned,
   weaknesses, and development areas addressed?
6. **Team values** — What is the team standing for? What are common values? What values are of
   paramount importance for reaching the team objectives? · **Individual expectations** — What
   does each team member need to be successful? What are the expectations regarding collaboration?

## Key Takeaways

1. Six phases: envision, focus, create, define, mobilize, (re)define — diverge then converge.
2. Match the technique to the ambition: "What if…" → Moonshot, "Imagine that…" → Everest,
   "How might we…" → Roofshot.
3. Strategic foresight looks 10+ years out — a different question set from 2–3 year planning.
4. Three to five objectives per team at a time; Alphabet caps at five objectives × four key
   results per quarter.
5. Key results ≠ KPIs: team-originated, objective-bound, date-bound, publicly visible.
6. Use the four-part key-result sentence (end date / outcome / underlying metrics / measurability).
7. Prioritize with urgent × important, and explicitly name what gets delegated or dropped.
8. Spend check-in time on the low-confidence OKRs.
