# Chapter 3 — Design Thinking 101: The Design Thinking Paradigm

*(printed pp. 57–73)*

## Core Idea

Design thinking is **a mindset, not an innovation method**: "finding the appropriate problem as
a starting point for developing solutions that matter for customers/users." The chapter's
warning to metric designers is that agile alone is not enough — "too often, design thinking is
assumed to be part of the agile toolbox, and companies realize too late that their teams need
additional skills in exploring the problem space."

The book's illustrated joke makes the point: *"The agile methodology is now installed!" …
9 months later … "Unfortunately, it won't work until your teams understand the customer
problems they have to solve and apply a design thinking mindset."*

The credo: **begin at the beginning.**

## Frameworks Introduced

### The Design Thinking Mindset (Stanford d.school, as presented here)

| Element | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Focus on human values** | Build empathy for the customer/user or stakeholder the team designs for. Explore needs, pains, gains, and jobs to be done, and obtain feedback. |
| **Show, do not tell** | Communicate the vision and prototypes meaningfully by creating an experience, applying illustrative visuals, and telling a powerful, inspiring story. |
| **Craft clarity** | Develop a coherent vision out of a wicked problem. Frame it to inspire the team and fuel ideation. |
| **Bias towards action** | Move the idea forward with the team. Maintain a bias toward doing and making over thinking and meeting. |
| **Embrace experimentation** | Prototyping is not just validation — it is integral. Ideation, prototypes, and testing are one entity for thinking and learning about a market opportunity. |
| **Radical collaboration** | Create teams with different T-shapes, thinking preferences, backgrounds, points of view. Breakthrough solutions emerge from diversity and ad hoc collaboration. |
| **Be mindful of the process** | Know and communicate where in the process the team is right now. Decide about the appropriate tools and methods. Remember what the objectives are. |

Added for the digital / business-growth era: **acceptance of complexity** and **application of
variable thinking states**.

### The Design Thinking Micro Process (Double Diamond)

`UNDERSTAND → OBSERVE → DEFINE POINT OF VIEW → IDEATE → PROTOTYPE → TEST` with **REFLECT**
as a constant companion. Understand+Observe form one entity (problem space); Ideate+Prototype+
Test form another (solution space). Usually started with a problem statement or an already
formulated **"How might we…?" (HMW)** question.

| Phase | What happens | Metric named in the chapter |
|---|---|---|
| Understand | Learn about the potential user, their wishes, tasks to fulfil; sharpen the creative framework; use *why* and *how* questions to open or narrow scope | "Professional design thinking teams measure **how confident the team is to work on the problem space**"; viable metrics tracking **constant interactions with customers/users** |
| Observe | Go where potential users are; validate persona assumptions in the real environment; ask open-ended questions, use a question map | Criteria for comparing personas "might be formulated, quantified, and measured" |
| Define Point of View | Evaluate, interpret, weigh findings; analysis and synthesis occur at the same time; structuring the analysis frames the problem, synthesis is emergent and creates connections that identify breakthrough ideas | — |
| Ideate | Brainstorming and creativity techniques (e.g. working with analogies); dot-voting; scoring ideas on desirability/feasibility/viability; business value vs urgency matrix | Assumptions of ideas are tracked with each experimental prototype |
| Prototype | Craft materials, paper, aluminium foil, strings, glue, scotch tape; from critical-experience prototypes to final prototypes | — |
| Test | Interaction with the potential user/customer, results documented; A/B testing online for large numbers | **Number of prototype iterations per feature** |
| Reflect | Constant companion throughout | — |

**The Point of View statement pattern** (the book's exact template):

```
Name of the user / persona: (who)
needs: (what is needed / desired)
in order to: (job-to-be-done / outcomes)
because: (insight / finding)
```

### The Design Thinking Macro Cycle
The micro process is repeated several times. Divergent phase: generate as many and as wild
ideas as possible, and prototypes that sharpen the vision. Converging phase: prototypes become
concrete and high fidelity; the **functional prototype** tests problem/solution fit for
individual elements before it matures into a **final prototype**; then implementation and
market launch, and — for many offerings — business ecosystem design on the basis of an MVP or
after the final prototype. Certainty rises across the macro cycle.

**The Groan Zone** is the most challenging area for most teams: selecting the features,
experiences, and solutions that make it into the high-resolution and final prototype.

### The Design Thinking Lens — Desirability / Feasibility / Viability

> "Innovation success evolves from a combination of the following: the needs of the
> customer/user (desirability), a solution that is profitable (viability), and technical
> implementability (feasibility)."

| Lens | Definition given | Questions the book prints |
|---|---|---|
| **Desirability** | Must be understood and acknowledged by the team for customer-centric innovation to be possible at all | Who has the problem? How is it solved today? Which jobs to be done are to be solved? Which pains and gains are known? Which needs and emotions are relevant? What critical functions and experiences should the solution include? How does the solution approach differ from known solutions? To what extent do customer benefits match the values of the service/product delivery? |
| **Feasibility** | "The simple reality of the possibility that a design challenge can be realized with reasonable effort and cost." For many digital solutions today, not a major challenge | What are important components for implementation? What kind of technology is needed? What skills are needed for design, construction, operation? Which systems are affected? What workflows are needed? How can "fail fast, fail often" be lived without destroying market opportunity? **How is success defined, measured, and communicated to stakeholders?** |
| **Viability** | "The long-term understanding that the project is worth the time and cost, taking into account all the effort and potential returns or benefits." At best the solution is valuable to customer *and* environment, supporting a more sustainable way of life | How are revenues generated with the solution? How can costs be saved with it? Where does added value arise for the organization, company, or a potential business ecosystem? How can the investments be justified? |

### Extract Long-Term Value — the three-engine chain

| Engine | Purpose | Distinct capabilities and metrics |
|---|---|---|
| **DESIGN THINKING** → *extract long-term value* | Understand users and potential customers | Understand pains, gains, jobs to be done, preferences, behavior, needs; establish a design-led organization moving from product-centric to personalized/customized services, products, experiences; react closer and faster to new customer behaviors and market changes |
| **AGILE** → *develop & test rapid* | Develop validated prototypes quickly and test on the market | Drive project speed and predictability with small, frequent delivery increments; establish a collaborative business and IT management framework; establish new risk management engineered directly into an agile execution life cycle |
| **EXPLOIT (lean thinking)** → *run cheap* | Efficient implementation, basis for scaling | Drive realization, knowing when to turn and when to persevere and grow a business with maximum acceleration; allow a principled approach to phases of exploiting, sustaining, and retiring portfolio elements |

> "Applying the well-established exploitation metrics for increasing operational efficiency and
> reducing waste in an effort to measure the ability of extracting long-term value will remove
> any chance of breakthrough discovery, invention, and transformation."

## Key Concepts

- **Announce the current behavior** — a design thinking principle: openly state whether the team
  is hunting for the next opportunity, or sharing insights/results/observations to define a
  point of view.
- **The Exploration Map** — documents the novelty and degree of change in every experiment over
  the macro cycle, plotted from *familiar, well-known behavior* to *new behavior required*.
  After each solution-space iteration, experiment outcomes plus a summary of test results are
  documented. It shows, at the end of the design cycle, the path the team took to the ultimate
  solution.
- **The Design Thinking Canvas** — captures the most important activities across the micro and
  macro process when full documentation is required.
- **Attitudes of the best-performing design thinking teams**: open to a world of possibilities
  and "what if…?" questions; free of preconceptions about how something will work; filled with
  curiosity to understand things more deeply; free of expectations about what will happen; open
  to failing early and often while learning quickly.
- **The 101s' purpose** — provide the basic understanding of the design thinking mindset and
  the impact of applying it, plus guidance on how to measure and create appropriate metrics and
  innovation measurement systems.
- **Data literacy for innovation** — "the continuous learning process that creates the ability
  to recognize, understand, interpret, create, communicate, and compute information." Hands-on
  interaction with data is often more valuable than repetition of statistical concepts.

## Mental Models

- **No shortcuts.** Efficiency-driven organizations take shortcuts because exploratory work
  takes time and teams must leave their comfort zone. "Solutions based on our own assumptions
  without customer validation often die quickly."
- **A team of experts is the wrong team.** "A group of experts usually tends to solve the
  problem in a known way due to their experience, which often only leads to incremental
  improvement and innovation."
- **Some metrics that look like vanity metrics still earn their place.** On number of prototype
  iterations per feature: "The measurement per feature gives the impression of a vanity metric,
  but it is still important because it enables a comparison between projects, regardless of the
  size of the project or the feature set."

## Anti-patterns

- **Starting with agile only** and discovering the missing problem-space skills nine months later.
- **Structured interview guides** — "that usually only confirms the assumptions that prevail in
  the team." Prefer open-ended questions and a question map.
- **Late pivots.** Pivoting after implementation "is in many circumstances a new innovation
  project."
- **Metrics that limit creativity** or obscure the goal of finishing design challenges and
  projects at an early phase.

## Reference Tables

### Why solutions die (the chapter's list)
Too expensive · lack of user acceptance · illegal · protected · already exists · too complex ·
technically not feasible.
**Better to search for:** behavior · need · pains · gains · job to be done · use cases.

### Typical Impact on (Re)design, Innovation Success, and Customer Satisfaction as Phases Elapse

| | UNDERSTANDING / DEFINING | PROTOTYPING | TESTING | IMPLEMENTING |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Process costs and costs for changes** | $ | $$ | $$$ | $$$ + |
| **Time required for changes** | Hours to days | Days to weeks | Weeks to months | Months to years (sometimes never) |
| **Resistance to change** | Low | Medium | Medium to high | High to very high |
| **Agility and effort to pivot** | Observing and understanding create many points of view, giving the basis for ideation and quick feedback | Depending on prototype resolution, changes might be accommodated with low-to-medium costs; resistance becomes more likely | Tests on finished prototypes operate with already defined functions and experiences; major changes are expensive and need extended approval | Pivoting is in many circumstances a new innovation project needing additional resources or major re-engineering |
| **Individual and team motivation** | A new design challenge energizes teams, awakens curiosity, inspires creativity | Enthusiastic teams retain creativity, think through problems, design radical new solutions | Many iterations and rework frustrate teams; motivation is lost if this state continues too long | Pessimism is widespread as employees resist realigning entire projects; changes are costly and time-consuming |
| **Impact on customer satisfaction and bottom line** | Discovering new or changing customer/user needs | De-risk potential of failure | Major changes in evolving and higher maturity | Market flop impacts revenues, brand image, retention, and acquisition |
| **Recommendation / mindset to avoid negative impacts** | **Problem exploration and definition are key** | **Fail fast, fail cheap** | **Test frequently and in every iteration** | **Plan implementation while hunting for opportunities** |

## Worked Example

### Writing a Point of View from an observation

The chapter gives the exact synthesis pattern that turns observe-phase findings into a
"How might we…?" question. Applied to the pattern the book prints:

```
Name of the user / persona:  (who)
needs:                       (what is needed / desired)
in order to:                 (job-to-be-done / outcomes)
because:                     (insight / finding)
```

The point of view is then reformulated as a **"How might we…?"** question, which becomes the
starting point of the IDEATE phase. Because analysis and synthesis occur simultaneously in
design thinking, the team structures the analysis to *frame* the problem while letting synthesis
stay emergent — "helping to create connections that identify breakthrough ideas and
opportunities."

### The cost-of-change argument, in the book's own currency

A change caught in **understanding/defining** costs `$` and takes *hours to days*, with *low*
resistance. The same change caught in **implementing** costs `$$$+` and takes *months to years
(sometimes never)*, with *high to very high* resistance. That single row is the chapter's whole
business case for measuring problem-space work: the metrics that matter most (customer
interactions, team confidence in the problem space, tested-vs-untested assumptions) are the
ones that pull change decisions leftward on this table.

## Key Takeaways

1. Design thinking is a mindset for finding the right problem — not a stage in the agile toolbox.
2. Understand+Observe = problem space; Ideate+Prototype+Test = solution space; Reflect runs
   throughout.
3. The POV template (who / needs / in order to / because) is the hinge between the two diamonds.
4. Desirability, feasibility, and viability are the three risk-reduction questions asked
   repeatedly across micro and macro cycles.
5. Design thinking → agile → exploit each need *distinct* capabilities and *distinct* metrics.
6. Cost, time, and resistance to change all rise steeply across the four phases — early
   problem work is the cheapest lever.
