# Chapter 5 — Measuring Impact

*(printed pp. 97–102)*

## Core Idea

The closing question of Design Thinking 101: **can the impact of design thinking be measured?**
The book's answer is unambiguous — "yes, the business impact from the combination of building
design thinking capabilities and value creation can be measured, and evidence of this exists
from various design thinking initiatives."

The impact is not one-off: "due to the increasing maturity of the capabilities in the teams and
the accelerated transformation, the potentials for the financial impact for both innovation
projects and a successful organizational transformation are enormous."

The chapter's central financial argument: **many organizations realize significant gains because
design thinking internally addresses the problem upfront, whereas without design thinking
capabilities, late changes, throwaway efforts, and rework are the norm.**

## Frameworks Introduced

### Two-level quantification of financial impact

The chapter splits the financial measurement into a **meta level** and a **phase level**:

| Level | Metric | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| **Meta** | Overall reduced cost from project start to implementation | USD |
| **Phase** | Reduced cost for project selection | USD |
| **Phase** | Reduced cost of prototyping and testing | USD |
| **Phase** | Savings for modification in late development state | USD |
| **Phase** | Increased profit from better customer/solution fit | USD |
| **Phase** | Ratio shift for time spent in problem definition to implementation refinements | % / USD |

**Critical qualifier the book prints as a footnote:** *"The defined period of time might vary
for different industries (e.g., for pharma 5–10 years in comparison to digital services
1–2 years)."* Any quantification of design thinking's financial impact must state its
measurement window.

### The deeper-benefits stack ("Financial bravado exists — deeper benefits are making the difference")

The chapter contrasts a cash-flow view (total costs / total benefits / cumulative benefits over
time) with a layered set of **qualitative measures about capability building and project-based
learning**, stacked from hardest to softest:

1. **Hard financial facts and outcomes**
2. **Measuring brand value and market perception**
3. **Measuring customer retention, loyalty, and conversation**
4. **Measuring team and organizational transformation**
5. **Measuring talent acquisition and degree of workforce up-skilling**
6. **Measuring future success based on mindshift changes**

The point: the cash-flow view alone ("financial bravado") is not where the difference is made.

## Key Concepts

- **Not a one-time effect** — increasing capability maturity compounds the financial impact.
- **The transformative character** — design thinking changes how teams collaborate across
  traditional organizational silos, not just what they build.
- **Design thinking as risk minimization** — "the underlying mindset, tools, and iterative
  process allow teams to validate the needs of the customer/user, profitability, and technical
  implementability. As a result, design thinking provides teams a natural way to minimize the
  risks and to make predictions about market success."
- **Cost cap heuristic** — "usually, the cost for the training and project-based learning is
  **not more than 40 percent of the total design thinking project costs.**"

## Mental Models

- **Time is a critical element.** "Working attitudes and routines cannot be changed from one day
  to the next." Change must run at a speed adapted to the pace of employees.
- **Not everyone will come along.** "It also needs to be accepted that not all employees will
  embrace the change. It is up to leaders to find appropriate roles and tasks for these
  employees that better fit their mindset, attitude, and personality."
- **The EXPLOIT-phase erosion risk.** "In a normal business cycle, there are also phases of
  focus, cost savings, and an over-weighing of EXPLOIT. In these phases, there is a danger that,
  in a very short time, the capabilities and talent that have been painstakingly built up will
  leave the company or be taken over by the new objectives."
- **The defence against that erosion** is to embrace design thinking as a mindset that adds
  value everywhere — from customer-need exploration up to strategy work.

## Anti-patterns

- **Re-branding and re-naming methods.** "It is strongly recommended to build on known tools,
  methods, and design thinking approaches and not to waste budgets on activities such as
  re-branding and re-naming of methods, tools, and frameworks. Usually that results in more
  confusion than a positive effect." Best acceptance comes from building on existing templates,
  literature, and process models known worldwide.
- **Narrowing to design thinking skills only** — continuously expand the toolbox (e.g. business
  ecosystem design skills) to achieve higher skill density.
- **Financial bravado** — reporting only the cash-flow view and missing brand, retention,
  transformation, talent, and mindshift benefits.

## Reference Tables

### Top Five Results from Building Design Thinking Capabilities
*(evaluation of various design thinking capability programs over a decade)*
1. Improved team collaboration
2. Increased customer interaction
3. More efficient processes and workflows
4. Acceleration of time to market
5. More clearly defined value propositions

### Top Five Challenges in Disseminating the Design Thinking Mindset
1. Perseverance and support from top management
2. Time and space for change and transformation
3. Appropriate tasks and roles in a changing culture
4. Healthy balance between EXPLORE and EXPLOIT
5. Perceive design thinking as a **mindset**

### Capability-building programme scaling profile (corporate practice)
| Parameter | Value from the book |
|---|---|
| Pilot cohort size | Usually **no more than five teams** |
| Design challenge duration | **4–12 months**, depending on complexity, available time budgets, and desired integration with defined OKRs |
| Scale-up after first success stories | **20–30 teams** |
| Trainer programmes | Initialized in parallel; first teams then work autonomously without support |
| Accompanying measures | Town halls and leadership boards to increase the spread of relevance |
| Training + project-based learning share of total cost | **≤ 40 percent** of total design thinking project costs |

### Where real costs are incurred
External support of the teams · surveying the intimate capabilities · conducting trainings ·
workshop facilitation conducted by design thinking experts · supporting the measurement of
impact from individual activities to overarching outcomes.

## Worked Example

### Innovation Project Portfolio Analysis — USD 4.0 M

The chapter prints a worked example labelled **"Innovation Project Portfolio Analysis — USD
4.0 M"**: the quantified benefit of a design thinking portfolio, built up from the meta and
phase metrics above.

The construction logic the chapter gives:

1. **Set the measurement window first.** Choose the period appropriate to the industry — pharma
   5–10 years, digital services 1–2 years. Everything below is measured inside that window.
2. **Meta metric:** overall reduced cost from project start to implementation, in USD, across
   the whole portfolio. This is the headline (here, USD 4.0 M).
3. **Decompose by phase**, so the saving can be attributed and defended:
   - Reduced cost for **project selection** (fewer wrong projects entered)
   - Reduced cost of **prototyping and testing**
   - Savings for **modification in late development state** — the largest lever, because per
     Chapter 3 a change during implementation costs `$$$+` versus `$` during understanding
   - Increased profit from **better customer/solution fit**
4. **Add the ratio shift**: time spent in problem definition versus implementation refinements,
   expressed in % and USD. This is the metric that shows the *mechanism* of the saving — effort
   moving leftward in the design cycle.
5. **Layer the qualitative measures on top** (brand value and market perception; customer
   retention, loyalty, conversation; team and organizational transformation; talent acquisition
   and workforce up-skilling; future success based on mindshift changes), because the cash-flow
   view alone understates the case.

### The cost/benefit calculation for a capability programme

The chapter's stated method: "Calculation of the costs depends on how much time employees spend
for the training and how much for the actual application." The scaling move that changes the
economics is making **some of the workforce become design thinking coaches early in their
respective training** — "this approach has a significant impact on the proliferation and number
of projects and teams executing projects with the design thinking mindset."

Options for building capability, with correspondingly varying cost/benefit: establishing an
**internal academy** · using **specialists** · involving **large consulting firms**, which
"have purchased the corresponding skills over the last decade through the takeover of smaller
design studios and agencies."

## Key Takeaways

1. Design thinking impact *is* measurable — at the meta level (total reduced cost start to
   implementation) and by phase.
2. Always declare the measurement window; it differs by industry by an order of magnitude.
3. The dominant financial lever is avoided late change, throwaway effort, and rework.
4. Keep training + project-based learning at or under 40% of total project cost.
5. Start with ≤5 pilot teams on 4–12 month challenges, then scale to 20–30 teams via internal
   coaches.
6. Report the six-layer benefit stack, not just cash flow — brand, retention, transformation,
   talent, and mindshift are where the difference is made.
