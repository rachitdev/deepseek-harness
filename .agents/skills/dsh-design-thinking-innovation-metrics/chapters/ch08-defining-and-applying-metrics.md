# Chapter 8 — Defining and Applying Metrics

*(printed pp. 157–166)*

## Core Idea

How to judge whether a metric — especially one that "has grown historically" — deserves to stay.
The chapter supplies **10 principles for assessing existing metrics**, a **four-category evidence
test**, the **input / process / output / outcome** taxonomy, and the master comparison table that
separates **North Star**, **exploration**, and **exploitation** metrics by description, timing,
cause-and-effect, and type.

The single fastest test in the whole book:

> **"The easiest way of evaluating metrics is to perform a sanity check. Ask what the team did
> differently if figures went up, went down, or stayed the same. If there is no answer, it is
> recommended to put those metrics aside and look for better ones."**

And the design rule: metrics "should be actionable and refer to **ratios and unit economies
rather than to gross quantities**."

## Frameworks Introduced

### The 10 Most Important Principles for Assessing Existing Metrics & Measurement Systems

| # | Principle |
|---|---|
| 1 | Good metrics have **significance and importance** (e.g. in terms of strategy, ambition, or objectives) |
| 2 | Meaningful metrics **describe a whole phenomenon** (e.g. the summary of different indicators of innovation success or behavioral change) |
| 3 | Key parameters for the metric are **accessible and manageable** (e.g. through recurring surveys or access to data points) |
| 4 | The **effort of collection and the value of measurement are in a healthy relationship**, otherwise the effort is not worthwhile |
| 5 | The metric is **understandable and comprehensible** — "the recipients of it immediately know which activities to develop" |
| 6 | A good system of metrics is **precise**, and the resulting measures are clear |
| 7 | Ideally the metric is created **in real time or in a timely manner**, so that rapid action or improvements are initiated |
| 8 | Metrics results are **communicated transparently** and made available to the respective teams/organization |
| 9 | All applied metrics are **meaningful and credible from different perspectives** so that they are widely accepted |
| 10 | The metrics applied are **as factual and evidence based as possible** |

### The Four Evidence Categories
Existing metrics or measures divide into four categories — the book's own examples:

| # | Category | The book's example |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **No evidence** | "Common sense shows that potential employees will join a specific company if it will pay them more than their direct competitors." |
| 2 | **Irrelevant evidence** | "We compare three randomly selected world-class companies that have nothing to do with our business model and analyze their revenue multiples." |
| 3 | **Unreliable evidence** | "Employees assume they are more innovative when they work by themselves in their home offices." |
| 4 | **Evidence-based** | "No interaction with potential customers reduces the likelihood of innovation success." |

### Input / Process / Output / Outcome

| Type | What it measures | Book's examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Input** | Assets and resources | Budgets for design thinking training programs; quality of insights generated by data and observations |
| **Process** | Efficiency or productivity of the innovation or business process | Time to complete validation of product/market fit; time-to-market for expansion of core value proposition |
| **Output** | The immediate-term result | Financial and nonfinancial results of innovation activity; number of in-depth interviews with customers/users; increased use of agile toolbox by teams |
| **Outcome** | Results that arise immediately after the end of a defined project activity | Number of new customers won with a new service; employee satisfaction rate; degree of solution fit with MVP trials of paying customers |

Process measurements are "usually quantifications related to time, quantity, development costs,
quality of innovation realized, as well as risk management indicators related to the developed
product, service, or experiences."

### The Master Comparison Table — North Star vs Exploration vs Exploitation Metrics

| | **North Star Metric(s)** | **Minimum Viable Set of Exploration Metrics** | **Appropriate Mix of Relevant Exploitation Metrics** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Description** | Focus on growth ambitions and directions to manage product, innovation, and business success | Inform business decisions by measuring **means relative to ends** | Quantifies business performance to improve scaling, efficiency, and cost savings |
| **Best time to be applied** | Definition of vision, strategy, and strategic choice | EXPLORE (**before** product/market fit) | EXPLOIT (**after** product/market fit or system/actors fit) |
| **Cause & effect** | Focus on **impact and actions** | Focus on **learning, improving, and effectiveness** | Focus on **effectiveness and efficiency** |
| **Represents mostly** | Company, organization (support for understanding better the why, how, and what) | Company, organization, team/individual behavior (support evidence for innovation work) | Performance of company and activities (support for monitoring the health of the company) |
| **Types of metrics** | Behavior change, ratios, rates | Ratios/rates, new capabilities, new behavior, unit economies | Gross quantities and unit economies |
| **Examples** | Attentions, engagements, transactions, frequencies, productivity, and reach | Time-to-insight, budget-to-development, time-to-adaption, maturity level of design thinking | Profitability; ROI; growth of users/customers |

### Learning Zone → Action Zone → Impact Zone
For the iterative design of measurement systems using design thinking, "it has proven useful to
roughly divide the step-by-step procedure into the dimensions of **learning, action, and impact
zone**," plotted against time & maturity, ending with "show impact of measures and created
measurement system."

## Key Concepts

- **Correlation vs causation.** "Some of the defined metrics will correlate (similarity between
  two metrics), and other metrics will have the ability to show **causation**. Causation is very
  important because it provides valuable insights to trigger change before things might go into
  the wrong direction. However, it is not always direct, and a specific event might have multiple
  causes."
- **No blueprints for EXPLORE.** "There are no blueprints for the measurement of ill-defined
  metrics in EXPLORE. For example, the respective minimum viable measurements for EXPLORE are to
  be defined depending on the situation, the culture, and a top-line metric."
- **Metrics are gameable.** OKR achievement is "usually **not directly linked to bonuses** for
  the team or single team members" precisely because of this.

## Mental Models

- **Compare means to ends.** Actionable metrics "help to make business decisions by comparing
  means to ends."
- **Ratios over gross quantities**, especially in EXPLORE.
- **Metrics must primarily support the appropriate next steps and activities** — not describe the
  past.

## Anti-patterns

### Metrics as a target for manipulation and subversion
"Especially in large organizations with top-down driven measurement systems and efforts, it can
be observed that metrics are either **gamed over time** or motivate teams to **operational hustle
and bustle** that doesn't have any real effect on value creation, delivery, and capture."

The chapter's two named failure pairs:

| When you measure … on … | … then you might get … |
|---|---|
| Innovation teams on **# of ideas created** | **1000s of ideas** |
| Design thinking teams on **# of co-creation workshops performed** | **End-to-end booked Outlook calendars** |

On idea counts: "In theory, the number of ideas provides the basis for upstream innovation; but
without putting the ideas into a broader spectrum of impact, this kind of measurement might be
impractical or lead to poor behavior."

Also: "the danger remains that **outdated measurement systems and approaches don't match the new
ways of working and innovation style, and are thus measured incorrectly or with little
significance.**"

## Reference Tables

### Examples of general application cases for metrics
| Application case | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Problem analysis** | A gap between the expected result of a measurement and the actual result; the result provides the reason to determine the cause and initiate corrective actions |
| **Trend development** | Measurement over time gives information about the dynamics of organizational measures and processes; "often, only the passage of time indicates which trends are significant and where one-time effects occur" |
| **Budgeting** | Past measurements used for future budget decisions, especially if significantly better results can be achieved through higher budgets |
| **Innovation** | A whole set of metrics applied to build new capabilities, change behavior, and measure the effectiveness and impact of innovation work |
| **Process improvements** | Quality, costs, or time measured to reveal optimization potential; adjustments can be extensive or incremental |
| **Recognition** | Metrics used to reward individuals for performance; in a performance dialogue, 360-degree feedback initializes a holistic assessment and derives targeted coaching measures |
| **Objectives & key results (OKRs)** | Modern performance measurements let teams define their own metrics indicating whether defined objectives are on track; also serve to verify or validate indicators of the actual metric |

### Purposes of Innovation Measurements and Metrics
- Provide strategic direction by indicating shifts in priorities
- Direct the (re)allocation of resources
- Assess the effectiveness of innovation spending
- Make teams and leaders accountable and incentivize them to achieve objectives
- Analyze and enhance innovation performance and culture
- Change behavior and processes

| | **High uncertainty** — Apply minimum viable set of exploration metrics | **Medium uncertainty** | **Low uncertainty** — Apply appropriate mix of relevant exploitation metrics | **Create, adjust, and apply overall IMS** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Measurements demonstrate** | Design thinking mindset applied; exploration leads to learning | Knowledge enhancement; new solutions are developed, tested, and re-designed or killed | Validation of viability; validation of value proposition; agile customer/MVP development | Innovation work creates the desired impact; configuration and validated scalable unit economics in different dimensions |
| **Input metrics** | Freedom given by leadership team to EXPLORE beyond core business; management commitment to defined culture of exploration and renewal | Cost to final prototype for extended design thinking team from problem identification to potential solution | Number of lead customers/users participating in MVP testing with a heterogeneity of profiles representing all potential persona groups; cost from problem definition to product/market fit related to number of sprints to solve the customer problem | Organizational growth and # of employees engaged in team-of-teams activities to create new market opportunities; scalability of operational tasks |
| **Output metrics** | Improvement of design thinking maturity based on # of project-based learning interactions; # of critical and unknown assumptions identified | # of experiments which contribute significantly to defined learning outcomes | Additional valuable ideas for add-on functions from users/customers collected in MVP testing; increased use of agile toolbox by teams which attended project-based training | Revenue growth from exploration activities; ROI on innovation activities and design challenges; customer engagement with new products, services, experiences; increase of application of new mindset/culture through project-based learning |

| **Outcome metrics** | Degree of fit of persona developed with strategic mass-market requirements defined in corporate strategy | Degree of compatibility of solution with existing technology, applications, and IT (feasibility) | Degree of solution fit with MVP trials of paying customers/users; degree of market fit with conversations (e.g. CAC, ARR, NPS) | Ratio of (expected) profits from users/customers from EXPLORE compared to adjustments of target segments in EXPLORE; (expected) increase in revenue from new customer/new product category as a percentage of total sales |

### General recommendation to manage metrics
The chapter prints four numbered recommendations; the extraction preserved the bullets of all
four but the headings of the first two are lost in the illustration (see Scope & Limits):
- *(1)* Trigger motivation for change · Define actions related to measurements
- *(2)* Analyze movement, numbers, interaction, or other relevant elements over time
- **(3) Easy to understand** — mind-map metrics, correlations, and causality · test whether the
  team understands the reason for the measurement
- **(4) Comparisons and/or directions** — showcase the direction the project, product, team, or
  individuals are moving · create timelines and find clues for comparisons

## Worked Example

### Input → Process → Output → Outcome for a design thinking training programme

The chapter prints a filled-in chain for design thinking training, showing how the four metric
types connect on the team/culture level to behavioral change (better collaboration across teams):

| Input | Process | Output | Outcome | Impact |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **$ budget for training**<br>**# design thinking coaches/trainers** | **# training hours / coaching hours**<br>**# trainings performed** | **# employees trained**<br>**# employee satisfaction** | **# maturity level design thinking**<br>**# individual skill levels** | **Products with outstanding customer experience** |

### Input → Output → Outcome in conjunction with OKRs

The parallel chain for a performance measurement system, where "the input of teams and capacity
is leading to output which is monitored, for example, on quarterly outcomes set by objectives and
key results":

| Input | Output | Outcome | Impact |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Planned initiative / mission**<br>**# engineering & design resources** | **# of new features**<br>**# customer tests** | **Degree of customer problem solved with new features** | **Driving up customer satisfaction with new features** |

### Connecting the Dots of Measuring — the full-cycle cause-and-effect chain

The chapter's spine illustration maps metric types onto the fits, running from **AMBIGUITY** to
**SUCCESS**:

`DESIGN CHALLENGE → PROBLEM/CUSTOMER FIT → PROBLEM/SOLUTION FIT → PRODUCT/MARKET FIT`
mapped to `Input → Process → Output → Outcome`

| Band | Contents |
|---|---|
| **Input** (North Star, project) | Mandate to innovate · team size · budgets · foresight |
| **Process** (speed, effectiveness, efficiency) | Progress of activities · time-to-market across EXPLORE/EXPLOIT portfolio · return on investments on various levels |
| **Output** (activities) | Additional valuable ideas · new functions required from users and potential customers · value proposition validated via MVP testing |
| **Output** (participation) | Learnings · awareness · attitudes · mindset · first steps of cultural change |
| **Outcome** (short/mid term) | Increase in revenue from the new customer/product category as % of total sales |
| **Outcome** (long term) | **Total added value of the project to all value the company generates from EXPLORE activities** |

## Key Takeaways

1. Run the sanity check on every metric: what would the team do differently if it went up, down,
   or stayed flat? No answer → discard it.
2. Score existing metrics against the 10 principles, especially #4 (collection effort vs value)
   and #10 (evidence-based).
3. Classify the evidence: no / irrelevant / unreliable / evidence-based.
4. Prefer ratios and unit economies over gross quantities.
5. Use the master table to pick the metric family by timing: North Star at strategy, exploration
   before product/market fit, exploitation after.
6. Chain input → process → output → outcome so each metric has a downstream consequence.
7. Never link OKR achievement directly to bonuses — it invites gaming.
