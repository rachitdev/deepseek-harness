# Chapter 4 — Facilitating Workshops, Working in Teams, Building Capabilities

*(printed pp. 74–96)*

## Core Idea

Before any metric can be defined, the *system that produces the innovation* must exist:
facilitated workshops, interdisciplinary teams organised as a team-of-teams, and a deliberate
capability-building programme. This chapter supplies the measurement hooks for each — team
metrics, organizational network analysis, and a four-level **Design Thinking Maturity Model**
that takes 2–3 years and several capability shifts to traverse.

> "If a company really wants change and aims to strengthen innovation, it cannot expect it to
> just happen. Rather, an environment must be created in which new market opportunities can
> thrive."

## Frameworks Introduced

### Design Abduction (after Dorst, 2017)
The cognitive process of design thinking, contrasted with traditional reasoning:

| Mode | WHAT (elements) | HOW (pattern of relationship) | OUTCOME (observed phenomenon) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Deduction** — solid reasoning from cause to effect | known | known | ??? |
| **Induction** — discovering patterns | known | ??? | known |
| **Normal abduction** — solid problem solving | ??? | known | known |
| **Design abduction** — two unknowns lead to a process of creative exploration | ??? | ??? | known |

This is why "the dance with ambiguity is challenging for both the facilitator and the team."

### The Hunting and Transport Model (Larry Leifer, Stanford ME310)
The core description of design thinking behavior.

**HUNTING** — the hunt for the next big market opportunity starts after exploring the problem
space, with divergent thinking and first experimentation with known solution approaches. Goal
identified → first prototypes built → tested against what is known to the customer/user. For
the unknown, customer feedback provides the information. By frequently alternating inductive and
deductive thinking, the unknown is revealed over time; **pragmatic abduction produces the
discovery.**

**TRANSPORT** — after the big idea is discovered it has to be made tangible, and the question of
how to bring it home is in focus: fix requirements, plan implementation, optimize resources.
Transport is not straightforward — "the way home also has ups and downs and road blocks."
Overcome these by involving key stakeholders who were already in the hunt.

**The hunting team composition:** a small, agile, interdisciplinary team with maximum diversity
of skills — a good hunter; a gathering specialist; a realist who keeps track of time, equipment,
and conditions; and someone who pays attention to team dynamics, emotions, and communication.

**The five rules — Accept, Learn, and Apply the Hunting and Transport Philosophy:**
1. Hunting is not wandering (have a purpose)
2. Never go hunting alone (multi-capability teams)
3. Don't give up too early (patience with failure)
4. Don't confuse transport with hunting (**declare the behavior**)
5. Bring it home (deliver results)

### Organizational Network Analysis (ONA)
"A structured method for visualizing the flow of communication, information, and decisions in an
organization." Networks consist of **nodes** and **links**.

| Node type | Description | Risk / value |
|---|---|---|
| **Central connectors** | Team members who seem to know everyone; share a lot of information and have quick influence on all teams | Often popular and highly involved in company news |
| **Knowledge brokers** | Build bridges between teams | "Without knowledge brokers, the exchange of information and ideas stalls" |
| **Shadow players** | Easily overlooked, not connected to the rest of the teams | Pose a risk — e.g. outstanding data scientists who don't collaborate with design thinking teams stagnate innovation and "are also easily convinced to take their talents elsewhere" |
| **Ties** | Formal and informal relationships between nodes | Optimal ties between central connectors and knowledge brokers ensure useful information is shared |

**The six ONA diagnostic questions the book prints:**
- Who holds the network of teams together?
- Who has the potential to become a bottleneck in execution?
- Who is a critical connection to external knowledge?
- Who needs to be connected internally to be effective?
- Who is totally disconnected and at risk of exit?
- Who is talented, but not connected to relevant future programs and projects?

ONA as the basis of organizational design helps: transform organizations in savvier ways by
identifying formal *and* informal leaders; develop talent more effectively by minimizing role
confusion and redundancy; increase operational efficiency and effectiveness through a
dynamically organized structure.

### Team-of-Teams
> "Team-of-teams is based on the fact that teams outperform individuals in complex contexts
> because individuals cannot comprehend the whole or possess all the required skills."

Progression illustrated: `TRADITIONAL COMMAND STRUCTURE → MANAGING TEAMS THROUGH A CENTRALIZED
[command] → TEAM-OF-TEAMS`.

**How to expand toward a Circle of WE / shift the culture:**
- Upshift leadership from commanding to building a shared consciousness
- Expect and empower decision making by whoever is closest to the content
- Work directly together to build trust
- Know your purpose
- Be willing to learn

**Lessons from working with team-of-teams:** teams outperform individuals in complex contexts;
any one individual cannot comprehend the whole or have all required skills; most organizations
have yet to even begin to understand how to gain the advantages of teams at scale.

Required mindset elements: **Dance with Ambiguity** (learn to expect the unexpected) and
**Radical Collaboration** (connect in a way that allows rapid self-configuration to respond to
new threats or opportunities). Leaders must link organizational elements vertically (up and
down the command chain) and horizontally (across units, divisions, and external partners).

> "The non-structured mesh of individual connections is what makes team-of-teams successful."

## Key Concepts

- **Interdisciplinary team** — comprising several disciplines; "ideas are produced on a
  collective basis. In the end, everybody feels responsible for the overall solution."
- **Multidisciplinary team** — "every member is an expert who advocates his or her
  specialization. The solution is often a compromise." Its advantage: everybody stands behind
  the commonly created product — "a factor of success that interdisciplinary teams cannot
  afford."
- **T-shaped profile** — the *vertical bar* is specialist skill from vocational training or
  specialized study (mirrored in job descriptions). The *horizontal bar* is many cultures, many
  disciplines, many systems (empathetic and communicative) — experiences and learnings "usually
  not apparent from the job descriptions."
- **Two parallel paths to capability**: capability-building programs *and* hiring talent. Hiring
  is quickest "but is usually only feasible to a limited extent."
- **Project-based learning** — the delivery vehicle: missions to complete, embedded in a design
  thinking capability program.
- **System agility** — needed at all levels, from the work environment to OKR measurement to
  organizational design, work processes, and structures.

## Mental Models

- **Declare the behavior.** The facilitator's job is to support the current behavior — hunt or
  transport — and the team's job is to say which one they are in.
- **Facilitation is more art than science.** Core principles that always stay true: being
  authentic, embracing constructive conflicts, active listening, and creating an inviting
  physical, hybrid, or virtual space.
- **Guides, not guardians.** "Every single member of a design thinking team plays a role in
  spreading design thinking mindsets by looking for opportunities to openly share and pass on
  their methods and tools. All of them should be guides to human-centered design, not its
  guardians."
- **Tweaks won't do it.** "Design teams need to get things off of the whiteboard and into the
  real world!"

## Anti-patterns

- **The facilitator taking command or dictating the outcome.** "The task of a facilitator is not
  to develop an outstanding creative idea or to make the right decisions." Objectivity is key;
  own contributions kept to a minimum.
- **Copying physical workshops into virtual environments unchanged.** "Simply copying existing
  experiences, procedures, and known workshop elements into the virtual environment has proven
  to be unpromising."
- **Traditional command-and-control hierarchies** blocking autonomy and accountability.
- **Shadow players left unconnected** — innovation stagnates and talent leaves.

## Reference Tables

### Golden Rules of Performing Design Thinking Workshops
| # | Rule |
|---|---|
| 1 | Professional preparation and planning |
| 2 | Information about the design brief / topic of the problem |
| 3 | Invitation with agenda, directions, and access code |
| 4 | Positive atmosphere for participants and sufficient space for collaboration |
| 5 | An experienced workshop facilitator able to apply tools and methods as needed |
| 6 | Use of appropriate warm-ups that bring out the desired mood state of the participants |
| 7 | Material or tools to build physical/virtual prototypes |
| 8 | Time and access to customers/users for testing initial features, experiences, solutions |
| 9 | Transparent communication about the intended mindset, the (virtual) etiquette of collaboration, and design principles |
| 10 | Assembling teams based on individuals' T-shapes and thinking preferences |
| 11 | Documentation of results, customer feedback, and experiments |
| 12 | **Measurement via a set of exploration metrics** |
| 13 | **Embedding of OKRs from problem identification to implementation and scaling of solutions** |

### Physical vs Virtual Design Thinking Workshop
| | PHYSICAL | VIRTUAL |
|---|---|---|
| **Pros** | Real human interactions; faster realization of moods, feelings, and work progress; creation of tangible product and service prototypes | 24-hour access from everywhere; unlimited space, breakout rooms, and participants; digital documentation of results on the go |
| **Cons** | Limited space and room; cost and time to travel to location; limited number of participants | Limited physical interactions; more breaks needed and shorter cycles of collaboration; limitations in building tangible prototypes |

Neuroscience finding cited: "the interaction of a team in front of a physical whiteboard
triggers more activity in different areas of the brain than the interaction using an online
whiteboard." Hybrid sits between the two.

### Typical Team Metrics and Measurements
- Team member performance related to **helpfulness**
- Team member performance related to **initiatives**
- Overall team **readiness** based on feelings and maturity with regard to mindset, tools, methods
- Overall team **commitment** for achieving joint objectives and key results
- Overall team **collaboration and processes** for decision making, communication, conflict
  resolution, problem handling

### Procedure for Creating Workshop Experiences in the Metaverse
1. Define the main objectives of the interaction with the design thinking players (team and
   community building, exploring the problem space, working with analogies and benchmarks)
2. Determine the roles of the design thinking players and other stakeholders (active
   participant, providing briefing, engaging for testing)
3. Launch the activities and tap into intuitive immersive experiences — do not overwhelm
   participants with information and options
4. **Measure results and analyze the behavior of participants in different thinking modes and
   activities (e.g., eye tracking and facial attributes data collection via XR technologies)**
5. Constantly adapt and improve the experience
6. Embed the metaverse activities in physical interactions or extended realities
7. Share best practices with the wider design thinking and innovation community

### Design Thinking vs Design Thinking & Innovation Metrics
| What | Design Thinking | Design Thinking & Innovation Metrics |
|---|---|---|
| **Objectives** | Product, service, process innovation; realizing new market opportunities; transformation/new mindset | Customer-led and data-led innovations; reducing uncertainty in dynamic environments; professionalizing/scaling transformation |
| **Focus** | Understanding the problem space; building empathy with customers; creating tangible prototypes; providing insights rich in strategic information | Improving evidence through measurement; establishing the basis for AI-supported innovation; creating a better understanding of effectiveness; providing standardization in comparison |
| **Qualitative methods** | Ethnographic research, synthesis, definition of point of view, brainstorming, idea selection, prototyping, solution tests | Understanding the need for measurements and metrics and consequences of possible actions; asking the right questions |
| **Quantitative methods** | Application of criteria score, define success criteria, digital-enabled A/B testing; applying mostly descriptive analytics | Operationalization of measurements and metrics; applying descriptive, predictive, and prescriptive analytics |
| **Typical metrics** | Problem/customer fit, problem/solution fit, product/market fit | North Star metrics, minimum viable exploration metrics, kill metrics, OKRs for innovation teams |

### Key Expert Facilitator Behaviors
- Manages complicated engagements spanning multiple customer needs, places, times,
  technologies, and actors
- Works successfully with all levels of the organization, including executives and high-impact
  stakeholders
- Iterates complex design challenges in real time to adapt to emergent group and project needs
- Innovates design thinking tools, methods, frameworks, and facilitation
- Creates a coherent web of interactions on an ad hoc basis, combining warm-ups, tools, thinking
  states, dialogue, and fluidity

## Worked Example

### The Design Thinking Maturity Model — the book's four-level, three-dimension grid

The chapter prints a maturity model across three **dimensions** and four **levels**, with an
explicit time axis of `START → YEAR 1 → YEAR 2 → FUTURE`. "Usually, it takes several capability
shifts over a time horizon of **2 to 3 years** until the teams master all capabilities."

| Dimension | **Level 1 — limited capabilities** (START) | **Level 2 — evolving capabilities** (Year 1) | **Level 3 — value driven** (Year 2) | **Level 4 — mastering all capabilities** (Future) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **New talent / future skills** | Low mastery; competency-based teams; limited design thinking training exposure; limited shared ownership or team accountability | T-shaped teams; teams supported with facilitation and design thinking training; challenged; still need for strong guidance | Team-of-teams established; strong contribution; self-organizing; strong purpose and customer focus; attracting best talent | Mindfulness and courtesy; circle of "we" established; servant leaders; belonging; engaged teams |
| **Mindset / tools** | Good understanding of agile, basic understanding of new ways of working and design cycle; limited transparency; fixed scope; limited value-driven projects | Empirical design thinking process established; first value-driven design challenges accomplished; mastering basic design thinking tools and methods | Ownership counts more than process; multiple team cadences; design thinking scaled across organization; continuous delivery of projects over macro and micro cycles | Driven by team objectives and results; mastering individual objectives; delivering tangible results and managing multi-design-thinking projects and implementation (bring it home) |
| **Organization / leadership / culture** | Competency-based structure; closed groups and organizational silos; push culture | Team-based structure; learning organization; push *and* pull culture | Self-organizing teams; strong team contribution; pushing boundaries | Innovative; exploring and high curiosity; ownership |

### A filled-in minimum viable exploration metric set for a capability programme

The chapter prints a concrete three-part acceptance criteria example — "minimum viable
exploration metrics that are predominantly measuring the adoption of the new mindset and team
maturity":

| # | Criterion | Definition of "met" |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Capability development** | Successful completion of the training modules and project-based learning sprints, with each of the teams reaching their learning goals |
| **2** | **Project delivery** | Positive feedback from the project customer on project delivery (e.g. time, improved or new products and services, product/market fit) |
| **3** | **Behavioral** | Positive feedback from the project sponsor and select team members on future skills and mindset displayed on the job |

**Example of validating the new innovation capabilities through improved project delivery —
"Success is:"**
- More projects successfully delivered
- Projects delivered faster
- Reduced change requests late into the process
- Failing earlier and learning from it
- New mindset with better ways of working (measured, for example, in team surveys)

### The scaling checklist for project-based learning
`SET A STRONG NORTH STAR` (customer-centric strategy; teams create strong missions based on
OKRs) · `ALLOW PROJECT-BASED LEARNING — IN EACH MISSION TO COMPLETE` · `SET CLEAR PRIORITIES AND
AMBITION FOR THE DESIGN CHALLENGES` (design principles and how much risk the organization will
take) · `MANAGE EXPLORE AND EXPLOIT` (actively review, measure, manage the portfolio; feedback
and clear governance) · `DEVELOP THE BASIS FOR A DESIGN THINKING ACADEMY AND PROFESSIONALIZE
FACILITATION` · `FUEL AND FUND INITIATIVES` (funding, talent, tools, sponsors).

## Key Takeaways

1. Design thinking cognition is **design abduction** — two unknowns — which is why ambiguity
   tolerance is a measurable capability, not a personality trait.
2. Hunting vs transport: declare which behavior the team is in; never confuse the two.
3. Interdisciplinary ≠ multidisciplinary: the latter produces compromises, the former collective
   ownership.
4. Measure teams on helpfulness, initiatives, readiness, commitment, and collaboration processes
   — not output counts.
5. ONA turns culture into a measurable network: central connectors, knowledge brokers, shadow
   players, ties.
6. Expect 2–3 years and several capability shifts to move through the four maturity levels;
   measure capability development, project delivery, and behavior.
