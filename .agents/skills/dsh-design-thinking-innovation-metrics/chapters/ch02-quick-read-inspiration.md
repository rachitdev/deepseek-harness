# Chapter 2 — A Quick Read to Get Inspired

*(printed pp. 45–56)*

## Core Idea

A compressed tour of the whole book: the **jungle vs superhighway** analogy for EXPLORE and
EXPLOIT, the **top five challenges** organizations hit when they try to do both, **seven tips
and tricks** for building an innovation engine and measurement system, and a side-by-side
**before/after** of a traditional innovation measurement system versus a modified one. The
chapter's golden rule:

> **START APPLYING THE METRIC WHEN IT MATTERS, AND NOT TOO SOON!**

## Frameworks Introduced

### The Jungle and the Superhighway

**EXPLOIT** feels like driving a car on a well-lit *superhighway*, with cruise control and
adaptive driving programs, thanks to intelligent assistance systems.
**EXPLORE** feels like being exposed to the *jungle*, far from known civilization, without a
path to the destination, where many things are unknown and ad hoc reaction is necessary.

The transition happens only "much later, after countless deep interviews with customers/users,
shared insights, formulated point of views, built prototypes, and iterations of individual
functions to the complete value proposition."

| TYPICAL ACTIVITIES IN EXPLORE | TYPICAL ACTIVITIES IN EXPLOIT |
|---|---|
| Breaking rules and dreaming | Outstanding in your job |
| Opening doors and listening | Being loyal to your team |
| Trusting and being trustworthy | Working with people you can rely on |
| Seeking fairness, not advantage | Seeking competitive advantages |
| Experimenting and co-creating together | Getting the job right the first time |
| Making mistakes, failing, and persevering | Striving for perfection |
| Asking if it is worth it | Returning favors |

### The Path from Status Quo to Superior IMS

Apply the design thinking mindset iteratively to the *design of the measurement system itself*.
Build → measure → learn is adapted to the design of metrics. Inflection points are anticipated
via **sigmoidal curves (S-curves)** and automatically trigger new design cycles of the IMS. The
strategic inflection points are influenced by new/changing customer behavior, business cycles,
capabilities, and technologies. The North Star forms the guiding principle for the teams, the
application of OKRs, and the definition of team and individual objectives.

Stack (bottom to top): **North Star Metric(s) → Objectives → Key Results**, plotted against
*time / maturity*.

## Key Concepts

- **Ambition levels of objectives**
  - **Moonshot** — bold, radical-innovation character, mid- and long-term. "True Moonshots in
    terms of agreed scoring are usually not 100 percent achieved; scores between **60 and 70
    percent are very decent results**."
  - **Everest** — applied with innovative business ecosystem initiatives; overall objective is
    new-to-market and new-to-customer value propositions. More room for creative solutions.
  - **Roofshot** — operational and short-term improvements, "usually 100 percent achievable."
- **Kill metrics** — equip Moonshot teams with metrics that stop larger innovation initiatives
  or design challenges when certain requirements, feasibilities, or budget limitations come
  into play (see Ch. 10).
- **IMS maturity ladder** — IMS 0 → IMS 2/3; AI-supported innovation measurement sits at the
  far end (see Ch. 7).

## Mental Models

- **Both engines are legitimate; the mistake is applying one engine's instruments to the other.**
  "The measurement is situationally different and a comparison with the activities on the
  superhighway is not adequate."
- **Metrics are vibrant, not fixed.** "Love it, change it, or leave it."
- **Fewer, modified metrics beat radically new ones early on.**

## Anti-patterns

The chapter's own **Top Five Challenges** table names them, each with its remedy:

| Challenge (the anti-pattern) | The book's counter-move |
|---|---|
| Jumping into solutions without understanding the problem | Beginning at the beginning for both creating new market opportunities *and* measuring innovation |
| Having one culture, measurement system, and mindset across all functions and teams | Building awareness for different settings and activities in EXPLORE and EXPLOIT |
| Outdated measurement systems bumping into iterative, customer-centric, agile customer development | Accepting the concept of minimum viable exploration metrics |
| Implementers and scalers not involved early in the EXPLORE process | Forming a transition from EXPLORE to EXPLOIT and the relevant measurements |
| No freedom, budgets, and time for teams to run through the entire macro-cycle | Guiding teams to optimize the core *and* declaring and empowering breakthrough thinking |

## Reference Tables

### The Seven Tips and Tricks

| # | Tip | Practice |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **It is hard to measure what hasn't happened — focus on the team and capabilities** | Start with a team that has the right attitude, mindset, and diverse skill set for wicked problems. Focus a minimum viable set of exploration metrics on capability building and training innovation skills "to survive in the jungle together." |
| 2 | **One size does not fit all — distinguish between EXPLORE and EXPLOIT** | Metrics that evaluate the existing core business usually have no relevance in EXPLORE. Experiment and create a *map* of exploration metrics fitting the culture, maturity level, objectives, and ambition. |
| 3 | **Keep it simple — create acceptance in the team/organization** | Avoid collecting all kinds of data just to have measured something. Use metrics that are simple, meaningful, intuitive for the design challenge at hand. "Metrics have the greatest impact when they are understood and accepted by the team." |
| 4 | **Leverage existing measurement systems — focus on the relevant metrics** | Organizations have historically grown systems with a strong EXPLOIT focus. Reflect constructively and adjust step by step, aligned with new capabilities, cultural change, and transformation. Fewer and modified metrics find more acceptance early than radically new or additional ones. |
| 5 | **Don't be afraid to modify or adapt a metric — love it, change it, or leave it** | Establish a culture where metrics are perceived as vibrant. As teams evolve to higher design thinking maturity, metrics should reflect that maturity. If a metric does not provide insight in the thick of the jungle, adjust or replace it. |
| 6 | **Focus on the customer with the metric — create actions from measurements** | Supplement internally oriented metrics with customer-orientation measures such as sales of new products. If the market opportunity has no significance for the customer, realign it. An initial simple dashboard with the most important metrics contributes significantly to changed culture. |
| 7 | **Don't let the wrong metrics stifle innovation — create transition into exploitation** | Let everyone know the team and organization are on track, and make sure there is a transformation from the jungle to the superhighway with the measurement system changing accordingly. |

### Begin at the Beginning — typical metrics by stage

| Stage | Typical metrics named |
|---|---|
| **Minimum viable set of exploration metrics** | Number of customer interactions over the entire design cycle (macro or micro); % of employees engaged in design thinking capability programs versus all employees; degree of collaboration between different disciplines/teams |
| **Typical OKR-level measures** | Clear deliverables, key results, and contribution to North Star ambition over the next four weeks |
| **Typical exploitation metrics** | Market share growth; rate of new customer success and stickiness; % of capital investment to new capabilities; satisfaction of actors in the ecosystem |
| **Financial/business metrics** | Customer feedback; impact on revenues; profit margin; efficiency gains |

## Worked Example

### Transforming a traditional measurement system into a meaningful one

The chapter prints two complete systems side by side. Reproduced with the book's own items.

**Example of a typical traditional innovation measurement system**

| INPUT | PROCESS EFFECTIVENESS | PERFORMANCE OUTCOMES |
|---|---|---|
| Spending (percentage of sales) | **1. Development activities** — % hitting gates on time; % meeting quality guidelines | % of sales from new products in the past N years |
| Human resources devoted to innovation | **2. Patenting activity** — # patents filed; # patents commercialized; % ideas covered by patents | Success ratio (percentage of meeting financial goals) |
| Pipeline of ideas/concepts | **3. Budget versus actual** — time; cost/investment | Revenue growth |
| Number of projects in active development | **4. Average time to market** — # of new products launched; % of projects that are major improvements | Return on investment in innovation (ROIC) |
| Percent of ideas/concepts from outside the firm | | % of profits from new customers (or occasions); % of profits from new categories |
| Ratio of ideas from inside/outside | | Average time to break-even/cash; customer satisfaction; profit growth due to new products/services; percent of profits from new products in a given period; NPV of portfolio; potential of portfolio to meet growth targets |

Why it fails: it is stage-gate logic built on "classical input variables and a collection of
many ideas (based on assumptions without customer validation), which are processed on the basis
of criteria per gate." It captures neither cross-functional collaboration nor learning, and
"provided almost no framework for actions or improving the culture, procedures, and processes."

**Example of a modified innovation measurement system** — organised along
`PROBLEM → PROBLEM/SOLUTION FIT → PRODUCT/MARKET FIT → SCALE` and by level:

| Level | Metrics |
|---|---|
| **Design Challenge / Project** | % of new customer problems solved versus identified in X-time logged over the design cycle; tested versus untested assumptions; cost spent for modifications after the market launch in X-time; # of customer adoption in X-time versus market size; customer stickiness versus all customers in X-time; unit cost per transaction versus IT investments to scale |
| **Portfolio** | Innovation portfolio balance (projects related to EXPLORE versus EXPLOIT); % of product/service innovations to market share in X-time; average time spent adapting to unexpected strategic changes; real options — market opportunities versus relevant opportunities; engagement level of ecosystem actors contributing to the proposition; actual versus realization of ecosystem capital in X-time |
| **Organization / Culture** | % of employees engaged in capability programs versus all employees; % of intrapreneurs versus innovation projects per business unit; # of employees using free-time-to-experiment; % of employees motivated by intrinsic motivation |
| **Team** | Degree of trust between teams and top management; number of conflicts within the team that were resolved constructively |
| **Individual** | Tracking of individual's confidence level |

The book's own note on this transformation: *"Even the transformation of shifting toward
actionable metrics might be a very strong metric for a certain time because it indicates the
speed of transforming the organization into a new mindset."*

## Key Takeaways

1. Golden rule: apply the metric when it matters, and not too soon.
2. EXPLORE and EXPLOIT run on different values and activities, so the innovation engine and the
   scaling engine each need their own metrics.
3. Start with a minimum viable set of exploration metrics — e.g. training and development of
   design thinking capabilities, or data points at the *tool* level.
4. Moonshot 60–70% achievement is a decent result; Roofshot should be 100% achievable.
5. Modify existing measurement systems rather than replacing them wholesale; fewer, modified
   metrics get accepted faster.
6. Design the measurement system itself with design thinking, and let S-curve inflection points
   trigger the next IMS design cycle.
