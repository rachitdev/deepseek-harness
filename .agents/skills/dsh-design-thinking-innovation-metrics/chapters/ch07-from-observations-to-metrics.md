# Chapter 7 — Measuring 101: From Observations to Real Metrics

*(printed pp. 145–156)*

## Core Idea

"Almost anything can be measured." But the chapter's real warning is the counterweight:

> **"It is also of paramount importance not to confuse the notion that everything that can be
> measured should be measured."**

The chapter fixes vocabulary (observation → quantification → measurement → metric), explains
*why* measuring works (uncertainty reduction), separates **well-defined** from **ill-defined**
measurement objects, and introduces the **IMS Maturity Model (IMS 0 → IMS 3)** that the rest of
the book uses as its progress ladder.

The purpose of measurement, stated plainly: **"achieving a reduction of quantitative uncertainty
based on observations. Here it is important to understand that even a marginal reduction in
uncertainty can be extremely valuable."**

## Frameworks Introduced

### The four-term chain — Observation → Quantification → Measurement → Metric

| Term | Definition the book gives | Worked value from the chapter's own example |
|---|---|---|
| **Observation** | The raw thing seen, in a period (e.g. "in Q1") | Solution tests with customers are happening; money is being invested in testing prototypes |
| **Quantification** | "Mapping observations into numbers" | Number of solution tests with customers = **98** |
| **Measurement** | "Generating quantifications or degrees of something" | Expenses for testing prototypes = **$4,000**; number of new insights collected |
| **Metric** | "Creating a meaningful derivative of measure" — a derivative of measures with a defined meaning in the innovation and business context, e.g. a calculation between two or more measures | **Average total cost in Q1 for a new insight in one micro design cycle = $20** |

OKRs are treated separately: "usually an agreed metric value of achieving an individual, team,
or organization's goals based on distinct confidence levels."

Why the vocabulary matters: "in many organizations pure observations, measures and metrics are
often lumped together and used synonymously. This often results in a **colorful mix of miracle
numbers** (both vanity metrics and meaningful measurements) on visually appealing dashboards
that are regularly distributed throughout organizations."

### Well-defined vs ill-defined measurement objects

| | Well-defined | Ill-defined |
|---|---|---|
| Character | "Metrics that have a clear and precise designation and a universally valid definition" | Require making the problem tangible *and* describing the object to be measured |
| Where they live | Mostly EXPLOIT | Mostly EXPLORE |
| Typical examples the book names | Standard financial and operational KPIs | **Purpose · creativity · employee experience · mindset · future skills adoption · brand image** |

**The "call it X" technique** — the chapter's antidote to falling in love with a word:

> "Often the measurement process starts with a predefined word that habitually already has
> certain assumptions associated with it. But instead of falling in love with a word, the
> development of a metric should begin by thinking thoroughly with the team about **what real
> quantity is to be measured.** … To avoid this trap, instead of *mindset* it is recommended to
> simply call it **X as placeholder**, which should stand for something very specific without
> room for interpretation. This way discussions about already existing metrics,
> misinterpretation, and any assumptions can be avoided."

Interrogation sequence for an ill-defined term (worked on "design thinking mindset"): What is it
about this mindset that contributes to better business success? And why? What real-world
behavior is it associated with? What does this behavior look like and when does it happen? Who
is involved? Is the subject to investigate the co-creation with the potential user/customer, or
rather the willingness-to-pay analysis that is central to MVP development?

### Leading vs Lagging Indicators

| | **Lagging indicators** | **Leading indicators** |
|---|---|---|
| Definition | "Metrics used to measure past performance"; "usually take a long time to change and confirm the later-stage results of the innovation efforts and activities" | "Metrics used as a predictive measure of future performance"; "measure the activities the team needs to complete to reach the goal. They can be tracked on a more ongoing basis" |

> "North Star, exploration, and exploitation metrics might be leading or lagging, and very often
> work best when combined."

### Qualitative vs Quantitative Data

| | Qualitative | Quantitative |
|---|---|---|
| **Pros** | Rich data; personal; directional information | Less costly; easier to validate; easier to collect; statistically robust |
| **Cons** | Expensive; difficult to interpret; difficult to make comparisons | Data is limited/missing; lacks depth and can be misunderstood; needs a sufficient sample size |
| **Answers** | The **WHY** | The **WHAT** and **HOW MANY** |

> "Often it is the case that the best data is qualitative data that can be quantified."
> "When selecting measures and metrics for innovation to track, it has proven beneficial to
> focus on metrics that provide a balance of qualitative and quantitative evidence."

### Four Dimension Pairs for Reflecting the Measurement Worldview

| Pair | Pole A | Pole B |
|---|---|---|
| **Logic of action / rationality** | **Causation** — the future is predictable; course of action is goal oriented; decision making selects from alternatives with maximum return and procures the necessary resources; contingencies are avoided by planning from stakeholders performing competitive analysis | **Effectuation** — the future is controllable; course of action is means oriented; decision making selects a satisfying alternative where a possible loss of resources is affordable; contingencies are opportunities to be exploited and stakeholders aim to establish partnerships |
| **Transported imagination of the future** | **Dystopia** — worst-case scenarios based on criticism of current trends, society, norms, systems; innovation teams have strong inner conflict with the system; turning points trigger tendencies to overthink the system and values | **Utopia** — ideal-case scenarios based on the perfect place, state, or condition; teams actively question the current system aiming at positive change; supports recognizing the positive aspects |
| **Activity level of measurements and foresight management** | **Reactive** — wait until a problem becomes obvious; activities triggered to solve it and return to the comfort zone; teams feel stressed, lost, reluctant to act; weak understanding of purpose | **Proactive** — embracing new situations as a challenge and learning opportunity; teams feel confident, strong, in control of the design cycle; purpose driven |
| **Form and actions of exploration** | **Opportunity recognition** — low uncertainty at the beginning; outcomes and probabilities well known; classical market analysis tools and instruments; leads to **incremental improvements** | **Value creation** — high uncertainty at the beginning; outcomes and probabilities unknown; mindset embedded in design thinking and growth mindset; leads to **breakthrough innovation** |

## Key Concepts

- **Two levels of measurement** — "a carefully described object, on the one hand, and, on the
  other hand, productivity as a measure of innovation."
- **Linkage between metrics** — "linkage or connection between metrics is particularly important
  in managing innovation projects, where there is a variety of metrics that define the
  innovation success."
- **The measurement system is itself a wicked problem** — hence design thinking is the right
  approach to designing it: "such a system is a wicked problem, and there is a need for
  appropriate metrics in the organization and respective teams."
- **The five capabilities of designing a measurement system** (the chapter's radar):
  understanding strategic choice (how to build a business, EXPLORE & EXPLOIT) · understanding
  inspiration (why are we passionate about it) · understanding teams and decision makers (what
  they need and want) · ability to experiment and engage the organization/team · ability to
  create a vision with purpose and inspire others · ability to align different teams with the
  same North Star.
- **Include the external world in the internal measurement system.** "Metrics that predict the
  future should always be compared with current macroeconomics studies, outlooks, and
  scenarios." Example given: a recession with high inflation makes **consumer price sentiment**
  a relevant leading indicator for consumer goods, informing whether to raise prices or vary
  package sizes so a 5 or 10 percent increase is less dramatically perceived.
- **The WEF (2022) globalization scenarios** are cited as an external frame: axes of *virtual
  integration* vs *virtual fragmentation* and *physical integration*, with **Globalization 5.0:
  Reconnection** as one of four future scenarios. "Each scenario of the future might have
  massive impact on the configurations of a value proposition or the business ecosystem itself."
  The question the book poses: *"What if the envisioned Globalization 5.0 will not take place as
  predicted?"*

## Mental Models

- **Marginal uncertainty reduction is still valuable.** You do not need certainty; you need less
  uncertainty than you had.
- **"Anything in the innovation space that can be observed should lend itself to some type of
  measurement methods."**
- **Metrics must not limit creativity** "or obscure the goal of completing design challenges and
  projects in the appropriate depth, with the necessary number of iterations and with
  appropriate latitude."
- **Both confidence and measurement literacy are learnable.** "Not only creative confidence can
  be learned, but also the skills that help to lose one's insecurity about measurements."

## Anti-patterns

- **Measuring everything measurable.**
- **Miracle-number dashboards** mixing vanity metrics and meaningful measurements.
- **Falling in love with a word** instead of defining the real quantity behind it.
- **Ignoring macroeconomics** when building predictive metrics.
- **Assuming every company should reach IMS 3.** "Not every company is aiming to reach IMS 3.
  For many organizations and teams, the first steps in EXPLORE are already a major milestone."

## Reference Tables

### Five key questions before measuring anything
| # | Question |
|---|---|
| 1 | **Why** is it important to make a measurement, and what decision could be derived from it? |
| 2 | What are the **consequences** that can be observed when the decisions are implemented? |
| 3 | How much **knowledge** about the problem to be solved exists and is known to the team? |
| 4 | **When and where** does a value or metric make a difference? |
| 5 | How much is the **additional information worth** and what impact will it create? |

These help in "analyzing, applying temporally, or operationalizing a metric."

### The IMS Maturity Model — the book's central progress ladder

| Dimension | **IMS 0 (Experimental)** | **IMS 1 (Professionalized)** | **IMS 2 (Data Driven)** | **IMS 3 (AI Driven)** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Project / Business** | Creating awareness for a balanced EXPLORE and EXPLOIT portfolio; distinguishing between metrics for EXPLORE and EXPLOIT; understanding the impact of value creation for long-term business success | Managing the transformation from EXPLORE to EXPLOIT; balanced leadership attention for EXPLORE and EXPLOIT; established and accepted measures for EXPLORE | Application of advanced forecasting techniques; dedicated leadership for EXPLORE and data analytics; application of the hybrid model (customer led and data led) | Apply AI to drive/support EXPLORE and EXPLOIT; leadership supported by higher machine autonomy; quick pattern recognition and definition of sophisticated measures |
| **Teams / Culture** | Learning about new mindsets, behavior, and culture; review of existing performance measurement systems; building capabilities for EXPLORE and awareness and skills for measuring | Metrics accepted and understood by the team; alignment between North Star and company and team objectives established; innovation metrics are part of the company/team culture | Data/measuring competencies in all functions and teams; coordinated measurement; culture of learning, exploration, and continuous innovation; data driven for innovation and efficiency | AI and humans as a team; addressing new or unknown problems; advanced measurements; improvements based on predictions and advanced prescriptive analytics |
| **Automation** | Limited, with ad hoc analysis; manual data collection; **static** information for decisions | Key metrics are operationalized; automated (**single mode**); **dynamic** information for decisions | High trust in insights from data; automated (**multimodal**); **real-time** information for decisions | AI part of objective setting; automated (**dynamic multimodal**); AI-driven/supported decision |

**Where companies actually are:** "Many companies start at **IMS level 0 or 1** with their
existing measurement systems in the definition of metrics for EXPLORE. Occasionally, global
innovation leading companies with a strong focus on utilizing new enabler technologies have
innovation measurement systems at **level 2**." The IMS "must fit the aspiration level of the
company, its culture, and its business alignment, and should evolve iteratively and dynamically."

## Worked Example

### Deriving a metric from an observation — the chapter's own arithmetic

The chapter walks a single Q1 observation all the way to a metric:

| Step | Content | Value |
|---|---|---|
| **Observation** (in Q1) | Solution tests are being run with customers; money is being spent on testing prototypes | — |
| **Quantification** | Number of solution tests with customers | **98** |
| **Measurement** | Expenses for testing prototypes | **$4,000** |
| **Measurement** | Number of new insights collected | *(counted)* |
| **Metric** | Average total cost in Q1 for a new insight in one micro design cycle | **≈ $20** |

The metric is *"a meaningful derivative of measure"* — a ratio of two measures with a defined
meaning in the innovation context, not a raw count. This is the pattern to replicate: never stop
at the quantification; combine two or more measures until the number carries a decision.

### Combining a lagging and a leading indicator

The chapter's worked pairing:

> **Measuring Design Thinking Macro Cycle Length (lagging) + Average Design Thinking Process Step
> Length (leading)**

The reasoning as the book gives it:

1. **The problem:** the time from problem identification to final prototype is too long, and the
   aim is to reduce the time to a validated final prototype.
2. **The lagging indicator** is therefore *macro cycle length*. But: "Without going through the
   entire design thinking macro cycle, teams and decision makers might not understand how
   already performed capability-building programs, up-skilling initiatives, enlargement of teams
   or other improvements are effective on the macro cycle length." You only learn once, at the
   end.
3. **The leading indicator** is *average design thinking process step length* — which "splits up
   the macro cycle into activities of teams working on a specific process step or even with a
   specific tool." Example: the average length for preparing, engaging with the customer, and
   collecting insights.
4. **What the pair buys you:** "Looking into all activities and micro-process steps individually
   enables teams to identify **bottlenecks** that extend the overall time from finding the
   appropriate problem to solving it."

This is the chapter's general recipe: pick the lagging indicator that names the outcome you
care about, then decompose it into the leading indicators you can move this week.

## Key Takeaways

1. Observation → quantification → measurement → **metric**. A metric is a derivative of two or
   more measures with defined business meaning.
2. Measure to reduce quantitative uncertainty; marginal reduction still pays.
3. For ill-defined objects (purpose, creativity, mindset, brand image), replace the word with
   **X** and define the real quantity.
4. Answer the five key questions before measuring anything.
5. Pair leading with lagging indicators — decompose the lagging outcome into movable leading
   steps.
6. Balance qualitative (WHY) and quantitative (WHAT / HOW MANY) evidence.
7. Locate yourself on IMS 0–3 and evolve iteratively; IMS 3 is not the goal for everyone.
