# Chapter 3 — The Role of Enterprise Architecture Practice

## Core Idea
The need for enterprise architecture is **fundamental, not incidental** — a natural organizational reaction to extensive IT landscapes and the chronic difficulty of business–IT communication. An EA practice is, above all, **a communication practice**. Its benefits are real but qualitative and cannot be reduced to ROI; like accounting or HRM it is a "necessary evil". This chapter also delivers the book's central polemic: modern EA best practices **emerged in the industry** and have essentially nothing to do with EA frameworks such as TOGAF, Zachman, FEAF and DoDAF, which are "proven anti-patterns".

## Frameworks Introduced

### All EA elements serve communication (Figure 3.1)
| Element | What aspect of communication it addresses |
|---|---|
| EA artifacts (especially dual ones) | Underpin conversations, reduce oral ambiguity, document agreements, give decisions durable representation |
| Structured EA-related processes | Invite the right parties at the right moments; ensure everyone's vital interests are satisfied |
| Formal governance arrangements | Ensure decisions are widely communicated, clearly understood, explicitly approved, officially sanctioned |
| Software tools | Facilitate creation, discussion, distribution and presentation of artifacts |
| Standardized modeling languages/notations | Enable unequivocal interpretation of diagrams |
| Architects themselves | Conductors of communication, negotiators of trade-offs |

### Benefits chain (Figure 3.2)
Use of EA artifacts for communication, balanced decision-making and disciplined planning → **direct benefits** (efficiency and effectiveness of IT investments, reduced costs, risks, complexity, duplication, legacy) → **indirect benefits** (operational excellence, customer intimacy, product leadership, agility, speed-to-market, managerial satisfaction).

## Key Concepts
- **Face-to-face is required.** Complex, interpretable organizational issues need rich media. Email and instant messaging cannot carry significant planning decisions; phone and video conferencing still cannot fully substitute physical presence. Also, much knowledge and concern (e.g. leaders' intuitive visions) can never be fully explicated. EA artifacts **complement but never substitute** open verbal communication.
- **Who practices EA**: seemingly all organizations employing more than **30–50 IT specialists** where IT supports main business activities. Practiced across banking, agriculture, insurance, retail, high-tech, oil, and in hospitals, universities, police departments, governments, ministries, agencies, customs. Almost two-thirds of large organizations practiced EA in 2010.
- **No ROI for EA.** An architecture function is a cost center adding no direct value to the value chain; EA is a continuous activity, not a project. It is "arguably impossible to quantitatively evaluate the precise contribution of an EA practice to the bottom line". There cannot be a business case for an EA practice in the classical sense — just as there cannot be for accounting or HRM.
- **Why EA will matter more**: three trends — IT is getting more sophisticated and harder for business people to understand; business dependence on IT keeps increasing; the innovative potential of IT for business keeps growing.

## Mental Models
- **The accounting/HRM analogy.** You cannot build a large successful organization by writing annual sales on napkins (no accounting), by hiring random people (no HRM), or by implementing arbitrary IT systems ad hoc (no EA). Large organizations value EA "not because it offers some specific extra benefits, but because it allows these organizations to change and evolve in a controlled manner."
- **Wars and medicine.** EA frameworks contributed to real EA best practices "only in the same sense in which wars contributed to the development of modern medicine" — they stimulated or forced development without providing any particularly useful ideas.

## Anti-patterns — "What Enterprise Architecture Practice Is Not" (Kotusev's twelve)
1. **Not purely technical planning** — not IT architecture; must integrate with business planning.
2. **Not a one-size-fits-all methodology** — no replicable step-by-step approach; practices are idiosyncratic in their details and must be established in-house and continuously fine-tuned. This book is descriptive, not prescriptive.
3. **Not automated planning** — artifacts facilitate but never automate planning; humans still do the planning.
4. **Not a substitute for competence** — cannot turn incompetent executives, IT leaders or specialists into competent ones.
5. **Not a work of dedicated experts** — plans produced by an isolated group of architects "typically end up lying on shelves".
6. **Not a one-time planning project** — the ongoing process of planning and communication matters more than the plans; discrete or intermittent bursts of planning have limited efficacy.
7. **Not a technology-specific practice** — technology-agnostic, vendor-neutral; focus is people, not technology (which is why EA best practices evolve slower than technology).
8. **Not enterprise modeling** — modeling is a narrow, relatively insignificant part; artifacts should be *adequate for their purpose*, not perfectly correct; "modeling for the sake of modeling is a useless activity"; sophisticated notations are barely understandable to business.
9. **Not enterprise engineering** — no rigorous analysis-synthesis, no formal blueprints, no sophisticated calculations; a "soft" organic planning approach relying on pragmatic documents, informal discussions and quick approximations. Enterprise engineering is "a utopian idea unfit for the real world".
10. **Not systems thinking** — thinking is solitary; misalignment requires cooperation, not better thinking. EA "is mostly about communicating and doing, rather than thinking."
11. **Not a breakthrough solution** — expect "a modest but sound and lasting effect", not order-of-magnitude improvements.
12. **Not an implementation of EA frameworks** — successful practices never fill the cells of the Zachman Framework, never follow the TOGAF ADM steps, never develop the heaps of TOGAF-recommended artifacts, even in organizations on The Open Group's own TOGAF user list; nor do they follow the general sequential logic of "plan the future state, then implement it".

## Reference Tables

### Direct benefits of using specific EA artifacts
| Benefit | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| Improved effectiveness of IT investments | Focus investment on the most strategically important business areas |
| Improved efficiency of IT investments | Estimate short-term business value; ensure positive financial returns |
| Reduced costs of IT operations | Limit the number of supported technologies, products and vendors |
| Reduced technical and compliance risks | Consistent proven implementation approaches, legislative compliance |
| Reduced complexity of the IT landscape | Reduce technology and approach diversity, untangle the landscape |
| Increased reuse of available IT assets | Manage and reuse existing systems, platforms, databases |
| Reduced duplicated and legacy systems | Identify duplication, manage asset lifecycle, decommission in time |
| Increased agility of IT planning | Plan landscape changes, explore options, fit solutions into the environment |
| Increased speed of project delivery | Accelerate delivery, minimize unnecessary delays |
| Improved quality of project delivery | Consistent, smooth, predictable delivery meeting business and architectural requirements |
| Improved overall conceptual consistency | Avoid inconsistent or incompatible decisions |

### Historical lineage of architecture-based planning methodologies
| Era | Methodology |
|---|---|
| 1970s | Business Systems Planning (BSP), Method/1 |
| 1980s | Information Engineering, Strategic Data/Information Planning |
| 1990s | Enterprise Architecture Planning (EAP), TAFIM |
| 2000s | FEAF |
| 2010s onward | TOGAF |

All are "slightly different variations of the same step-wise formal planning approach": analyze current IT support, define the required future architecture aligned with strategic goals, produce a migration roadmap.

## Worked Example
**The Federal Enterprise Architecture (FEA) failure.** "Seemingly inspired by the advice of 'leading industry experts', the U.S. Federal Government in 1999 initiated the infamous Federal Enterprise Architecture (FEA) program to develop enterprise architectures for all government agencies, which eventually delivered only the heaps of largely useless architectural documents and reportedly wasted more than one billion dollars." The consequence: "the concept of architecture has been largely discredited and even the very word 'architecture' subsequently became a bad word in many organizations."

## Key Takeaways
- EA is a communication practice; every element of it exists to improve communication and decision-making.
- Benefits are qualitative; do not attempt to build an ROI business case for the practice itself.
- Applicable from ~30–50 IT staff upward, industry-agnostic.
- EA frameworks are marketing-driven management fads and should be treated as proven anti-patterns.
