# Chapter 1 — Introduction

## Core Idea
Modern organizations are socio-technical systems of business and IT that can only be changed synchronously. The persistent failure to change them coherently is called business and IT misalignment, and its **root cause is miscommunication** between diverse groups of decision-makers — not bad technology and not bad strategy. Enterprise architecture (EA) is defined here, for the first time in the book, as **a collection of special documents that helps establish effective communication between all relevant actors involved in strategic decision-making and implementation of IT systems**.

## Frameworks Introduced

### People / Process / Technology
Realizing business value from an information system is never equivalent to installing hardware and software. It requires coordinated, harmonized change in three aspects:
- **People** — roles, skills, responsibilities, motivation, compensation, attitude, culture.
- **Process** — routine tasks, operating procedures, regulations, information requirements, decision rights, autonomy of performers.
- **Technology** — software, hardware, infrastructure, support, access management, security, monitoring, maintenance.

### The three boundaries (Figure 1.3)
All actors relevant to alignment split along three axes of diversity:
1. **Business actors vs. IT actors** — disparate knowledge and backgrounds.
2. **Strategic actors vs. tactical actors** — disparate planning horizons and scopes.
3. **Internal actors vs. external actors** — disparate perspectives (organization-specific knowledge vs. industry best practice).

### Five groups of actors
| Group | Who | Their questions |
|---|---|---|
| Business executives | CEO, C-level, heads of business units — global strategic planning and investment decisions | What is our business strategy? What are our core capabilities? What are our competitive advantages? |
| IT executives | CIO, IT directors, heads of delivery/support | What is our IT strategy? What are our IT assets and capabilities? What new technologies can we leverage? |
| Business unit managers | Sales managers, marketing specialists, product designers running daily processes | What are our business processes? How are we running them? How can we optimize them? |
| IT project teams | Project managers, business analysts, developers, infrastructure engineers | What projects are we delivering? On time? Are we satisfying business requirements? |
| Third parties | Consultants, vendors, outsourcers delivering per industry best practice | What IT services are we offering? What are the industry best practices? How do we apply them for clients? |

## Key Concepts
- **Business and IT alignment** — IT goals, plans and systems are consistent with business goals, plans and processes.
- **The "big brain" ideal** — the organization acting as one entity able to make globally *and* locally optimal decisions on all available information. No single actor can do this: individuals are bounded by expertise and cognitive bias, and authoritative managers cannot force decisions whose rationality is not understood ("resisted, sabotaged or simulated").
- **Causal chain (Figure 1.4)**: Poor communication → inadequate IT planning (stakeholder interests neglected) → business and IT misalignment (wasted IT investments, disappointment in IT, reduced business performance).
- **Reverse chain (Figure 1.5)**: Use of EA → effective communication → adequate IT planning → business and IT alignment.
- **Competitive advantage from IT** in three areas: operational excellence and cost leadership; product differentiation and leadership; customer intimacy and focus.

## Mental Models
- **Alignment is a communication problem, not a modeling problem.** Everything else in the book follows from this.
- **Every EA document is a translation device.** The same document tells business actors the business impact, IT actors the IT implications; strategic actors the long-term consequences, tactical actors the short-term outcomes; internal actors the industry best practice, external actors the internal specifics.
- **Alignment is intractable, not solvable.** It is an inherent, natural problem of communicating between heterogeneous groups with conflicting objectives — it can be reduced, never eliminated.

## Anti-patterns
- Treating an IT project as a technology installation and skipping the people/process changes.
- Making planning decisions on behalf of stakeholders instead of with them.
- Delegating IT control wholly to IT managers — in the 21st century it is a direct responsibility of business executives.

## Reference Tables

### Symptoms of poor business and IT alignment (Kotusev's list)
- IT projects uplift the least important business capabilities
- IT projects address urgent local needs but do not move the organization towards its strategic vision
- Business executives do not understand where the IT budget is spent, what IT is delivering and when
- Different business units implement the same business processes in different ways
- Business units cannot access the data they need
- The most commercially sensitive data is stored in the least secure IT systems
- Fragile legacy systems threaten critical business operations
- Frequent outages caused by unproven technologies or approaches
- The IT landscape is overly complicated, inflexible and difficult to change
- Each new IT project introduces a new expensive vendor product or technology
- Available platforms are not leveraged; all IT systems are implemented from scratch
- Established best practices are not reused; each project "reinvents the wheel"
- The IT budget is spent supporting duplicated or redundant systems

### What every IT project should achieve
Fulfils local short-term needs · contributes to global long-term goals · implemented predictably, cost-effectively, risk-free · leverages and reuses available IT assets · does not create redundant assets · can itself be leveraged later · built on technologies the organization plans to keep · implemented consistently with similar projects · does not introduce complexity beyond necessity.

## Worked Example
**IT spending figures Kotusev uses to motivate the book.** In the United States, private business investment in IT (hardware, software, communications equipment) grew from under \$100 billion in 1980 to more than \$500 billion in 2010; IT as a proportion of total capital investment grew from 32% in 1980 to 52% in 2010. Worldwide IT spending grows 3–4% per year and rose from about \$3.4 trillion in 2016 to more than \$3.7 trillion in 2019. Average IT budgets as a percentage of revenue rose almost 50% between 2005 and 2019 — from 3.60% to 5.38% of revenue, a CAGR of about 3%. Moore's law (1965) — microprocessor computing power doubles roughly every eighteen months; the law of mass digital storage — storage per dollar doubles roughly every fifteen months.

## Key Takeaways
- EA is a *collection of documents for communication*, not a blueprint of the enterprise.
- Misalignment is caused by three boundaries separating five groups of actors.
- The value of IT is realized only through harmonized people, process and technology change.
- IT management and planning is a business executive responsibility, not a delegated IT job.
