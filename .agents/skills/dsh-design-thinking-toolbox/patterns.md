# Patterns — The Design Thinking Toolbox

The book's reusable moves, stated as **When / How / Trade-offs**. They are grouped by the part of practice they belong to. Every pattern is drawn from the book's own tool pages and expert tips, not from general design thinking literature.

---

## A. Framing the work

### Pattern 1 — Calibrate the problem statement to the resources
- **When** At the start of any project, and again whenever the team is either paralysed by scope or bored by triviality.
- **How** Write ten or more candidate problem sentences. Analyse each with W+H questions; name a specific actor and the context. Assemble the final sentence **in reverse order** (context → actor → problem → HMW form). Test it against two rules: (1) broad enough to allow creative freedom to unfold; (2) narrow enough to be solvable with existing resources — team size, time, budget. Expect multiple rounds; "the joy in precise formulation and choice of words is a crucial factor."
- **Trade-offs** Time spent here feels like time not spent solving. Too broad and the team wanders; too narrow and you have prescribed the solution. A design brief adds precision at the direct cost of creative leeway — take it only when the client can supply real detail.

### Pattern 2 — Make the brief its own design thinking project
- **When** The client has little design thinking expertise but wants a detailed brief.
- **How** Rather than accepting a bad brief or refusing one, run a small cycle to produce it — design space and scope, existing approaches, design principles, scenarios, milestones, implementation challenges.
- **Trade-offs** Adds a cycle before the "real" work starts; buys a brief the client actually owns.

### Pattern 3 — Agree what success means before you go looking for it
- **When** Before the solution space, with decision makers in the room.
- **How** Run "define success" (eight questions, 60–90 min, 4–10 people including decision makers), vote on the core elements, and set milestones. Reuse the result later as the implementation milestone check.
- **Trade-offs** Requires senior calendars early, which is exactly why teams skip it — and exactly why late-stage pitches then fail on criteria nobody agreed.

## B. Learning about people

### Pattern 4 — Go where the user is, and split observation from interpretation
- **When** Any time the team is reasoning about users from inside a meeting room.
- **How** Use AEIOU (activities, environment, interaction, objects, users) for structured field notes; use peers observing peers with a sheet that physically separates *what was observed* from *what we think it means*. Cluster into patterns afterwards.
- **Trade-offs** Field work costs hours to days per observation. The split sheet slows note-taking but is the only reliable defence against recording your own conclusions as data.

### Pattern 5 — Recruit at the extremes
- **When** The mainstream user gives you only confirmations of what you already believed.
- **How** Find extreme users and lead users; study their **workarounds**, which are latent needs made visible. Feed the findings back into the persona.
- **Trade-offs** Extremes can pull the solution toward a niche. The book's guard is to sharpen the persona with the finding rather than to redefine the target group.

### Pattern 6 — Ask why until it hurts
- **When** Any answer that sounds like a preference rather than a cause; also during test feedback.
- **How** Ask 5x why, 2 people, 30–40 min, recording each rung. Repeat it inside interviews, feedback capture grids and retrospectives.
- **Trade-offs** Feels interrogative; in the test phase the book softens it into "listen even more closely and find out why."

## C. Ideation and selection

### Pattern 7 — Separate ideation from evaluation, structurally
- **When** Every ideation session, and especially where hierarchy or extroversion would distort the room.
- **How** Generate first, judge later, using tools that enforce it: brainstorming rules (no criticism, quantity before quality, build on others), 6-3-5 done **silently**, dot voting done **privately and simultaneously with the boss last**.
- **Trade-offs** Silence removes cross-talk, which is also where some good combinations come from — hence the book pairs silent 6-3-5 with a spoken clustering round afterwards.

### Pattern 8 — Cap the session, then change the stimulus
- **When** Roughly 5–15 minutes into a brainstorming session, or as soon as ideas start repeating.
- **How** "After a certain amount of time, creativity declines and must be rekindled by new incentives." Switch to special brainstorming — negative brainstorming, figuring storming, bodystorming — or to analogies.
- **Trade-offs** Frequent switching can feel gimmicky; the book positions special brainstorming explicitly as the *unsticking* move, not the default.

### Pattern 9 — Read the empty quadrant
- **When** Any 2x2 prioritization.
- **How** After placing all ideas, look for quadrants with nothing in them: "they signify potential for further opportunities and unfulfilled needs." Also check the development fields when fewer than three ideas land top-right.
- **Trade-offs** Requires SMART axes; not every axis pair yields target-oriented results, so expect to re-run with different axes.

### Pattern 10 — Park, don't discard
- **When** Ideas that are exciting but off-problem, and losing ideas after a vote.
- **How** Keep a visible parking lot. Return to it when the team stalls: "if the team does not make good headway, the parking lot is a good place to get inspiration." Never criticize an idea out of existence — "we might need it again later."
- **Trade-offs** A parking lot can become a graveyard that nobody revisits; schedule the revisit rather than trusting it will happen.

### Pattern 11 — Transfer pains, not features, via analogy
- **When** Ill-defined and wicked problems where direct benchmarking gives only incremental answers.
- **How** Find a world — another industry, animals, organizations — that has already solved *your pain*, then port the mechanism back. Absurd distance ("How would an elephant solve the problem?") is a feature.
- **Trade-offs** Expensive: research, attribute identification and expert discussion "often require several hours."

## D. Prototyping

### Pattern 12 — Keep the resolution as low as the question allows
- **When** Every prototype, but especially the first ones.
- **How** One or two functions per low-resolution prototype. Build several small experiments rather than one large one. Use embarrassment as the timing signal: "If you aren't ashamed of your prototype, you have invested too much time in it."
- **Trade-offs** Low resolution buys honest desirability feedback but cannot answer feasibility or pricing questions — which is why the solution interview later demands a high-resolution prototype.

### Pattern 13 — Attach a written assumption to every prototype
- **When** Before building anything.
- **How** "Prototype to test": state what you are testing and why before you start; think in **variants** rather than "building away like mad"; criticize the prototype only *after* the test.
- **Trade-offs** Slows the first build; speeds every subsequent iteration by preventing prototypes that answer no question.

### Pattern 14 — Show, don't tell
- **When** Every test, demonstration and pitch.
- **How** Hand the artifact over. Separate any context explanation in time from the interaction. In pitches, show the prototype or run a role-play instead of describing it.
- **Trade-offs** Testers will misuse the prototype — which is the data you came for, and also uncomfortable to watch.

### Pattern 15 — Record the delta, not the position
- **When** Placing experiments on the exploration map.
- **How** Mark where you expected the experiment to land *and* where it actually landed; the distance between them is the measured gain in knowledge. Spend little time on precise positioning — the map is rough orientation.
- **Trade-offs** Tempting to treat as a measuring instrument; the book warns against exactly that.

## E. Testing

### Pattern 16 — Plan roles and verification criteria before the test
- **When** Every test, including informal ones.
- **How** Fix the location (ideally the user's premises), the sequence, and who asks / who documents / who observes. Define in advance "the criteria for a thesis to be considered as verified."
- **Trade-offs** Adds preparation to tests that "could just be done"; without it, teams argue afterwards about whether a hypothesis passed.

### Pattern 17 — Listen, watch, don't sell
- **When** Every user interaction in the test phase.
- **How** Let the tester see and experience the prototype first. Ask them to think aloud. Do not explain, do not demonstrate, do not ask questions at first, and never use leading questions. If a user gets stuck, ask what should happen next rather than helping.
- **Trade-offs** Sessions feel slow and awkward for the team; the alternative is data contaminated by your own framing.

### Pattern 18 — Match the question set to the prototype's resolution
- **When** Preparing an experience test.
- **How** Lo-fi questions probe the problem ("What is the problem your idea solves? How do users solve it today?"); med-fi questions probe the concept and the sequence ("What features are missing? If the user had a magic wand, what would he change?"); hi-fi questions probe behaviour ("Is the navigation sensible and intuitive? How likely is it that the user recommends it?").
- **Trade-offs** Requires knowing which resolution you actually have — teams routinely ask hi-fi questions of lo-fi artifacts and conclude the idea is bad.

### Pattern 19 — Ask for a comparison instead of a verdict
- **When** Feedback on a single prototype is vague or polite.
- **How** A/B testing: two variants of the *same* prototype differing in exactly one property; one assumption per test; criteria fixed in advance; quantitative (split the user group) or qualitative (everyone sees both).
- **Trade-offs** Only answers "which of these two"; it cannot tell you that both are wrong.

### Pattern 20 — Uniform conditions when you need to compare
- **When** Several variants or several testers must be judged against each other.
- **How** Structured usability testing: defined tasks, same information to every test person, a dry run beforehand, an uninvolved moderator, and a success / struggle / fail rating per task.
- **Trade-offs** Costs setup time and an extra person; loses the exploratory looseness that early tests need.

### Pattern 21 — Document so that non-attendees can act
- **When** Every test, since "not all team members have to be included."
- **How** Quotes verbatim, photos, and preferably short videos of the most important statements; ask permission first and sign a data use agreement if the material will be reused. Cluster the collected grids by answer and by persona.
- **Trade-offs** Filming changes behaviour and adds consent overhead; without it, findings degrade into the memory of whoever was in the room.

## F. Reflection

### Pattern 22 — Make criticism sayable with fixed sentence stems
- **When** Any feedback round, especially in sensitive projects or across hierarchy.
- **How** Permit only "I like… / I wish… / I wonder…", optionally plus "What if…?" and "I learned". Each participant gets at least three Post-its, **reads them aloud before sticking them**, and the recipient does not open a discussion. Say thank you.
- **Trade-offs** Suppresses immediate dialogue by design; the tool trades depth of debate for a mood in which people keep giving feedback at all.

### Pattern 23 — Retro every iteration, not just at the end
- **When** After each iteration, at milestones, and after a project. Even a two-person workshop post-mortem qualifies.
- **How** Sailboat: wind (what pushed us) · anchor (what slowed us) · cliffs (risks outside our control) · island (shared vision). Read Post-its aloud, cluster, **dig for causes rather than symptoms**, define concrete measures, close with feedback on the retro itself so the group parts with good feelings.
- **Trade-offs** 60–120 minutes per iteration. Teams that know each other well skip it — "That's a pity." A major retro needs an external moderator, which costs more still.

### Pattern 24 — Wait out a bad mood before a lessons learned session
- **When** The project ended badly.
- **How** Postpone roughly **six weeks**: "seen from a greater distance, many issues can be looked at without stirring up too many emotions."
- **Trade-offs** Memory decays, and log books kept during the project become essential rather than optional.

### Pattern 25 — Reflect at two levels
- **When** Any lessons learned session.
- **How** Project level ("what went well, which methods did we apply, what must change, what are the key learnings?") *and* meta level ("what does design thinking mean to me, how does it differ from and combine with other approaches, where else could it apply?"). Consolidate the meta level across teams to adjust the design principles.
- **Trade-offs** The meta level feels abstract mid-project; it is what makes the learning transferable and is best kept for the end.

### Pattern 26 — Define few actions and emphasize what worked
- **When** Closing any reflection session.
- **How** "Less is more": emphasize the things that proved successful *and* those that failed, then define a small number of measures. Do not focus only on the negative and do not get personal.
- **Trade-offs** A short action list leaves real problems unaddressed; a long one gets ignored entirely.

## G. Carrying results outward

### Pattern 27 — Package the idea before you ask for anything
- **When** End of an iteration, and at intervals with stakeholders.
- **How** NABC for a one-page package; the seven-block pitch (entry story · problem · customers · solution · business model · next steps · summary) for a presentation. Story first, key figures over words, pictures over words, videos over pictures, prototype over slides. Repeat two or three key messages at the end because that is all a listener retains.
- **Trade-offs** "The shorter the pitch, the more difficult it is to prepare" — an elevator pitch costs more preparation per minute than a long one.

### Pattern 28 — Test the riskiest assumption first
- **When** Any time a lean canvas is complete enough to read.
- **How** Iterate blocks 1–5 (problem, customer segments, value proposition, solution, existing alternatives) until stable; colour-code Post-its by risk (pink = high risk, test quickly; yellow = medium; green = tested or low). Then run experiments on the riskiest assumptions rather than the easiest.
- **Trade-offs** The riskiest assumption is usually the one the team most wants to be true — this pattern is a discipline, not a convenience.

### Pattern 29 — Keep the canvas honest by counting Post-its
- **When** Reviewing a lean canvas.
- **How** Aim for 3–4 Post-its per field, one statement each. **Too many = lack of focus. Empty or nearly empty = needs work.** Photograph after each iteration; go electronic only when pivots become rare.
- **Trade-offs** A crude heuristic that punishes genuinely complex segments; useful precisely because it is crude.

### Pattern 30 — Ask "who" before "how" in implementation
- **When** Building the road map for implementation, as early as possible.
- **How** "Who needs the solution? Who is the user/customer? Who will build it? Whom do we need for the implementation? Who pays? Who is funding the project?" Position every implementer on the map, since you may need to win them over later. If you cannot name them all, the goal is not clear yet.
- **Trade-offs** Names create political exposure early; the alternative is discovering the missing sponsor after the pitch.

### Pattern 31 — Coordinate use case, industrial case and business case
- **When** Preparing an implementation decision.
- **How** Cover all three on the road map and make sure they agree with one another; structure the accompanying pitch as NABC, adapted to what the specific decision makers need and to "what kinds of 'stories' are 'in' at the moment."
- **Trade-offs** Tailoring to decision makers shades into telling them what they want to hear; the prototype and the test data are the anchor.

## H. Portfolio and scaling

### Pattern 32 — Start the funnel in problem identification mode
- **When** Building or reforming an innovation portfolio.
- **How** Do not filter many ideas through gates. Enter through problems, fed by two inputs: **extrapolation** (today's customers, technologies, trend reports, expert interviews) and **retropolation** (choose a positive, profitable future scenario, ask "how might our company make a maximum contribution to it?", stay in the future while reasoning, then infer what to build now).
- **Trade-offs** Retropolation has no near-term evidence to check it against; the book pairs it with extrapolation rather than choosing between them.

### Pattern 33 — Keep dead initiatives on the board
- **When** Any project is discontinued.
- **How** Leave it in the funnel. Examine the causes at regular intervals, e.g. in a retrospective. Split the funnel into existing business (A) and future fields (B) so the balance of investment is visible. Update monthly and discuss resource allocation on that basis.
- **Trade-offs** A visible record of failures is politically uncomfortable and is exactly what makes the pattern valuable.

### Pattern 34 — Scale by granting resources, autonomy and permission to fail
- **When** Trying to spread design thinking through an organization.
- **How** The kickbox model: give every employee a RedBox (20% of time over 2 months plus a small budget and expert access), gate to a BlueBox (prototype and experiment with real customers, CHF 10,000–30,000), gate to a GoldBox (full-time, CHF 100,000–500,000, spin-off or internal department). The idea owner stays throughout as "CEO". Secure C-level support, ideally from the CEO. Let intrapreneurs negotiate their own time budget — "it makes them all the more motivated."
- **Trade-offs** Bottom-up ideation generates volume that needs sponsors and scoring; without a scoreboard following lean start-up logic, the gates become politics.

### Pattern 35 — Package methods as cards with examples
- **When** Distributing a method set across an organization (the Siemens co-creation toolbox).
- **How** Study which tools were actually used most frequently and most successfully in your company. Describe each with what / why on the front, how plus **an application example** on the back. Colour-code by process. Supply matching templates. Provide both physically and digitally, and train people before the project starts.
- **Trade-offs** Card sets invite cookbook use; the counterweight is coaching and the standing instruction to adapt.

### Pattern 36 — Empower teams of teams rather than coordinating them
- **When** Several teams work the same problem space (ME310, multi-team workshops).
- **How** Give each team freedom to act and decide without the consent of the others; rely on network thinking to disseminate results. "If each team has the freedom to act without the consent of the other teams, the decisions are better balanced." Target a "Wow!" moment, not an acknowledgement.
- **Trade-offs** Duplicated effort and divergent directions — accepted deliberately, because hierarchy filters information and cannot respond as fast.

### Pattern 37 — Let the target group answer questions about the target group
- **When** Designing for a demographic you are not part of.
- **How** Deploy a Young Innovators community (18–25) with full self-organization and let them pitch their own results. Formulate the challenge's intent clearly — young people "always want an answer to the 'Why?' question" — grant freedom within the design principles, and close every challenge with an IL/IW/IW round.
- **Trade-offs** You may get your thesis rejected outright; the Snapchat case exists in the book precisely to show that as the success condition.

### Pattern 38 — Apply the mindset to yourself first
- **When** Establishing a new mindset in an organization, or coaching individuals through a transition.
- **How** "Design thinking life": run the normal cycle enriched with **acceptance** (distinguish solvable problems from facts) and **self-reflection**. Explore values, thinking preferences, environment and flow activities; hypothesize; design paths; test them; evaluate; implement in small steps; reflect regularly. 6–8 weeks per cycle.
- **Trade-offs** Emotionally loaded — the moderator must be prepared for emotional outbursts. Cannot be mandated top-down; it works by example and by granted freedom.

## I. Facilitation

### Pattern 39 — Prepare as long as you facilitate
- **When** Every workshop.
- **How** Budget preparation time equal to the workshop itself. Stay neutral: moderate, do not contribute content. Allow at least 15 m² of space per group of four. Work standing up — sitting slides the group into "conference mode."
- **Trade-offs** The neutrality rule removes the facilitator's domain expertise from the content, deliberately.

### Pattern 40 — Adapt the tool, always
- **When** Every use of every tool in the book.
- **How** "Love it, change it, or leave it." Tool descriptions are a guide, not a recipe. Match the tool to the setting, the problem statement and the participants; keep an extra warm-up and an alternative tool ready in your mind.
- **Trade-offs** A detailed inflexible agenda plus wrong tools at the wrong time produces both a bad workshop *and* no viable solution — after which the team resists design thinking permanently. That is the risk this pattern exists to avoid.

### Pattern 41 — Photograph the wall
- **When** The end of any session that produced Post-its.
- **How** Photograph the walls; Post-its "easily get lost in the heat of the moment." The same applies to the lean canvas after each iteration and the road map once drawn.
- **Trade-offs** None worth the name — this is the cheapest pattern in the book.
