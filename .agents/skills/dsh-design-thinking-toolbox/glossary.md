# Glossary — The Design Thinking Toolbox

Terms, tools and named concepts as the book uses them. Chapter references point to the chapter file that covers the term in depth; page numbers are the book's own.

**2x2 matrix** — Prioritization and pattern-finding grid; any two meaningful axes (e.g. VALUE × EFFORT, "cool" × "doable"). 2–8 people, 15–45 min, ~30–60 s per idea. Empty quadrants signal unmet needs (Ch 6, p. 155).

**5W+H questions** — Who, What, When, Where, Why, How — the six-column analysis grid used to open up a problem or build an interview question set. 3–5 people, 30–60 min (Ch 3, p. 71).

**6-3-5 method** — See *brainwriting*.

**AARRR (pirate metrics)** — Dave McClure's KPI set recommended for the lean canvas's key-figures block (Ch 9, p. 253).

**A/B testing** — Comparing two variants of *one* prototype that differ in a single property, quantitatively (user group split x%/y%) or qualitatively (all users see both). 1–2 people, 5–15 min (Ch 8, p. 233).

**Acceptance (phase in DTL)** — One of the two steps "design thinking life" adds to the standard cycle: accepting facts that are not solvable problems (Ch 9, p. 289).

**AEIOU** — Observation framework: Activities, Environment, Interaction, Objects, Users. Structured field notes clustered into patterns. 1–2 observers, 60 min – 24 hrs (Ch 4, p. 107).

**Analogies & benchmarking** — Drawing inspiration from another industry, animals, persons or organizations; works by transferring *pains*, not features. Signature prompt: "How would an elephant solve the problem?" 3–6 people, 30–120 min (Ch 6, p. 171).

**Analysis questions builder** — Deriving 3–5 factors of influence and turning them into W+H analysis questions. 2–5 people, 30–60 min per question (Ch 4, p. 111).

**Anchor (retrospective)** — The sailboat field for what slowed the team down (Ch 9, p. 243).

**Ask 5x why** — Root-cause laddering by asking "why?" five times. 2 people, 30–40 min (Ch 3, p. 67).

**Bandwagon effect** — The distortion dot voting is designed to prevent: private, simultaneous voting; the boss votes last (Ch 6, p. 160).

**Beginner's mind** — Free of prejudice about how something works, free of expectations, curious, open to possibility, willing to fail early and often (Ch 1).

**Bingo** — Warm-up in which participants find people matching card categories. 7–15 min (Ch 2, p. 42).

**BlueBox** — Second Swisscom kickbox stage: prototyping and testing, >20% of time over 4–6 months, CHF 10,000–30,000 (Ch 9, p. 277).

**Blue ocean tool** — Differentiation framework using the four actions (eliminate, reduce, raise, create) plus the buyer utility map. 4–6 people, 30–120 min (Ch 6, p. 181).

**Bodystorming** — Special brainstorming variant placing testers physically in a situation to infer ideas by trial and error (e.g. Vaseline on glasses to simulate ageing eyes) (Ch 6, p. 168).

**Brain dump** — A preliminary brainstorming round letting participants get existing solutions out of their heads before working on the actual problem (Ch 6, p. 151).

**Brainstorming** — Volume-first ideation from one HMW question. 4–6 people, 5–15 min; quantity before quality; no criticism (Ch 6, p. 151).

**Brainwriting (6-3-5)** — Silent structured ideation: 6 participants × 3 ideas × 5 further rounds; up to 108 ideas in under 30 minutes. 3–5 min per round (Ch 6, p. 163).

**Business ecosystem design** — The stage between product/market fit and scale in the innovation funnel, where multiple actors are arranged to create value together; realized through an MVE (Ch 9, p. 263).

**Business model canvas** — The alternative to the lean canvas; the book recommends switching to it only once the lean canvas is valid and cost-structure optimization becomes the priority (Ch 9, p. 251).

**Buyer utility map** — Blue ocean matrix crossing buyer experience stages with utility levers to locate untapped value (Ch 6, p. 181).

**CEP — critical experience prototype** — Very low-resolution prototype testing one single experience, in the problem space (Ch 7, p. 188).

**CFP — critical function prototype** — Very low-resolution prototype testing one single function, in the problem space (Ch 7, p. 188).

**Clapping game** — Warm-up for positive mood; learning objective is handling mistakes (Ch 2, p. 41).

**Cliffs (retrospective)** — Sailboat field for hazards and risks the team has no power over — market, technologies, competitors (Ch 9, p. 243).

**Clustering** — Grouping Post-its by affinity at intervals during and after ideation (Ch 6).

**Co-creation** — Developing solutions together with stakeholders to raise innovation level, accelerate development and minimize risk; the frame for the Siemens toolbox (Ch 9, p. 273).

**Co-creation toolbox (Siemens)** — 65 method cards, colour-coded to either the industrial design thinking process or the customer value co-creation process, with matching templates, physical and digital (Ch 9, p. 273).

**Context mapping** — Categorizing everything surrounding the user's problem; deliberately leave 1–2 fields open. 2–4 people, 40–60 min (Ch 5, p. 133).

**Convergent phase** — The narrowing half of a cycle: higher-resolution, more specific prototypes (Ch 1).

**Critical items diagram** — Eight critical elements (four experiences, four functions) that a solution must get right; feeds CEP/CFP prototypes directly. Whole team, 30–60 min (Ch 5, p. 145).

**Customer journey map** — Eight-layer before/during/after map of touchpoints with an emotion curve. 4–6 people, 120–240 min (Ch 4, p. 103).

**Dark horse prototype** — Very low-resolution, deliberately radical prototype in the problem space; often realized as Wizard of Oz / mechanical Turk or video (Ch 7, p. 189).

**Dark zone** — The far, risky region of the exploration map, entered deliberately once the team knows the comfort zone (Ch 7, p. 198).

**Define point of view** — Third phase: evaluate, interpret and weight findings into a synthesis (Ch 5).

**Define success** — Eight-question canvas run with decision makers present to agree what success means and its milestones. 4–10 people, 60–90 min (Ch 5, p. 137).

**Design brief** — "The translation of a problem into a structured task": design space and scope, existing approaches, design principles, scenarios, next steps and milestones, implementation challenges (Ch 1).

**Design principles** — Up to nine guiding rules on a three-tier pyramid, agreed with the team. 5–12 people, 90–180 min. Lessons learned is a good source for revising them (Ch 3, p. 53).

**Design thinking life (DTL)** — Applying the mindset to one's own life and career; the standard cycle plus *acceptance* and *self-reflection*. 1–5 people, usually 6–8 weeks per cycle (Ch 9, p. 289).

**Desirability** — One of the three dimensions: the solution is wanted by users (Ch 1).

**Digital ambition** — Step 2 of the digital transformation road map: goals in the dimensions of time, finance and space, then prioritized (Ch 9, p. 281).

**Digital fit** — Step 4 of the digital transformation road map: evaluating options against "fit with business model" and "meeting customer requirements" (Ch 9, p. 281).

**Digital implementation** — Step 5 of the digital transformation road map: customer interactions, partners, and consequences for processes, resources, skills, IT and contracts (Ch 9, p. 281).

**Digital potentials** — Step 3 of the digital transformation road map: best practices and enablers, from which own options are derived (Ch 9, p. 281).

**Digital reality** — Step 1 of the digital transformation road map: outlining the existing business model, value chains, actors and customer requirements (Ch 9, p. 281).

**Digital transformation road map** — Five-step, systems-thinking-based procedure for transforming a business model. 4–6 per team, several teams, weeks (Ch 9, p. 281).

**Divergent phase** — The widening half of a cycle: many wild ideas, many low-resolution prototypes (Ch 1).

**Dot voting** — Criteria-based selection with 3–5 glue dots per person, cast privately and placed simultaneously. 5–10 people, 5–20 min (Ch 6, p. 159).

**Double diamond** — The British Design Council model onto which the book maps its phases: phases 1–3 = problem space, phases 4–6 = solution space (Ch 1).

**Drippy T** — Variant of the T-shaped profile: depth in one domain plus breadth across others (Ch 1).

**Early adopters** — Named block on the lean canvas and a pitch prompt: the first customers, and the co-creation partners (Ch 9).

**Elevator pitch** — Shortest pitch type: an informative outline of the idea in roughly 30 seconds to 1 minute (Ch 9, p. 247).

**Emotional response cards** — Set of 50 adjective cards used to capture three emotion words per user plus the why-story. 1–2 people, 15–20 min (Ch 3, p. 87).

**Empathy map** — Documentation of a user's emotional state → pains, gains and jobs to be done (Ch 4, p. 93).

**Explorative interview** — Guide-driven narrative interview about everyday life. 1–2 people, 60–120 min plus ~40 min to build the guide (Ch 3, p. 63).

**Exploration map** — Experience × function map of all experiments, recording the **delta** between expected and actual result. Core team, ideal 4–6, 10–45 min (Ch 7, p. 195).

**Extrapolation** — Reasoning forward from today's customers, technologies and trends to the near future; one of the two inputs to the innovation funnel's problem identification mode (Ch 9, p. 265).

**Extreme users** — Users at the edges of the distribution whose workarounds reveal latent needs. 2 people, 30–120 min (Ch 3, p. 79).

**Failure mindset** — Treating mistakes not as failures but as opportunities to change and learn; explicitly promoted by the retrospective (Ch 9, p. 243).

**Feasibility** — One of the three dimensions: technically implementable (Ch 1).

**Feedback capture grid** — Four-field test documentation sheet (likes · criticism · questions · new ideas), A3, one per tester per interview. At least 2 people, 10–15 min per test, 2–3 tests (Ch 8, p. 217).

**Figuring storming** — Special brainstorming from a third party's point of view: "How would X solve the problem?" (Ch 6, p. 168).

**Final prototype** — Highest-resolution prototype: an elegant reduced system that demonstrates problem/solution fit (Ch 7, p. 194).

**Freestyle persona** — Life-size drawn user in context on a 2×1 m sheet. 2–5 people, 20–60 min (Ch 4, p. 99).

**Functional (system) prototype** — Medium-resolution prototype in the solution space implementing the main function only, by simple technical means (Ch 7, p. 192).

**Funky prototype** — Low-resolution problem-space prototype built from simple materials, often combined via a morphological box (Ch 7, p. 190).

**Future user persona** — Persona extrapolated forward, built from a 12-year generational comparison. 2–5 people, 45–60 min (Ch 4, p. 100).

**GoldBox** — Third Swisscom kickbox stage: implementation and scaling, 100% full-time, CHF 100,000–500,000 (Ch 9, p. 277).

**Groan zone** — The divergent→convergent transition; the vision prototype is the artifact that bridges it (Ch 1, Ch 7).

**Guerrilla testing** — Quick, informal usability testing in the field; a named variant alongside hallway, laboratory and field tests (Ch 8, p. 229).

**Heuristic evaluation** — Informal appraisal of a product against agreed best practices, standards or guidelines (Ch 8, p. 221).

**High-level concept** — Lean canvas sub-field paired with the unique value proposition (Ch 9, p. 252).

**"How might we…" (HMW)** — The question form that opens ideation; calibrated as verb + noun + user type; must be neither too broad nor too narrow. 3–5 people, 5–15 min (Ch 5, p. 125).

**Hunting vs. transport** — The two ME310 behaviours: purposeful search for the new (hunting) vs. delivering what is known (transport). Both are required; do not confuse them (Ch 9, p. 270).

**"I like, I wish, I wonder" (IL/IW/IW)** — Feedback ritual permitting only these three sentence stems, optionally extended with "What if…?" and "I learned". 3–5 people, 15–90 min (Ch 9, p. 239).

**Ill-defined problem** — A poorly structured problem; one of the situations analogies are recommended for (Ch 6).

**Innovation funnel** — See *problem to growth & scale innovation funnel*.

**Interview for empathy** — Story-eliciting interview producing assumptions and emotion keywords. 1–2 people, 30–60 min (Ch 3, p. 57).

**Island (retrospective)** — Sailboat field for the team's shared vision and motivation (Ch 9, p. 243).

**Jobs to be done (JTBD)** — Prioritized "When… I want to… so I can…" statements with pains and gains. ≤5 people, 60–120 min (Ch 3, p. 75).

**Key figures** — Lean canvas block; the industry's own KPIs, with AARRR recommended as a default (Ch 9, p. 252).

**Kickbox** — Adobe-originated, Swisscom-adapted intrapreneurship program: RedBox → BlueBox → GoldBox, up to 24 months, with the idea owner acting as "CEO" throughout (Ch 9, p. 277).

**KISS** — "Keep it short and simple" — the pitch rule, with a maximum of 10 slides (Ch 9, p. 248).

**"Las Vegas principle"** — Retrospective ground rule: "What happens in this room stays between us for now!" (Ch 9, p. 245).

**Lead users** — Users ahead of the market whose needs anticipate mainstream needs (Ch 3, p. 79).

**Lean canvas** — Nine-block canvas (adapted from A. Maurya, *Running Lean*, 2013) documenting problem/solution fit. 1–4 people, ~60 min initially, 10–15 min per update; iterate blocks 1–5 first (Ch 9, p. 251).

**Lessons learned** — Structured recording of positive and negative experiences at two levels: project level and meta level. Core team, 40–60 min; log books kept during the project (Ch 9, p. 255).

**Lilly** — The persona who carries the use-case comic on every tool page of the book (Ch 1).

**"Love it! Change it! Or leave it!"** — The design thinking motto governing prototype feedback, and the book's anti-cookbook stance toward its own tools (Ch 1, Ch 7).

**Macro-cycle** — Repeated runs of the micro-cycle: divergent early, convergent later, ending in implementation and market launch (Ch 1).

**Marshmallow challenge** — Warm-up: build the tallest structure from spaghetti, tape, cord and one marshmallow. 20 min; teaches thinking with your hands and the value of iteration (Ch 2, p. 46).

**Mechanical Turk** — See *Wizard of Oz*.

**ME310** — Stanford's project-based engineering design course (since 1967): 9 months, 2–3 international teams of 4–6 students solving a corporate design challenge, ending in a proof-of-concept final prototype (Ch 9, p. 269).

**Meta level (reflection)** — The second reflection level in lessons learned: what design thinking means, how it differs from and combines with other approaches, where else it could be applied (Ch 9, p. 256).

**Micro-cycle** — Understand → Observe → Define point of view → Ideate → Prototype → Test, plus Reflect (Ch 1).

**Minimum viable ecosystem (MVE)** — The ecosystem-level analogue of the MVP: the minimal arrangement of actors that lets a value proposition be delivered; referred to *The Design Thinking Playbook* (Ch 9, p. 263).

**MVP — minimum viable product** — An offer reduced to a variant that is complete (viable) from the customer's point of view but absolutely minimal (minimum). Ideally 2–4 interdisciplinary people; one MVP at a time (Ch 7, p. 207).

**Morphological box** — Combination technique used with funky prototypes (Ch 7, p. 190).

**NABC** — Need, Approach, Benefit, Competition — the one-page package for feedback and the basis of a short pitch. 1–6 people, 20–40 min (Ch 6, p. 177).

**Negative brainstorming** — Reversal technique: focus on everything that would make the problem worse, then invert (Ch 6, p. 167).

**Ninja** — Movement warm-up requiring quick thinking and body control. 10 min, lots of space (Ch 2, p. 45).

**Observe** — Second phase: go where users are and test assumptions against reality (Ch 4).

**OKR — objectives and key results** — Named as an alternative to the digital transformation road map (Ch 9, p. 281).

**Parking lot for ideas** — Place to park ideas that sound exciting but do not currently contribute to the problem; a source of inspiration when the team stalls (Ch 6).

**Peers observing peers** — Observation method splitting the record into observation and interpretation. 3–7 people, ≤3 per observation, 60–240 min (Ch 4, p. 115).

**Persona / user profile** — Eight-field canvas describing a representative user for one problem statement. 2–5 people, 20–40 min (Ch 4, p. 97).

**Pictures of the future** — Siemens technique named for extrapolation and especially retropolation in the innovation funnel (Ch 9, p. 265).

**Pitch** — Brief presentation of results to team or stakeholders in seven blocks: entry story, problem, customers, solution/idea, business model, next steps, summary. 4–6 people, 60–120 min preparation (Ch 9, p. 247).

**Pivot** — A major change to the lean canvas; once pivots become rare, an electronic version can replace the paper one (Ch 9, p. 253).

**Point of view (POV) statement** — "Name of the user/persona … needs … in order to … because …" (Ch 1, Ch 5).

**Powerful questions in experience testing** — Resolution-matched question sets (lo-fi / med-fi / hi-fi) for testing with real users. Moderator plus observers; 60–90 min planning, ≥5 users × 15 min (Ch 8, p. 221).

**Problem/customer fit** — The first target of the macro-cycle: the right problem for the right customer (Ch 1).

**Problem identification mode** — The entry state of the modern innovation funnel: start with problems, not with filtered ideas (Ch 9, p. 263).

**Problem/solution fit** — The state demonstrated by the final prototype and documented by the lean canvas (Ch 1, Ch 7, Ch 9).

**Problem space** — Phases 1–3 (understand, observe, define point of view) (Ch 1).

**Problem statement** — One consolidated sentence, distilled from ≥10 candidates: broad enough for creative freedom, narrow enough to solve with existing resources. 3–5 people plus 1–2 stakeholders, 30–40 min (Ch 3, p. 49).

**Problem to growth & scale innovation funnel** — Portfolio funnel running problem → solution → problem/solution fit → test & validate → product/market fit → business ecosystem design → scale; discontinued initiatives stay in the funnel. 1–2 maintainers, updates 30–60 min (Ch 9, p. 263).

**Product/market fit** — The stage after problem/solution fit: evidence that a market wants the solution (Ch 1, Ch 9).

**Prototype to test** — Pairing each prototype with the written assumption it tests. 30 min – many days (Ch 7, p. 199).

**RedBox** — First Swisscom kickbox stage: idea validation (understand, define point of view, ideate), 20% of time over 2 months, small exploration budget; available to every employee (Ch 9, p. 277).

**Red and green feedback** — Adhesive-dot feedback alternative: red = attention, green = all ok (Ch 9, p. 239).

**Reflect** — The seventh phase the book adds to the six-phase cycle: learn from action (Ch 1, Ch 9).

**Resolution (of a prototype)** — The degree of finish. Rule: the higher the resolution, the less feedback on desirability and functionality; the lower the resolution, the more honest the answer (Ch 7).

**Retropolation** — Reasoning backwards from a chosen future scenario to what must be built today; the second input to the innovation funnel's problem identification mode (Ch 9, p. 265).

**Retrospective board (four quadrants)** — Alternative to the sailboat: continue the same way / do differently next time / not relevant / want to try out (Ch 9, p. 245).

**Retrospective "sailboat"** — Reflection on wind (accelerators), anchor (inhibitors), cliffs (external risks) and island (shared vision), ending in defined measures. 4–6 people, 60–120 min (Ch 9, p. 243).

**Road map for implementation** — A0 map from current position (A) to destination (B) with KPIs, fields of action, standard perspectives (desirability/feasibility/viability) and named implementers. 60–120 min per meeting over several days (Ch 9, p. 259).

**Scale** — The final stage of the innovation funnel, after business ecosystem design (Ch 9).

**Self-efficacy** — The capacity to act on one's own initiative; the outcome design thinking life and the kickbox both aim at (Ch 9).

**Self-reflection** — The second step "design thinking life" adds to the standard cycle; the basis of self-efficacy (Ch 9, p. 289).

**Service blueprint** — Five-band map across the line of visibility covering customer actions, front-stage, back-stage, support processes and KPIs. 3–6 people plus process owners, ~4 hours (Ch 7, p. 203).

**Sketch notes** — Visual note-taking technique used with analogies and retrospectives. 1–12+ people, 30–60 min (Ch 6, p. 173).

**Solution interview** — Late-phase, solution-space interview testing acceptance of a final prototype or MVP, up to pricing. Teams of 2, 20–30 min; four phases: warm-up, context, experience the solution, summary (Ch 8, p. 225).

**Solution space** — Phases 4–6 (ideate, prototype, test) (Ch 1).

**Special brainstorming** — Umbrella for negative brainstorming, figuring storming and bodystorming; the unsticking move when a group goes in circles. 2–10 people, 10–20 min per technique (Ch 6, p. 167).

**Split testing** — Multi-variant form of A/B testing (Ch 8, p. 233).

**Stakeholder map** — Map of actors with typed relationship lines; reused in the road map, blueprint and funnel. Design team plus a stakeholder representative, 60–240 min (Ch 3, p. 83).

**Stop & go** — Warm-up breaking social obstacles and heightening concentration; command escalation, no materials (Ch 2, p. 43).

**Storytelling** — Turning findings into a story grid, storyboard, bullet draft or short video. 2–5 people, 10–30 min (5–10 min per user story) (Ch 5, p. 129).

**Structured brainstorming** — Variant in which participants write privately, then explain their Post-its one at a time while new ideas are generated (Ch 6, p. 152).

**Structured usability testing** — Testing operable prototypes with real users performing defined tasks under uniform conditions; three phases with a dry run; tasks rated success / struggle / fail. At least 2 people, 40–90 min (Ch 8, p. 229).

**Success / struggle / fail** — The per-task rating scheme recommended for usability tests (Ch 8, p. 231).

**T-shaped** — Profile combining depth in one discipline with breadth across others; the recommended team composition (Ch 1).

**Teams of teams** — Network-thinking structure in which teams collaborate without controlling each other and decide independently; contrasted with information-filtering hierarchy (Ch 1, Ch 9).

**Test** — The sixth phase: interact with the potential user and document (Ch 8).

**Testing sheet** — Plan-and-document sheet for any test: location, criteria, sequence, roles, key questions, quotes, media, findings. 2–3 people, 10–30 min per test (Ch 8, p. 213).

**Think aloud** — The instruction given to test persons in every test tool in the book: relate everything you think (Ch 8).

**Trend analysis** — Trend tube map plus affinity chart, locating the product at trend intersections. 2–5 people, 120–240 min (Ch 4, p. 119).

**Understand** — The first phase: learn about the potential user, their needs and tasks, and define the creative framework (Ch 3).

**Unfair advantage** — Lean canvas block: what cannot easily be copied or bought (Ch 9, p. 252).

**Unique value proposition** — Lean canvas block: the single clear reason the offer is different and worth buying (Ch 9, p. 252).

**Viability** — One of the three dimensions: economically profitable (Ch 1).

**Vision cone** — Past · present · future cone used to name future scenarios and reverse-engineer next steps. Whole team, 90–120 min (Ch 5, p. 141).

**Vision prototype** — Very low-resolution single-sentence (or sketch/video) prototype that bridges the groan zone (Ch 7, p. 191).

**VUCA** — Volatility, uncertainty, complexity, ambiguity — the context cited for A/B testing and agility (Ch 8, p. 234).

**Warm-up finder** — The book's selector matching warm-ups to setting, group and learning objective (Ch 2).

**"What if…?"** — Optional fifth column of the IL/IW/IW grid, used to push feedback into ideation (Ch 9, p. 240).

**Wind (retrospective)** — Sailboat field for what went well and pushed the team forward (Ch 9, p. 243).

**Wizard of Oz** — Prototyping technique in which a hidden human simulates a system that does not yet exist; typical of the dark horse prototype (Ch 7, p. 189).

**"X is finished" prototype** — Medium-to-high-resolution solution-space prototype detailing the essential sub-functions, with a first cost estimate (Ch 7, p. 193).

**Young Innovators** — Cross-company communities of 18–25-year-olds deployed on design challenges as lateral thinkers, product testers for young target groups, and a recruiting pipeline. At least 5 people; 90–120 min for a minor challenge (Ch 9, p. 285).
