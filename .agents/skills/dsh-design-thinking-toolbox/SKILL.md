# The Design Thinking Toolbox

**Author**: Michael Lewrick, Patrick Link & Larry Leifer (illustrations: Achim Schmidt) — John Wiley & Sons, 2020
**Pages**: 311
**Chapters**: 9 (this skill's grouping of the book's own phase sections)
**Generated**: 2026-08-23

A quick-reference catalogue of just over 50 tools, selected from 150 candidates by a global survey of 2,500+ design thinking practitioners in 44 countries, and described by 100+ named experts. Every tool page follows one fixed structure: *I would like…* / more information / what you can do with it / alternative tools / supporting tools / group size + typical duration + materials / template and step-by-step procedure / a named expert's tips / a "Lilly" use-case comic.

## Core Frameworks & Mental Models

**The seven-phase micro-cycle.** Understand → Observe → Define point of view → Ideate → Prototype → Test, plus **Reflect** added at the end. Mapped onto the British Design Council double diamond: the first three phases are the **problem space**, the next three the **solution space**. The book classifies every tool by which phases it serves (full/half Harvey ball per phase). The **macro-cycle** runs the micro-cycle many times: divergent early (many wild ideas, many low-resolution prototypes), convergent later (higher-resolution prototypes, functional prototype, final prototype, then implementation/market launch).

**"Love it, change it, or leave it!"** The book's explicit anti-cookbook stance. Tool descriptions are a guide, not a recipe. A detailed inflexible agenda plus wrong tools at the wrong time produces a bad workshop *and* no viable solution — after which the team will resist design thinking permanently. Adapt every template to the setting, the problem statement, and the participants.

**Problem before solution.** "In design thinking, we always start with the problem, never with a solution." Two rules for a problem statement: (1) broad enough to allow creative freedom to unfold; (2) narrow enough to be solvable with existing resources (team size, time, budget). Canonical form: *How might we help [user] to achieve [goal/need] applying [restrictions/principles]?*

**Desirability / feasibility / viability.** Solutions must be wanted by users (desirability), technically implementable (feasibility), and economically profitable (viability). These three dimensions reduce launch risk, speed up learning, and push past incremental solutions. Targets in sequence: **problem/customer fit**, then **problem/solution fit** (shown by the final prototype), then **product/market fit** and **scale**.

**Resolution discipline.** "The higher the resolution of a prototype, the less feedback we receive regarding desirability and functionality. The lower the resolution, the more honest the answer." Corollary rules from the book: never fall in love with a prototype; implement no more than one or two functions per low-resolution prototype; if you are not embarrassed to show it, you invested too much time.

**Team and process awareness.** Team size 3–7, **five is ideal**; over 10 coordination costs swamp effectiveness — split into teams-of-teams. Members should be **T-shaped** or "drippy T" (depth in one domain plus breadth). Everyone must know where the team stands in the cycle and when it is in the **groan zone** (divergent→convergent transition), which the *vision prototype* exists to bridge.

**The nine success factors.** 1) Start with human beings. 2) Create awareness of the problem. 3) Interdisciplinary teams. 4) Experiments and prototypes. 5) Be mindful of process. 6) Visualize and show ideas. 7) Bias toward action. 8) Accept complexity. 9) Co-create, grow & scale with varying mental states (combine with data analytics, systems thinking, lean startup).

**The beginner's mind.** Free of prejudice about how something works, free of expectations about what will happen, curious, open to possibility, fail early and often. Behavioural rules: ask simple questions, try things out, learn from it.

**Facilitation economics.** Preparation takes as much time as the workshop itself. The facilitator is neutral — moderates, does not contribute content. Minimum 15 m² of space per group of four. Ideally work standing up; sitting slides the group into "conference mode."

## Scope & Limits

- **Edition**: English Wiley edition, 2020 (originally Verlag Vahlen GmbH, Munich). ISBN 978-1-119-62919-1. Companion volumes referenced throughout: *The Design Thinking Playbook* (2018) and *The Design Thinking Life Playbook*.
- **What it is**: a tool reference, deliberately not a cookbook and not a theory of innovation. It gives no research evidence for the tools beyond the popularity survey, and no guidance on organizational change management beyond the six application programs in Chapter 9.
- **What it is not**: it does not cover agile/Scrum delivery, detailed business-model economics, quantitative market research methods, or systems-thinking/business-ecosystem design — those are explicitly deferred to *The Design Thinking Playbook*.
- **Extraction gaps** (the PDF's text layer dropped some purely graphical pages; these are flagged again in the relevant chapters):
  - The **empathy map's own template page and timing box (pp. 94–95)** did not survive extraction. The chapter reports only what the book states in prose.
  - The **eight brainstorming rules** graphic (p. 152) survived only partially: #1 Creative confidence, #2 Quantity before quality, "build on the ideas of others", "only one person speaks at a time", #7 No prejudices, #8 Continue to… Rules 3–6 and the text of #8 are not recoverable.
  - The **"Example of axes in a 2x2 matrix"** reference table (p. 156) is a graphic and did not survive; only the axis pairs named in prose are reported.
  - The **Tools Quickfinder Harvey-ball matrix** printed on the book cover is present but with broken column alignment; the matrix in `cheatsheet.md` is rebuilt from each tool's own page (stated phases, stated duration, stated group size) rather than from the garbled scan.
  - The **50 emotional response cards** are named as a set but the individual adjectives are not printed in the book.
  - Templates are supplied by the publisher as downloads at `www.dt-toolbook.com/<tool>-en`; the printed templates are reproduced here as their labelled fields and steps, not as artwork.
- **Dating**: the global survey ran in spring 2018; tool popularity rankings, the digital-transformation material, and tool URLs reflect 2018–2020.
