# Chapter 5 — Define Point of View

*(Book pages 123–148; 6 tools)*

## Core Idea

"At the end of the problem analysis, the results are summarized, clustered, discussed, and evaluated. The team view of the problem is formulated as a **point of view**. It is used later as a starting point in the search for a solution. Various methods, such as storytelling and 'How might we…' as a questioning technique, support this process."

This is the hinge of the double diamond: the last phase of the **problem space** and the gateway into the **solution space**. Everything gathered in *understand* and *observe* is compressed into (a) a shared team picture and (b) a question specific enough to ideate against.

## Frameworks Introduced

Same fixed tool structure as the rest of the book: ***I would like…*** → some information → what you can do with it → alternative tools → supporting tools → **group size / typical duration / materials** → **template + procedure** → named expert's tips → the Lilly use case with key learnings.

## 1. "How might we…" question (p. 125)

***I would like…*** *to formulate a question that makes it possible later, in the "ideate" phase, to work in a targeted manner.*

- **Why the three words matter.** *"'How' implies that there are more possible ways to solve the question. 'Might' creates a safe space in which we know that a potential idea might work. 'We' reminds us that we solve the problem as a team."* The HMW question is called "an elementary component in The Design Thinking Toolbox."
- **Group size** develop each HMW question in groups of **3–5** · **Duration** **5–15 min** — "The HMW question can be formulated quickly if good findings have been identified. The definition of an HMW question usually takes no longer than 15 minutes." · **Materials** whiteboards or movable walls, Post-its, pens, paper.
- **Template — the Goldilocks scale.** The printed template is a three-band diagram: **Too broad | Just right | Too narrow**, with a *How?* arrow narrowing the focus and a *Why?* arrow expanding it. The book's own three worked calibration examples:

| Band | Printed example |
|---|---|
| **Too broad** | "How might we revolutionize the business model of banks?" |
| **Just right** | "How might we design an investment product for young people?" |
| **Too narrow** | "How might we use nappa leather to design a wallet for seniors?" |

- **Grammar rule.** *"Each question should adhere to the logic of 'How might we…' followed by a **verb** (e.g. design), a **noun** (e.g. investment product), and the **type of user** (e.g. name of persona)."*
- **Procedure.** Reflect on the findings from *understand* and *observe* — the result is a synthesis of the insights. Determine which needs the team should address and what qualifying additional information is relevant. Motivate the team to produce **several** HMW questions. **Read the HMW question aloud** and ask whether the team is inspired to find many solutions; if not, it is too narrow (it anticipates a solution or does not allow exploration) or too broad (it tries to improve the world and the team feels lost). To correct: ask **WHY to expand** the focus, **HOW to narrow** it. Once rolled out, start ideation, e.g. with an open brainstorming session.
- **The verb bank printed with the tool** (p. 126) — verbs to slot after "How might we…": *define, predict, frame, solve, create, identify, reflect, apply for, develop, compare, describe, demonstrate, experiment, change, construct, match, differentiate, choose, ask, paraphrase, recognize, discover, prepare, check, develop, select, research, produce, correlate, imagine, investigate, transform, show, separate, negotiate, tell, describe, judge, analyze, design, visualize, compare, transfer, structure.*
- **Alternatives — six reframing question types printed with the tool:**
  - **Devil's advocate** — "What would happen if people could not remember things?"
  - **By contrast** — "What would happen if the pain is transformed into a gain?"
  - **Vice versa** — "What would happen if, for example, the patient diagnosed the doctor?"
  - **Time frame** — "What will/did the situation look like in 20 years / 20 years ago?"
  - **Provocation** (photo/fabric/quote/sound/smell) — "How do we design a solution when nobody wants to use anything else?"
  - **Analogy** — "What can we learn from the pit stop at Formula One on the subject of time?"
  - **Miracle** — "If we were magicians, how would we solve it?"
- **Expert (Andrés Bedoya, Innovation Project Manager, d.school Paris at École des Ponts)**: *"There is no right and no wrong in the definition of questions."* Rely on gut feeling. **"If the HMW question fits, we feel the urge to search for ideas to answer it. If the question is not appropriate, we usually cannot think of any ideas."** More effective to prepare several HMW questions for a subject cluster than to argue about the right "cruising altitude" of one — ***"Each HMW question can be understood as a prototype and tested in a short brainstorming session."*** Always name the target customer. Be bold: thick marker, A5 paper and Post-its so everybody can see the question.

## 2. Storytelling (p. 129)

***I would like…*** *to present my insights, ideas, and solutions to the members of my team and other stakeholders.*

- **Positioning.** Usable in many phases across the cycle. *"For thousands and thousands of years, storytelling has helped mankind to share knowledge across generations. In the context of design thinking, it helps us to connect to the team, to focus, arouse motivation, and generate incentives for creativity and empathy!"* You can also **tell stories about data** — animated visualization of data creates Wow! effects.
- **Group size** **2–5** is ideal; for the discussion of findings, each member recounts the story he/she observed · **Duration** **10–30 min**; summarizing findings into a story takes roughly **30 minutes** depending on the design challenge and number of findings; **the story per user should be no longer than 5–10 minutes** · **Materials** large whiteboard, flip chart or printed/painted template, Post-its, pens, markers.
- **Template — a grid, one row per interviewed person:**
  1. **Step 1** — print the template or draw the structure on flip chart/whiteboard.
  2. **Step 2** — every team member completes **one line** (e.g. per interviewed person): the **highlights and special features of the person or user** go in **column 1**, plus **important quotes from the person**.
  3. **Step 3** — interpret the results as a team and **define the meaning**.
  4. **Step 4** — draw a conclusion together and summarize the **key findings** from the interview. Then formulate the draft of a story in bullet points, create a **storyboard**, or produce a **short video** that enacts the story.
- **Expert (Jessica Domínguez, entrepreneur and freelance designer, Colombia)**: don't only write keywords on Post-its — **draw sketches**; hang up photos or artifacts so the storytelling board expands like a **mood board**; **give the users names and identifying features** — "the funny guy with the blue hat," "the Chanel lady for whom it's very important to look good." Storytelling doubles as a **prototyping tool** and summarizes test findings. Experiment with types: a cliff-hanger makes people concentrate; an emotional background arouses trust and connection.

## 3. Context mapping (p. 133)

***I would like…*** *to deal with the context, for example, of a problem.*

- **Purpose.** Gives designers unexpected insights into a system and sub-system; lets you observe the user in his/her everyday experience. ***"The use of this tool makes explicit what's implicit."*** *"The point of context mapping is **not** to gain many insights. The goal is to find out more about how the respective experience is perceived."* Governing principle: ***"Knowledge is information with additional context."***
- **Group size** **2–4** depending on complexity — "If the groups are too large, the momentum is often lost" · **Duration** **40–60 min** for a well-thought-out context map; findings already developed with other tools accelerate the procedure · **Materials** paper, pens, camera, movable walls or whiteboard.
- **Template — a categorized map with deliberately empty fields. Procedure:**
  1. Go outside as often as possible to observe and understand — *"There is no substitute for seeing reality from the point of view of the user."*
  2. Observe user and environment. Typical questions: **What does he do? Where does he do it? With whom does he do it? What is the impact of his activities on the environment? Which individuals lend support? Are there shared tools or resources?**
  3. **Take pictures** of the environment and the user.
  4. **Define the areas of focus** — extensive context or limited context.
  5. **Determine the categories** of the respective context, for example **trends, the economy, location, or technology fields**.
  6. If necessary, **rearrange the categories** to find new connections.
  7. Fill the categories on the template with the insights.
  8. ***Deliberately leave one or two fields empty*** so the team is encouraged to add categories that seem important.
- **Variation — context map without prelabeled elements.** Instead of prioritizing elements onto a list (where the top of the list reads as most important), use a **daisy map**: *"All eight petals of the daisy are equal, so to speak; none is visually seen as more important."* The team then decides which things are important or critical and enters them.
- **Expert (Denise Pereira de Carvalho, Brazil Innovation Leader, DuPont do Brasil)**: walk in your user's shoes — accept the user may see things differently, be open to surprises, *"the users are the true experts of their routine and their experience."* People are not aware of their everyday experience and overlook the details that matter in design. Liberate yourself from expectations of right and wrong; acquire a **"beginner's mind" of "not knowing."**
- **⚠ Extraction note.** The template graphic's printed category labels did not survive extraction; the categories listed above (*trends, economy, location, technology fields*) are the ones the book names in prose, and the Lilly comic shows the team **replacing** "environment, economy, politics" with **"technology, trends, fears."**

## 4. Define success (p. 137)

***I would like…*** *to provide support to the team across the entire design cycle, especially in relation to the range of options.*

- **Positioning.** Used at two points with the same questions but different time horizons: as part of the **definition of the point of view**, and again in the **later implementation** of the project. *"What do we want to have achieved in one month? Where do we stand with the business model in 5 years?"* It determines **milestones** and creates the basis for **KPIs** if the project wants them.
- **Group size** **4–10** — ideally the design team **plus the decision makers who will have to approve the project later** · **Duration** **60–90 min**; reflect on the results regularly and adjust for external/internal changes; **always use prior to selecting solutions for the prototype (about 5 minutes)** · **Materials** large whiteboard or movable walls, flip chart, Post-its, pens, markers.
- **Template — eight printed questions in a three-column canvas (Define questions | Answers | Evaluate & select!):**
  1. **How great might the financial success be** (e.g. sales, revenue or market share, requirements of lenders or partners)?
  2. **What is the value the project will have for the company or the users/stakeholders?**
  3. **What might the success for our users look like?** (solving a problem; better than the current solution; offering an answer to a specific new goal)
  4. **What might be the success for our key partners and stakeholders?**
  5. **How important might the success be for each team member and the team?**
  6. **How important would the success be for the management?**
  7. **What is the business case for my major stakeholders?**
  8. **What might be the most important milestones?**
- **Procedure.** Use Post-its so every member can share thoughts. Prepare a list of relevant issues (what internal and external success mean) to ensure a **360° vision** emerges. Everybody shares first; only then discuss and narrow down; select core elements by clustering; vote on the main areas, e.g. with **dot voting (p. 159)**. Involve decision makers up front "so you ensure already in the run-up that no time and no money will be wasted."
- **Expert (Hélène Cahen, Innovation Consultant, Strategic Insights, USA)**: everybody writes their answer down first "so that we achieve a neutral view of the wishes"; when a Post-it is shared, ***"we simply listen and stick the answer to the wall. We don't comment, and we don't yet judge."*** Budget ***twice as much time for assessment and selection as for collecting*** the factors of success. Also **search for known success stories** in and outside the organization to sense what is possible. Her printed list: *Braun (simplified IoT electric toothbrush) · PepsiCo (design thinking as part of strategy) · Procter & Gamble (product development) · Bank of America (Keep the Change) · Deutsche Bank (evolution of design thinking in IT) · GE Health (a better MRI scanner for children) · IDEO & Cambodia (sanitation) · Nike · Airbnb (avoiding bankruptcy) · Apple · IBM (cultural change) · Google (how to brainstorm like a Googler).*

## 5. Vision cone — "past · present · future" (p. 141)

***I would like…*** *to design a desirable future and explore what has to be done now to attain this goal over time.*

- **Purpose.** Connects current innovative developments with past and future; substantiates the overall vision and breaks it into actionable steps. *"The vision cone transforms daring uncertainties into positive aspects of innovation projects, for example, reinterpreting a problem as a possibility or market opportunity."*
- **Group size** ideally the **entire design team** on one cone; alternatively members work individually and consolidate later · **Duration** **90–120 min**, budgeted as **30 min status quo + 30 min past + 30 min first draft of possible futures + the rest for reverse engineering the required steps** · **Materials** paper, pens, Post-its; cords and pins optionally to create the cone outlines.
- **Template — two connected cones labelled PAST · NOW · FUTURE. Seven steps:**
  1. Define a topic matching the current challenge (e.g. mobility, health); label the two cones.
  2. **Start with NOW** — status quo of the project, state of the art, current perception in society (e.g. *semi-autonomous driving*).
  3. **Focus on PAST** — research findings to date plus important technological and sociological changes; be accurate about dates and link related events (e.g. *1960s, cars piloted by magnetic strips in the United States*).
  4. **Focus on FUTURE** — write down all findings about a fictitious future, nobody knows the probability (e.g. *autonomously flying cars*).
  5. **Identify possible scenarios** and give them **memorable names** for better storytelling.
  6. **Select a desirable future**, then **work back from it and reverse-engineer** the steps that would have to happen **NOW**.
  7. Infer specific next steps.
- **Four types of future** (expert, Samuel Huber, Strategy & Development Lead, Goodpatch, Germany & Japan): **planned · plausible** (based on the latest knowledge) **· possible** (based on specific future technologies) **· absurd** (will never happen). ***"The absurd future yields the best results since we only hit the limits of what is possible when we think the unthinkable."*** Park fears and restrictions at the beginning. *"The vision cone is not about predictions but about opportunities."* **"We should be careful not to derive the future linearly from the past."** Pair with **vision prototypes (p. 191)** — "design fiction is the best story of the future."

## 6. Critical items diagram (p. 145)

***I would like…*** *to structure the findings from the early phases and prepare for ideating and experimenting.*

- **Purpose.** The team agrees on the **critical success elements** for the target group — *"These elements are the ones that must be solved later with the final prototype."* Each element either **describes the experience a user expects** the solution to provide or **presents an expected function**. The elements should be questioned after each iteration, though some will stay relevant to a **critical experience or critical function** right up to the final prototype.
- **Group size** ideally the **entire design team**; the presentation also helps discussion with clients when defining the problem statement more precisely · **Duration** **30–60 min** — "The clearer the situation and the less uncertainty, the faster the main topics are defined" · **Materials** large sheet of paper, Post-its, pens, movable walls or whiteboard.
- **Template — an eight-element daisy map** (adapted from *Playbook for Strategic Foresight and Innovation*, Tamara Carleton & William Cockayne, 2013, `www.innovation.io/playbook`). **Procedure:**
  1. Ask: ***"What is critical for a successful solution to the problem?"*** — based on the findings from *understand* and *observe*.
  2. Sketch the diagram and discuss which **experiences the user must have** and which **functions are critical**.
  3. **Each team member writes the eight elements that are critical to them** on Post-its.
  4. **Each member names four experiences and four functions**, one of which focuses on **completely new or future expectations**.
  5. Consolidate and agree as a team on **eight critical elements**, then define HMW questions "interesting enough to launch the 'ideate' phase successfully."
- **Expert (Christian Hohmann, Lecturer for Product Innovation, Hochschule Luzern)**: the eight elements are an **iterative** artifact — "it is impossible to describe them perfectly the first time you try"; check them after each experiment. ***"It is not advisable to work too long with this tool. There is a risk that you get lost in discussions. If we cannot come to an agreement, we simply include more elements and check their importance by means of little experiments."*** Ideally **no longer than 60 minutes; the shorter, the better**. Put the currently valid diagram on display as a huge poster. **Show, don't tell** — enrich elements with visualizations and quotations from interviews or testing.

## Mental Models

- **The Goldilocks band.** An HMW question is not right or wrong, it is calibrated. The test is behavioural, not analytic: read it aloud and see whether ideas come.
- **HMW as prototype.** Rather than debating one question's altitude, generate several and run each through a short brainstorming session; the one that produces ideas wins.
- **WHY expands, HOW narrows.** The two levers for moving a question along the broad/narrow scale.
- **Make explicit what's implicit.** Context mapping's whole job — depth of perception, not quantity of insight.
- **Reverse engineering from a desirable future.** The vision cone runs backwards from a chosen future to today's next step, deliberately refusing linear extrapolation from the past.
- **Experiences and functions are separate currencies.** The critical items diagram forces four of each, which is exactly what the critical experience prototype (CEP) and critical function prototype (CFP) later test (see Ch. 7).

## Anti-patterns

- **HMW questions that already contain the solution** — the classic "too narrow" failure ("use nappa leather…").
- **HMW questions that try to improve the world** — "the team feels lost when confronted with the task."
- **Discussing an HMW question for too long.** *"Time pressure helps in staying agile and not getting bogged down with the final phrasing."*
- **Stories that are not true.** *"Stories are valuable if they are true, so they must always be substantiated with real events."*
- **Prelabeled, prioritized context lists** — the top of the list is read as most important; use the equal-petal daisy map instead.
- **Commenting or judging Post-its as they are shared** during define success.
- **Defining success without the decision makers** — frustration accumulates during the cycle or at its end.
- **Falling in love with the past** in the vision cone, and deriving the future linearly from it.
- **Getting lost in discussion over the eight critical items** — add elements and settle it with small experiments instead.

## Worked Example

**HMW question, Lilly's team (p. 128).** "Everyone on the team has prepared some questions. Together, they discuss the positioning or formulate the HMW question by means of 'how' and 'why' questions until there is consensus on the team, and everybody is convinced the right question has been found. Lilly allows enough time to find the HMW question. **Especially in large groups, enough time must be allowed for the important discussion and interaction**, so a good HMW question is formulated in the end. With the right question, we reach our goal faster." Key learnings: "Do not discuss an HMW question for too long. Time pressure helps in staying agile and not getting bogged down with the final phrasing. It is essential to be optimistic and close to the needs of the users in order to come up with several good HMWs."

**The three calibrated HMW questions printed on the template (p. 126)** — reproduced above as the Goldilocks table: *too broad* = revolutionize the business model of banks; *just right* = design an investment product for young people; *too narrow* = use nappa leather to design a wallet for seniors.

**Storytelling, Lilly's team (p. 132).** "Lilly motivates the team to switch from the divergent phase to the convergent phase again and again and to summarize and structure the findings while doing so. It is not always easy to pool the relevant statements. **Often, the team is unable to see the forest for the trees and gets lost in details.** Lilly has achieved good results with this storyboard. It helps identify the key points, determine their meaning, and infer from it the key findings on the team."

**Context mapping, Lilly's team (p. 136).** The topic is **fear of AI and digital transformation**, "since the differences between Asia and Europe are quite substantial." In the comic the team explicitly renegotiates the axes: *"First, we need to check and enter the dimensions. Are the dimensions right?"* — *"**Instead of the environment, the economy, and politics, I'd rather take the dimensions of technology, trends, and fears.**"* Another member marks a slot: *"Here is a good place to include the statements of the Asia expert"* — *"Being afraid of AI [is] something that is [more] relevant in Europe."* Key learnings: "Understand where the user comes from. The context and the experience change in different situations. **Initial thoughts about the situation are usually wrong.**"

**Define success, Lilly's team (p. 140).** "Lilly's team defines at an early stage what success means for them and which components need to be fulfilled for everybody on the team to say: **'Wow! That was a great project!'** What turns out is that **not only the primary economic factors are of vital importance but also the collaboration on the team and the benefits for society as a whole.** Hence the team will pay more attention to the social and emotional aspects in the next iterations."

**Vision cone, Lilly's team (p. 144).** "In the vision cone, Lilly's team also considers the social aspects of AI and digital transformation. The team discusses **whether there might be a global tax soon on the work of robots.** This would have a great impact on the distribution of income and on the way we work. Alongside social topics, the technologies are scrutinized. **The Gartner Hype Cycle helps the team classify what constitutes a hype and what has already reached a certain maturity in the market.**"

**Critical items diagram, Lilly's team (p. 148).** Lilly first uses the tool to **prioritize the hypotheses** gained from interviews, inquiries, and analyses; "these assumptions are then checked in the **critical experience and critical function prototype**." She later reuses it to summarize the findings from the iterations. In the comic: *"Have we gathered all the critical factors and findings from our previous interviews, tests, and observations?"* — *"In principle, yes. Perhaps we should **weight accessibility higher than freedom of choice**. I'll change that."*

## Key Takeaways

- The point of view is a **synthesis**, not a summary: findings in, one question out.
- Write several HMW questions and let short brainstorming sessions choose between them.
- HMW grammar: *How might we + verb + noun + user type*.
- Context mapping trades breadth of insight for depth of perceived experience; leave fields empty on purpose.
- Define success early, with the people who will sign off, and spend twice as long selecting as collecting.
- The vision cone runs **backwards** from a chosen desirable future; the absurd future is the most productive one to draft.
- The critical items diagram is the direct input to the CEP/CFP prototypes and to the HMW questions that open ideation.
