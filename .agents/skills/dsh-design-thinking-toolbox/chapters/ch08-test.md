# Chapter 8 — Test

*(Book pages 211–235; 6 tools)*

## Core Idea

"The test of the respective prototype takes place in the interaction with a potential user. This means we not only receive feedback on the prototype but also refine our view of the problem and the user. In addition, **we are reconnected to the 'understand' and 'observe' phases, which in turn can yield a new point of view.** This micro-cycle is repeated as often as desired and characterizes the iterative procedure in design thinking."

The phase has no single canonical tool. "There are different test procedures. **Which of them is the most helpful depends on the kind of prototype.** Of course, the presented tools for testing overlap in terms of their purpose (testing); nonetheless, each testing approach and each procedure deliver valuable information that helps us to improve the prototype in question."

Two rules run through every tool in the chapter. **Testing is need finding, not selling** — "we should not under any circumstances attempt to 'sell' our idea to the user; instead, we must learn from the feedback we receive." And **the resolution of the prototype dictates the questions** — lo-fi, med-fi and hi-fi prototypes each get their own question set (p. 222).

The six tools sort into three families:
- **Documentation frames** that make any test repeatable and shareable — testing sheet, feedback capture grid.
- **Question craft** — powerful questions in experience testing.
- **Formal test procedures** with defined conditions — solution interview (acceptance, late phase), structured usability testing (operability, uniform conditions), A/B testing (preference between two variants).

## 1. Testing sheet (p. 213)

***I would like…*** *to prepare the test sequence and document the test results.*

- **Positioning.** *"Among other things, the point of testing is to learn as much as possible about the user and his/her needs by having the user interact with the prototype. It is worth planning the test situation and considering what the test sequence is, who has what role, and which key questions should be asked."* A test run is usually performed by **two or three people** — *"Not all team members have to be included. Much more important is the documentation of the tests (e.g. with photos and quotes or short videos) that allow us to share the findings with the team."*
- **Group size** one person makes notes and documents the findings, the other performs the test; **optionally one more person observes** · **Duration** **10–30 min per test** for low-resolution prototypes — *"The higher the degree of detail, the longer the tests take. With high-resolution prototypes, tests can stretch over a few weeks."* · **Materials** notepad and pen, camera (for photos and videos), the prototype to be tested, some prototyping material, template for test documentation.
- **Alternatives** structured usability test (p. 229), solution interview (p. 225). **Supporting tools** powerful questions in experience testing (p. 221), empathy map (p. 93), feedback capture grid (p. 217).
- **Procedure — the three steps.** *Initial situation: the prototype has already been built; now the test scenario must be planned.*
  1. **Test planning.** Think about **where** the test should take place — *"It is best to carry out the test in the context of the problem on site on the user's premises."* **Define the test criteria prior to the test:** *"What are the criteria for a thesis to be considered as verified?"* Plan the sequence, the assignment of roles, and the key questions. Define **who asks the questions, who makes notes and documents, and who observes**.
  2. **Test procedure.** Run the test and **observe the user keenly**. Ask for feedback — *"It is very valuable and constitutes the basis for further decisions on the development of the prototype."* **Write down the most important quotes.**
  3. **Test documentation.** Document with photos or, better, **short videos of the most important statements**. Summarize the main findings and learnings.
- **⚠ Extraction gap — the printed testing-sheet template (p. 214)** is a graphic that did not survive the text layer. The fields recoverable from the prose steps are: *test location · test criteria (verification thresholds) · sequence · role assignment (questioner / note-taker / observer) · key questions · quotes · photo/video documentation · findings & learnings.*
- **Expert (Isabelle Hauser, Lucerne University of Applied Sciences and Arts; Program Director CAS Design Thinking, co-founder Entux GmbH)**: *"The mindset is not only a recommendation but a must. If you don't experience design thinking yourself, you won't be able to estimate its benefits."* Her tips:
  - The testing sheet is **a basis for any type of test** — usability tests, experience tests, solution interviews.
  - ***"Allowing enough time for testing is the most important aspect of planning."***
  - During preparation, think up questions **relevant to attaining the test objective**; "powerful questions in experience testing" helps here.
  - **Digital prototypes have two routes:** online — integrate the test directly into the website and ask the user specific questions; or the traditional way — stay close to the user and observe precisely what he is doing.
  - **Bring prototyping material to the test.** With adhesive tape, Post-its and other things, the test person or the team can **adapt the prototype immediately**.
  - *"If the users don't think the solution is any good, we recommend listening even more closely and finding out why. Then we are often able to explore exactly the piece of the puzzle that had been missing… **Ask 'Why?' several times.**"*
  - **Permissions:** "If you want to make videos and photos, always ask permission first. If there's a need for further use, have a **data use agreement** signed."

## 2. Feedback capture grid (p. 217)

***I would like…*** *to test my prototyped ideas quickly and simply and write down the results for further development.*

- **Positioning.** *"The feedback capture grid supports the testing of ideas using prototypes because it allows us to document test results in a very simple form. It is primarily used when it comes to finding out **how well an idea solves a previously identified user problem**."* The grid aims at *"acquiring profound understanding as to whether the problem can be solved and how it can be solved and whether the idea actually is the right approach to a solution."* It can also be used generally **for feedback on the process, a workshop, or other things**.
- **Group size** **at least 2** — *Tester 1* interviews, observes and demonstrates the prototype; *Tester 2* documents the results and asks further questions if needed · **Duration** **30–60 min** overall; *"A test takes approx. **10–15 minutes**. As a rule, we need several tests to be able to make accurate statements. If possible, always perform **2–3 tests**."* · **Materials** visualization of the idea as prototype / MVP / digital prototype; **one printed or drawn grid per tester and per interview — the optimal printout size is A3**.
- **Alternatives** testing sheet (p. 213), empathy map (p. 93). **Supporting tools** all kinds of prototypes (pp. 187–194), ask 5x why (p. 67), lean canvas (p. 251) as an information base before or a summary after the test, 5W+H questions (p. 71).
- **Template — the four fields, as described in the printed steps:**

| | Left | Right |
|---|---|---|
| **Upper row** | **What the user liked** | **What he might not like so much** (criticism) |
| **Lower row** | **Questions that were asked**, plus **new ideas** the user or the observers had while observing | *(the fourth field completes the grid; the printed labels are lost — see the gap note)* |

- **Procedure.** *Initial situation: a rudimentary prototype (e.g. lo-fi) exists, and information about the persona, the need, and the problem hypothesis has been developed over the previous phases.*
  1. Draw the grid on a sheet of paper or print the template.
  2. ***"Always begin a test with the tester seeing and experiencing the prototype."***
  3. **Ask the tester to think aloud.**
  4. Fill in the fields with those thoughts — written directly or on Post-its.
  5. **Ask "Why?" questions** (ask 5x why, p. 67) to understand the answers better. *"Pay attention to emotions, conflicting body language, and initial reactions."*
  6. **Collect the grids from the various interviews** and work out similarities or major differences with the team; use them for the further development of ideas and prototypes.
- **⚠ Extraction gap.** The printed grid graphic (p. 218) did not survive extraction; only the field descriptions in the prose steps above are recoverable. The book's own steps name three of the four contents explicitly (likes · criticism · questions and new ideas).
- **Expert (Katrin Fischer, Innovation Consultant, Liechtenstein)**: her motivating experience — *"starting with an idea, an (expensive) development project was launched and the testing was only done at the end. The solution was often developed out of sync with market requirements, and valuable resources were wasted."* Her tips:
  - **Get into the shoes of your customers.** Choose **different places and different user groups** for testing. ***"People who are not yet customers are particularly valuable suppliers of ideas in this phase."***
  - ***"The rule when testing is: Listen, watch, and try not to ask questions at first or explain the prototype."***
  - Write down **direct quotes and immediate reactions**. *"Testing is need finding. The same rules apply as with the interview."*
  - **Take a step back and reflect.** Always reflect on the results **with the team** — *"A new point of view can be formulated this way."* **Group the completed grids according to answers and personas.**
  - *"As always: Iterate, iterate, iterate!! — until our potential users are happy."*

## 3. Powerful questions in experience testing (p. 221)

***I would like…*** *to evaluate ideas, prototypes, services, or a product by testing them with real customers or users.*

- **Positioning.** *"'Powerful questions' is a great tool for gathering more findings in a test. As early as with low-resolution prototypes, we should pose the right questions and develop them for high-resolution prototypes."* Testing must always be based on a specific prototype. *"During the test experience, the test person should interact as much as possible with the prototype. The observers watch and listen attentively and write down the results."* A **simple test plan** with goals, test environment, process, moderation and test participants is created.
- What it delivers: *"Leave the world of imagination and conquer the real world by exploring the prototype with real customers"* · find out whether it works for users as intended · **obtain feedback: "love it," "change it," or "leave it"** · gather qualitative *and* quantitative data by asking the right questions in order to discern **whether problems occur with the experience or with the use**.
- **Group size** a **moderator and several observers**, e.g. members of the design team — *"Depending on the setting, not too many observers, though, since it might intimidate the users."* · **Duration** **60–90 min for planning** ("Why, how, and what is to be tested"), depending on complexity; the test itself: **at least 5 users, each test lasting 15 minutes**. *"The more natural the environment, the more truthful the results."* · **Materials** observation rooms with recording software, camera (sound & image), Post-its, flip chart or whiteboard.
- **Named later-stage methods** the book lists as more exact successors: unit test, integration test, functional test, system test, stress test, performance test, usability test, acceptance test, regression test, beta test. Also the **heuristic evaluation** — "an informal appraisal to evaluate or check a product based on a number of agreed-upon best practices, standards, or guidelines."
- **Template — the three question sets by prototype resolution (p. 222), reproduced:**

| **Test lo-fi prototypes** | **Test med-fi prototypes** | **Test hi-fi prototypes** |
|---|---|---|
| *"we might have no more than a rough idea or the proverbial sketch on the napkin"* | *"initial wireframes for our rough concept… neither interactive nor functional but they illustrate what should be used and how"* | *"a simple copy of the ultimate solution, that is, a semi-functional prototype… What is missing is the glamour and beauty of a final product"* |
| What is the problem your idea solves? | Does the prototype do what is expected of it? | Does the prototype do what it is meant to do? |
| How do users solve this problem today? | What is the users' reaction to the product design? | Does the design of the product match its purpose? |
| Can the user think of another product with similar characteristics? | As soon as we show the prototype, does the user understand what it does? | What would the user like to do first with the product? Does this possibility exist? |
| What made other solutions fail? | How does the prototype meet the expectations of the user? | Is the user confused when using the product? |
| Do the users understand the benefits of the product or service? | What features are missing? | Is the user distracted by something when using the product? |
| How does the user rate the product or service? | What is in the wrong place or unnecessary? | Are there any functions that are completely ignored by the user? |
| Can the user think of competing products? | How does it feel to the user when he uses the prototype? | Is the navigation sensible and intuitive? |
| What has the app/website/function been designed for? | **If the user had a magic wand, what would he change on the product?** | Do the users feel that the product was developed for them? |
| Does the potential user actually have a need for this product? | How high is the probability that the potential user will use the finished product in the future? | What would prompt the user to use this product more often? |
| What other objects or interactions does the user himself imagine? | | How likely is it that the user recommends the finished product to a friend? |
| What use scenarios can he/she imagine? | | How would the user describe the product in his own words? |

- **Expert (Natalie Breitschmid, Chief Experience Enthusiast at SINODUS AG; lecturer, Lucerne University of Applied Sciences and Arts)**: ***"Testing is doing. Doing is like wanting, just more blatant. To become successful, choose the earliest day possible to prove whether your idea works or not."*** And: *"No proposition has any intrinsic value: **The only person assigning value to a product or a service is the customer.**"* Her tips:
  - **Test, test, and test again.** *"Don't skip the testing due to time pressure or for other reasons. Testing is too important. Allow for enough time for testing. It is the most important phase. Here is where you learn."*
  - ***"Don't tell or show the test participants how it works; it would automatically influence the way they see the prototype. So let them do it and watch them closely. Ask them why and how they think about it."***
  - **Hand the prototype over** to the users to take with them **for a day or even a week**, so they use it as often as possible; ask them to be **filmed or to live record themselves** while using it.
  - For a **service (app or website)**, user validation goes much faster: **online surveys** integrated into the app or website.
  - The startup statistics she cites: **90% of all start-ups fail (Patel, 2015); over 40% fail because there is no market demand for their product (Griffith, 2014).**

## 4. Solution interview (p. 225)

***I would like…*** *to find out whether a solution is accepted by the user.*

- **Positioning.** *"As the name suggests, solution interviews are a tool used in the test phase **with advanced (high-resolution) prototypes**… In order to make a clear distinction from the testing tools already presented, the solution interview is **mainly used in the solution space**. This is why we focus on the 'acceptance' of a **final prototype or MVP**. In this late phase, solution interviews supply insights on the acceptance of a solution by the user, **right up to the pricing**."*
- What it delivers: whether an intended solution is valued — *"convincing in terms of functionality, user-friendliness, and user experience"* · **questioning the underlying task of the project** — "examine whether you are focusing on the crucial issues" · deeper understanding of needs, behaviors and motivations · **measuring the value of the solution for the user**.
- **Group size** **teams of 2 are ideal** — one is the interviewer, the second **makes notes, observes, and follows up on questions**; if appropriate, a third takes photos or films · **Duration** **20–30 min**; *"30 minutes is a good orientation for a solution interview. **The perceived gain in knowledge is the qualitative criterion for the duration** of the interview."* · **Materials** interview guide, prototype of the solution, paper and pencils, if needed some prototyping material, recording device / video camera / mobile phone.
- **Alternatives** feedback capture grid (p. 217), testing sheet (p. 213), A/B testing (p. 233). **Supporting tools** HMW question (p. 125), persona/user profile (p. 97), various kinds of prototypes (p. 187 ff.).
- **Template — the solution interview canvas (p. 226), reproduced by its labelled blocks:**

| Block | Fields |
|---|---|
| **Solution** | Solution to be tested |
| **Context → Task** | Design challenge of the project · "How might we…" question |
| **Context → Goal** | Interview goal · key question that should have been answered after the interview |
| **Context → Personas** | Personas and their focus of the solution · point of view statement |
| **Interview planning → Candidates** | Identify candidates similar to the personas · determine the number of interviews required |
| **Interview planning → Team** | Size of the interview team · role assignment |
| **Interview planning → Material** | Core equipment · reference to the last interview · supplementing materials |
| **Interview guide → Agenda** | 1. Warm-up (time span: ___) 2. Introduction to the context (___) 3. Experience the solution (___) 4. Summary (___) |
| **Interview guide → Content** | Discussion points · specific questions · form of presentation of the solution |

- **Procedure.**
  1. **Define the interview goal.** Reflect on the task and the persona the solution should address. *"Depending on the current phase in the macro-cycle, the goal is to **check the impact** of the solution or **measure the value** of the solution."*
  2. **Determine the interview team, including role assignment.** When selecting candidates, make sure they **resemble the persona** the solution is intended for. Think about what to take along (e.g. reference points from previous discussions).
  3. **Plan the interview guide in four phases:**
     - **Warm-up** — "Create an atmosphere that allows for uninhibited statements. **Check the similarity between interviewee and persona.**"
     - **Introduction to the context** — define what context information about the usage scenario should be given.
     - **Experience the solution** — *"Let the interviewee work out the solution by himself; ask him to 'think aloud.'"*
     - **Summary** — *"Summarize the statements of the conversation partners in your own words. **Watch the reaction.**"*
- **Expert (Niels Feldmann, Lecturer for Service Design Thinking, Karlsruhe Institute of Technology)**: *"At IDEO, they say design thinking is 'enlightened trial and error.'"* On why it matters: *"Solution interviews are **a moment of truth**… In the solution interview, we now compare our interpretation of reality with reality itself."* His tips:
  - **Attention:** *"One of the greatest challenges is to avoid misleading statements of the interviewees **on account of politeness**."*
  - **Select candidates:** *"Mix people who are known from the problem interviews with unbiased people who now test the solution."*
  - **Define an interview team:** use **third parties as interviewers if the (technical) language is not known** — "Specialist staff, children, and minorities give different answers to their peers than to nonspecialist colleagues, adults, or members of the majorities."
  - **Choose the material carefully:** *"Unlike the tests in earlier phases, where paper mock-ups are used deliberately, **a high-resolution prototype is needed** (e.g. to test the monetary value of a solution)."*
  - **Get a feel for the other person:** consider personality traits too — *"**Innovators give different answers than the early majority**, for instance."*
  - ***"Study and don't sell!"*** — *"Let the people who are interviewed explore the solution themselves. Demonstrations put interviewees too easily on preconceived paths. To have **few discussion points** is helpful in the early phases; **specific questions** are helpful in later phases."*
  - **Record keeping:** *"Film the interview if possible. **Behavior reveals much truth beyond the spoken words.**"*

## 5. Structured usability testing (p. 229)

***I would like…*** *to test my prototypes with potential users at defined and uniform conditions.*

- **Positioning.** *"**Anything that is 'operable' can be tested**; this applies to both physical and digital products. With usability testing, it can be checked whether something works **effectively, efficiently, and satisfactorily** for the user. The testing should be as specific, frequent, and as early as possible. This requires **real users who perform given specific tasks** with the prototype at defined and uniform conditions."* The uniform structure is the point: *"it allows for testing and comparing **several ideas or variants on the basis of the same criteria**."* Before starting, *"it is important to be aware of what is to be tested and how it is to be measured."*
- **Named variants:** *hallway, guerrilla, laboratory test, field test, and so on.*
- **Group size** **at least 2 people** — one guides and supports the user, another or several others observe and document · **Duration** **40–90 min**; *"Varies depending on the complexity of the prototype and the task to be performed. **The tasks and the sequence must be defined beforehand.**"* · **Materials** prototype (hardware or software), camera for video and sound recordings, **script (guide) with defined test scenarios (tasks)**, material to take notes, **evaluation grid**.
- **Alternatives** solution interview (p. 225), A/B testing (p. 233), consumer clinics, focus group. **Supporting tools** various kinds of prototypes (p. 187 ff.), persona/user profile (p. 97), 5W+H questions (p. 71).
- **Procedure — three phases.**
  1. **Planning phase.** Draw up a concept describing *what it is all about; what the test object is; what exactly is to be found out; which assumptions and hypotheses already exist; and what the chronological sequence should be.* Then choose the location, define the roles (moderator, observers, etc.) and the test persons, define the **exact test scenarios (tasks)**, prepare the documents, and finally **conduct a "dry run" to verify that everything works as it should.**
  2. **Test phase.** Conduct the actual test. *"Try to stick to the sequence and test scenarios as closely as possible and **always provide all test persons with the same information**."*
  3. **Final phase.** Evaluate and document the collected findings and, if desired, present the results to the relevant stakeholders. Use the results to continue improving usability.
- **Expert (Pascal Henzmann, Project Manager, innovation management team at Helbling Technik AG, Switzerland)**: *"Since it is not the management that buys the products but the customers, making their needs the entry point is crucial."* And the closing quote he cites: ***"Remember that your system will be tested for usability — even if you don't do so yourself." — Jakob Nielsen.*** His tips:
  - **Test scenarios must be clearly defined.** *"They help to determine which aspects of the prototype should be tested."*
  - **Score each task** — success factors per task can be rated as ***"success," "struggle," or "fail"*** to make subsequent evaluation easier.
  - **Match the level of detail** of the prototype to the test scenarios: *"Particularly in the case of fundamental questions (e.g. about the operation sequence), unnecessary details should be omitted."*
  - **Never fall in love with your prototypes** — *"Don't waste too much time on details. Otherwise, you'll have a hard time saying good-bye to your idea later."* And: *"Even an existing product or **competitive product** can be tested in a very early phase of a project."*
  - **Test users and moderation:** test persons must match the selected personas as closely as possible; ***"The moderation is best done by an independent person who is not involved (objectivity)."*** A positive and grateful attitude toward the test person is vital — ***"the system is tested, not the person."***
  - **The test persons should think aloud**, relating everything they think.
  - ***"No leading questions!"*** — the printed example: *"'Can you click it anywhere else?' already tells the user that he can click it somewhere else."*
  - ***"If a user gets stuck, don't help but ask what should happen next."***
  - **Documentation:** digital screen capturing or video is helpful for communicating results — in the case of physical products, video.

## 6. A/B testing (p. 233)

***I would like…*** *to review an assumption or compare two variants (in terms of quantity or quality) to find out what the preferences of the users/customers are.*

- **Positioning.** *"The A/B test can be used as a **stand-alone test or as an expansion of a prototype test**… It is a simple tool for testing **two variants of a prototype simultaneously**. The test of the prototype usually answers a question with different characteristics."* It is "quite well suited to advance an existing prototype/MVP or to test a new variant in comparison with a basic prototype." **Make it very clear before the test what is to be tested and compared (e.g. by means of key figures).** The core psychological argument: *"**Most users find it easier to give feedback when comparing two prototypes than when they are asked to comment on one prototype.**"*
- What it delivers: a true A/B test, or several variants as a **multi-variants test / split testing** · quantitative evaluation · qualitative survey with the number and content of feedbacks evaluated · comparison of **individual variants of a function** (e.g. buttons, visuals, arrangement).
- **Group size** **1–2 people per test**, depending on procedure and tool support — *"With tools, 1 person suffices; without, at least 2 people."* · **Duration** **5–15 min**, depending on the number of users and whether a tool or manual evaluation is used; *"Make time for preparation and follow-up."* · **Materials** different materials depending on the prototype, pen and paper to capture the feedback, **online prototype via software and voting tool**.
- **Alternatives** structured usability test (p. 229), solution interview (p. 225). **Supporting tools** powerful questions in experience testing (p. 221), online tools that support the entire process of an A/B or multi-variant test including the evaluation.
- **Procedure — six steps.**
  1. **Define the basic prototype** and decide who is to be the test group (selection of the target group).
  2. **Consider variants** of the prototype and choose **two** to compare. **Define key figures** for what kind of testing is to be done (quantitative or qualitative).
  3. For **quantitative** tests, **assign the users at random** and conduct the test.
  4. Evaluate the results.
  5. **Use the preferred variant for improving the prototype.**
  6. Repeat the tests with new variants, or perform another test for validation.
- **The differentiation the book prints as a note:**
  - **Quantitative A/B test** — *"The user group is divided (x% variant A, y% variant B)."*
  - **Qualitative A/B test** — *"The variants are tested against one another (**all users see variants A and B**)."*
- **Expert (Christian Langrock, Innovation Manager, Hamburger Hochbahn AG, Germany)**: *"Agility and design thinking are based on a similar mindset that helps us focus on the customer again and develop the right products and services for and with the customer in the **VUCA world**"* (volatility, uncertainty, complexity, ambiguity). His observation: *"In design thinking, it is **not yet so common to test prototypes against each other. Usually, one prototype that is favored by the team is tested.**"* His tips:
  - ***"The test is always preceded by the consideration of which assumption is to be tested."***
  - Decide first whether qualitative or quantitative feedback is needed — the answer determines the procedure.
  - ***"Only check one assumption at a time"*** (e.g. design, visuals, buttons, wording, or process). His printed example: *"Should the log-in process in a newly developed app take place in one step or be divided into several steps?"*
  - *"Each variant in the A/B test should **support the assumption** despite different characteristics."*
  - **Define the evaluation criteria in advance**, be flexible, and *"adapt the prototype if several users give you the same feedback on things they don't like."*
  - Decide before the test **which target group (persona)** should test, and **whether all users see both variants or the users are split into two groups**.
  - **Use web tools/apps** — especially for digital prototypes and testing web front-end solutions.
  - **The number of tests is not limited** — *"Understand the test as a cycle with a non-limited number of runs. **The simpler and more trivial the test seems to be, the more insightful the result may be.**"*

## Mental Models

- **Testing reconnects to the problem space.** A test does not only grade the prototype; it "refines our view of the problem and the user," which can yield a new point of view. The cycle is a loop, not a finish line.
- **Testing is need finding.** The same rules as the empathy interview apply — listen, watch, don't sell, ask why.
- **The person who assigns value is the customer.** "No proposition has any intrinsic value."
- **The prototype's resolution selects the question set.** Lo-fi asks about the problem; med-fi asks about the concept and the sequence; hi-fi asks about behavior, navigation and recommendation.
- **Comparison is easier than judgment.** People give better feedback on "A or B?" than on "what do you think of this?" — the entire argument for A/B testing.
- **Uniform conditions buy comparability.** The value of structured usability testing is not the single result but that several variants can be judged on the same criteria.
- **A struggling user is data, not a problem to be rescued.** "If a user gets stuck, don't help but ask what should happen next."
- **The system is tested, not the person.**
- **The gain in knowledge is the clock.** For the solution interview, perceived knowledge gain — not the schedule — is the qualitative criterion for how long to keep going.

## Anti-patterns

- **Skipping testing under time pressure** — "Testing is too important."
- **Selling the idea to the tester** instead of learning from them. Never "sell."
- **Explaining or demonstrating the prototype first** — it "automatically influences the way they see the prototype"; demonstrations "put interviewees too easily on preconceived paths."
- **Asking questions too early in the session** — "Listen, watch, and try not to ask questions at first."
- **Leading questions** — "Can you click it anywhere else?" already gives the answer away.
- **Politeness bias** — interviewees making misleading statements to be nice; the greatest challenge of the solution interview.
- **Too many observers in the room** — "it might intimidate the users."
- **Testing without defined criteria or a defined verification threshold** — decided *before* the test, not after.
- **Testing more than one assumption at a time** in an A/B test.
- **Confusing A/B testing with testing two different ideas** — "A/B testing does not mean testing two different ideas but **two variants of a prototype**."
- **Testing a solution interview on a low-resolution mock-up** — the late-phase interview needs a high-resolution prototype, especially to test monetary value.
- **A moderator who is invested in the outcome** — usability moderation should be done by an independent person.
- **Test persons who don't match the persona.**
- **Filming without permission**, or without a data use agreement when the footage will be reused.
- **Single tests treated as conclusive** — "as a rule, we need several tests to be able to make accurate statements"; 2–3 per grid, at least 5 users per experience test.

| Hi-fi / semi-functional | Structured usability testing, A/B testing | Operability, navigation, preference between variants |
| Final prototype / MVP | Solution interview | Acceptance, value, up to pricing — the solution space |

**Test-role checklist (from the testing sheet)**

| Role | Responsibility |
|---|---|
| Tester / interviewer | Runs the test, asks the key questions, asks "Why?" repeatedly |
| Note-taker | Documents the findings, records the most important quotes |
| Observer (optional) | Watches behavior, emotions, conflicting body language |
| Photographer/filmer (optional, solution interview) | Photos or film — with permission |

## Worked Example

**Testing sheet, Lilly's team (p. 216).** "Lilly's team tests **simultaneously the customer needs and the existing interest as well as the functionality** with various prototypes and, later, MVPs. The team knows that the tests are extremely important. **The better the tests are planned and carried out, the better the findings.** They always do the testing **in the context and at the users' premises, if possible**. The test criteria, sequence, and the roles in the test have been defined. **The team works well together, so roles can be swapped sometimes.** If, for example, the person who has been assigned to observe and take notes has a better rapport with the test person, the observer and the tester can swap roles."
In the comic, the test person narrates the interaction with the robot prototype: *"Wow, am I curious as to how the voice input works!"* — *"I probably have to switch it on here — and then? What do I have to do now!"* — *"Move forward!"* — *"WOW. It moves. 'Move slower!'"*
Key learnings, verbatim: *"Through the tests, we get feedback on the prototype **and on the user as well**. Plan the test situation, think about what the test sequence will be like and which team member will play which role. The documentation of the tests is important so the results can later be shared with the team."*

**Feedback capture grid, Lilly's team (p. 220).** "Lilly's team uses the feedback capture grid in **almost all phases** of the design thinking cycle. They use it mainly for the documentation and summary of the tests with the users. Lilly and the design thinking team also like to use it for **reviewing an iteration as part of the retrospective**. Lilly highly values the direct, open feedback and constructive criticism."
The comic shows the feedback protocol being enforced live: *"Please always make a positive statement first and then a wish. Whose turn is it?"* — *"What I liked in particular was that we created a simple clickable prototype with Proto.io that enabled the user to have the experience."*
Key learnings: *"Always conduct interviews with two people. **Don't sell the idea but listen with curiosity, observe, and learn.** Discuss the findings with the team and formulate a new shared point of view from them."*

**Powerful questions, Lilly's team (p. 224).** "After various low-resolution prototypes, Lilly's team has **programmed a small robot to test the human-robot interaction**. Before the tests, the design thinking team went through **a lot of brainstorming regarding the test requirements**, so that later the results of the experience testing actually correspond to the desired experience. The valuable test findings are immediately integrated by the team into the next prototype, and **that is done until customers love the prototype**."
Key learnings: *"The only person assigning value to a product or a service is the user/customer. Ask the user in a targeted manner and **give him the feeling that his answers are valuable**. Have the user/customer think aloud during the test."*

**Solution interview, Lilly's team (p. 228).** "The solution interview helps the team to validate the solution idea. **Lilly uses it to challenge the ideas of the team.** The team frequently has a hard time letting go of their own ideas. She likes saying, ***'Love is for people, not for prototypes,'*** but that is of little use here. **It is far more helpful when the customer says it.** At times, Lilly and her team have used the solution interview in earlier project phases to question the solution or throw it overboard. But mainly they use solution interviews in later phases for a thorough test of the solution."
In the comic: *"First take the prototype into your hands. In which cases could you use it in this way?"* — *"Yes, for example, digital transformation workshops or to conduct change projects…."* — *"Thank you for the interesting discussion. **Is there anything else you'd like to add?**"*
Key learnings, verbatim: *"Solution interviews are used **to gain knowledge — not to sell something**. Well-thought-out solution interviews lead to meaningful and compelling answers. Statements not only deliver insights but also observations. **The result is the interpretation of the data, not the data itself.**"*

**Structured usability testing, Lilly's teams (p. 232).** "Lilly's individual teams want to bring **more structure** to the testing of their prototypes. The team begins with drawing up **specific tasks (test scenarios) for each prototype**. Simultaneously, the team has already begun to **look for test persons who match the target customer (persona)**. The team's plan is to have the test persons conduct all tests in interaction with the prototype **during one whole day**."
In the comic: *"Is the test structure right this way? So we can really test the usability? What do you think?"* — *"Stop, guys! Come now! We discussed long enough, **let's test it with real users**."* — *"Cool. Can it also operate a vacuum cleaner?"*
Key learnings: *"Set clear-cut goals for the usability test. Select test persons from the target group and urge them to 'think aloud.' Avoid asking any leading questions. **It's better to run first tests early on with only a few subjects/users than later with many.**"*

**A/B testing, Lilly's team (p. 236).** "Lilly uses A/B testing **only for quantitative online tests**, for example, when she wants to investigate **which landing page brings a higher conversion**. Lilly's teams, however, also use the tool for the test of **physical prototypes**, for example, to find out which property is important for the customer and is more appreciated by the target group. For this purpose they create **two variants that differ only in one property**. After the actual test, the team learns many intriguing aspects of the users' needs, **especially during personal conversations**."
Key learnings: *"A/B testing does not mean testing two different ideas but two variants of a prototype. The test target groups should match the target persona. Make a decision on the setup prior to the test, that is, whether qualitative or quantitative feedback is needed."*

## Key Takeaways

- Testing feeds back into *understand* and *observe*, not just into the prototype — expect a new point of view to come out of it.
- Which test tool you use is decided by the prototype's resolution, not by preference.
- Plan before you test: location (ideally the user's premises), roles (tester / note-taker / observer), key questions, and the **verification criteria**.
- Listen, watch, don't explain, don't sell. Ask "Why?" several times. Have the user think aloud.
- Document with quotes, photos and short videos — the documentation is what the team can actually use later; get permission first.
- Feedback capture grid: A3, one per tester per interview, 10–15 minutes, 2–3 tests minimum, then cluster by answer and persona.
- Experience testing: at least 5 users × 15 minutes, in the most natural environment you can arrange.
- Solution interview is a late-phase, solution-space tool needing a high-resolution prototype: two people, ~30 minutes, warm-up → context → experience → summary.
- Usability testing needs uniform conditions, defined tasks, a dry run, an uninvolved moderator, and a success/struggle/fail rating per task.
- A/B testing: one assumption, two variants differing in one property, criteria fixed in advance, unlimited runs.
