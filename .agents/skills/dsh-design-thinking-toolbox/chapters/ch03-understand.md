# Chapter 3 — Understand

*(Book pages 47–90; 10 tools)*

## Core Idea

"A good understanding of the problem is the be-all and end-all in design thinking. In the 'understand' phase, we want to make ourselves familiar with the problem. In order to substantiate the design challenge, we formulate a question, also referred to as the 'problem statement.' Through various techniques and tools, the problem statement can be broadened or narrowed down… The aim is to learn as much as possible about the needs of a potential user. These findings in turn help us to sharpen the problem statement iteratively and come to a common understanding of the problem on the team."

## Frameworks Introduced

Every tool in the Toolbox is described in the same slots: ***I would like…*** (one-sentence intent) → more information → what you can do with it → alternative tools → supporting tools → **group size / typical duration / materials** → **template + numbered procedure** → a named expert's tips → the Lilly use case with key learnings. This chapter reports each tool in that order, compressed.

---

## 1. Problem statement (p. 49)

***I would like…*** *to define the key problem statement coherently and capture it in a simple sentence.*

- **Group size** 3–5 from the design team, optionally 1–2 stakeholders or the client · **Duration** 30–40 min ("formulating normally requires some time as well as blood, sweat, and tears"; short cycles of at least half an hour are useful) · **Materials** pens, Post-its, several A4 portrait sheets and A3 landscape sheets.
- **Template — the four framing questions**, each on its own A4 portrait sheet with space beneath for answers, in different colors for questions vs. answers:
  1. **What is the problem? Why is it a problem?**
  2. **Who has the problem? Who has a need?**
  3. **When and where does the problem occur?**
  4. **How is it solved today?**
- **Procedure.** Step 1 — produce **at least 10 such problem definitions**. Step 2 — attach the sheets to the wall with an A3 landscape sheet underneath; consolidate or select the most appropriate (e.g. via dot voting, p. 159). Step 3 — transfer systematically into an overarching problem in the form: ***"How might we redesign… [what?] …[for whom?] …so that…[his need]…is satisfied?"***
- **Alternatives**: design brief (p. 29), design principles (p. 53). **Supports**: context mapping (133), cause–effect diagrams, HMW questions (125), 5W+H (71).
- **Expert (Stefano Vannotti, ZHdK)**: reframe the problem — "it is certainly helpful for a better understanding"; in certain cases formulate several problem statements from different user perspectives. "Often, problems, causes, and effects get all muddled together. Try to create structural clarity."

## 2. Design principles (p. 53)

***I would like…*** *to define guidelines that constitute the framework for the team during a design thinking project.*

- **Group size** 5–12 (design team + selected stakeholders + client), usually led by the facilitator or project manager · **Duration** 90–180 min ("it can take up to several hours") · **Materials** whiteboard, pens, Post-its, colored dots for the vote, history/documentation of other design challenges.
- **Template — the "basket" and the pyramid.** Procedure: Step 1 — sketch a basket and a pyramid on the whiteboard; everyone writes principles on Post-its and places them in the basket, explaining *why* each is a design principle. Step 2 — when the basket is full, sort the principles onto the pyramid in **three groups**, by the rule *the higher up the pyramid, the more project-specific the principle*; general principles at the bottom. Step 3 — vote (e.g. glue dots) to reduce to **a maximum of three per section, i.e. a maximum of nine per pyramid**. Step 4 — post the adopted principles where the team constantly sees them.
- Inspiration source named in the book: `www.designprinciplesftw.com`.
- **Expert (Slavo Tuleja, ŠKODA AUTO DigiLab)**: "Design principles act as guard rails across the entire design cycle." Start with why (Why this project? Why now? Why in this scope?); show it boldly (huge poster); show, don't tell — decision-makers want to see principles in action.

## 3. Interview for empathy (p. 57)

*(including the variation: in-depth interview with a question map and a journey map)*

***I would like…*** *to consider the problem from the point of view of the user and build empathy with a user.*

- **Group size** 1–2 — "always best with two people: one conducts the interview, and the second person takes notes on emotions and body language" · **Duration** 30–60 min · **Materials** notepad/template, pen, camera or smartphone (with permission).
- **Template fields**: *Existing assumptions about the persona and the problem* | *How might we build a relationship with the interviewee in order to give him/her such a good feeling that he/she shares personal stories in the context of the problem?* | *Key questions for the exploration of stories* | *Keywords and topics in connection with the emotions shown* | *Outline of the story*.
- **Rules of conduct.** Introduce yourselves and explain the problem; emphasize the interview is **not about finding a solution** but learning motives. If they tell their story, interrupt as little as possible and do not influence with your assumptions. Listen sincerely; use open W+H questions; **avoid yes/no or single-word questions**. Ask additional questions not directly associated with the problem, or introduce statements that confuse at first but open other perspectives. Watch gestures and body language; note and clarify contradictions between signals and answers.
- **Variation — in-depth interview**: **group size 2–3** (1–2 observers, one interviewer) · **duration 30–120 min** (interview up to an hour; add up to 90 minutes for documentation and transfer to journey stages) · materials: paper for the question map, pens, visualization of the journey stages on a large whiteboard. Step 1 — build a **question map**: topic at the center of the sheet, W+H questions radiating out. *"Under no circumstances use a structured questionnaire. It would only serve the purpose of confirming our assumptions."* Step 2 — work with **journey stages** alongside the question map to spot patterns early; ask the interviewee to sketch or lay out a chronological sequence. Example probe set for an online-shopping user: where they gathered information beforehand, what information was valuable, when and why they decided to purchase, how easy payment was.
- **Expert (Adharsh Dhandapani, IBM)**: "Mirror neurons are triggered when people perform an action and also when they see other people performing the same action — with no difference between the two! It follows that mirror neurons are the reason that humans can empathize." Hence: address as many senses as possible; meet users face-to-face in their natural environment. Also: assure confidentiality; state from first contact that you are reaching out strictly for research and never as a marketing/sales opportunity; validate with role-playing or shadowing; share via storytelling.

## 4. Explorative interview (p. 63)

***I would like…*** *to learn more about the user before thinking about new products.*

- **Group size** 1–2 · **Duration** 60–120 min (plus ~40 min to develop the interview guide) · **Materials** interview guide, pen and paper, camera/smartphone if permitted.
- **Template — the three-part interview guide:**
  1. **Introduction** — broad questions to open the conversation and break the ice. Printed examples: *"What is your profession?"*, *"Tell me what you experienced recently."*, *"Tell me what annoyed you recently."*
  2. **Get to know the entire story** — questions that surface hopes, fears, motivations. Printed probes: *"What…?"*, *"What is the biggest challenge in this context?"*, *"Wait… what exactly do you mean by that?"*, *"What happened before/after/during?"*, *"Why?… (pause)"*.
  3. **Conclusion** — explain what happens with the answers and thank the interviewee. *"Always be appreciative!"* Closing question: *"If you had one wish to make…"*
- **Procedure notes.** Hold the interview at the location of typical use or where the participant feels comfortable — *"the most suitable place may be his or her home."* Be prepared to depart from the guide. Ask what/why/how, never yes/no. Ask for concrete examples to avoid standard answers ("When did you last…"). Dig deeper ("What does this mean to you…", "Why did you…"). Speak the participant's language, avoid technical terms — *"the interviewee is the expert in his life."*
- **Framing contrast printed on the page**: **product-centered vs. human-centered**.
- **Expert (Rasmus Thomsen, IS IT A BIRD)**: "If we asked a 5 year old what a radio is, we will get an answer that is completely different from the answer we get from an adult." Use sketches and pictures for abstract topics; ask participants to *show* rather than talk; ask about objects with personal meaning; **good conversations have room for silence** — use short lulls so participants can analyze the situation.

## 5. Ask 5x why (p. 67)

***I would like…*** *to understand a problem in depth, not just scratch the surface.*

- **Group size** 2 (one converses, one documents) · **Duration** 30–40 min · **Materials** notepad, pen, smartphone/camera with consent.
- **Template — the 5x why ladder** (each rung has a printed effect label):
  | Rung | Prompt | Effect label |
  |---|---|---|
  | 0 | Detailed description of the problem (use photos or sketches) | — |
  | 1. Why is it a problem? | What is the problem? What are its symptoms? | **Consequence** |
  | 2. Why? | Why does the problem occur? What technology is used? | **Direct impact** |
  | 3. Why? | What could be another cause of the problem? | **Cause → effect** |
  | 4. Why? | How could the problem be avoided? | **Organizational hurdles** |
  | 5. Why? | The systematic approach — might it prevent the occurrence? | **Systematic hurdles** |
- **Stopping rule**: "Stop asking 'Why?' once it no longer makes sense." Then explore another problem the same way or go in-depth on the answers given. Integrate simple prototypes and sketches into the solution discussion to get first reactions.
- **Variation**: also ask **5x "How?"** — why-questions review the problem, how-questions determine how to solve it.
- **Expert (Florence Mathieu, Aina)**: don't assume the root; don't stop asking too soon — *"We keep asking until it gets uncomfortable or until the real cause is discovered."* **Check with reverse questions** in if-then form. Her printed example: *Why did you get sick? — Because I spent some time outside in the fresh air on the weekend.* Reverse: *If you hadn't spent time outside in the fresh air, then you wouldn't be sick now?* — *"The lack of a jacket was probably more of a cause than being in the fresh air."*

## 6. 5W+H questions (p. 71)

***I would like…*** *to gain in-depth insights as well as new findings and information in order to grasp the problem or situation holistically or simply to find relevant questions for an interview.*

- **Group size** 3–5 ("the team size is secondary") · **Duration** 30–60 min · **Materials** printed 5W+H grid or sketched on paper, pens, Post-its.
- **Template — the six-column grid**, with the printed seed questions:

| Who | What | When | Where | Why | How |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Who is involved? | What do we already know about the problem? | When did the problem start? | Where does the problem occur? | Why is the problem important? | How could this problem be an opportunity? |
| Who is affected by the situation? | What would we like to know? | When do people want to see results? | Where was it resolved before? | Why does it occur? | How could it be solved? |
| Who is the decision maker? | What are the assumptions that should be scrutinized? | — | Where did similar situations exist? | Why was it not yet solved? | What has already been tried to resolve the problem? |

- **Two application situations, as printed.** *Situation 1 — understand the problem better*: raise and answer all relevant W+H questions; skip any that don't make sense in context; look for uncertainties and derive interview questions. *Situation 2 — learn more about the needs*: prepare sub-questions as a mind map, vary and "play" with them, build the interview questions or a question map from them.
- **Expert (Kristine Biegman, launchlabs Berlin)**: ask several times even when you think you know the answer; find **more than one answer per question** — conflicting answers are of particular interest. **Turn questions into the negative** to create new perspectives: *"When does the problem NOT occur?"*, *"Who is NOT affected?"* And: "No fake news — always go with the facts," underpinning answers with desk research and data analytics.

## 7. Jobs to be done (JTBD) (p. 75)

***I would like…*** *to focus the problem solution on things that are an added value for the customer and help him accomplish his tasks.*

- Attributed to **Clayton Christensen** — *"Customers don't just buy products, they hire them to do a job."*
- **Group size** teams of all sizes, efficient size **≤5**; direct customer interviews best in pairs · **Duration** 60–120 min overall; **30–60 min per problem statement per interview**; conduct **at least two interviews** · **Materials** Post-its, pens, markers, flip chart/wall, optionally templates with sample customer tasks.
- **Template — the three-slot sentence:**

> **When… [situation], I want to… [motivation], so I can… [expected result].**

  Printed example: *"When I take a photograph with the digital camera, I want to be able to edit it in such a way that it looks like it's been taken by a professional photographer, so I can show perfect [photos]"* — with a second motivation variant, *"I want to be able to edit it in an easy and simple manner."*
- **Procedure.** Capture tasks along the **customer journey** (p. 103) or across the product life cycle; write each task individually. Ask *why* for each task ("Why does the customer buy from us?"), then ask **"why not"** ("Why does the customer not buy from us?"). From the JTBD sentences generate ideas and the experiences and functions critical to a user.
- **Key emphasis**: "The aim is, above all, to satisfy the deeper **social, emotional, or personal** tasks of the customers."
- **Expert (Patrick Schüffel, School of Management Fribourg in Singapore / STO Global-X)**: customers are reluctant to talk about needs but able to talk about the tasks they must perform. Consider adjacent and emotional tasks and touch points across the life cycle; **do not combine tasks — measure them separately**; each task can be measured by KPIs defined beforehand; the expected result is "independent of solutions" and should be verified with quantitative survey techniques. Also look at **pains and gains**, and at **workarounds and improvised solutions** — "They show points where customers invest time and money to solve their problems."

## 8. Extreme users / lead users (p. 79)

***I would like…*** *to find new, innovative ideas and user needs (as yet) unknown to the average user.*

- **Definitions as printed.** A **lead user** often appears as an innovator himself; has needs way ahead of the mass market; has had a strong need already regardless of whether a product exists and may have created a solution — since they have a lot to gain. *"In the case of complex products, the traditional lead user methods hit their limits."* An **extreme user** goes beyond usual usage limits or uses the product/system/space significantly more than an average user (**taxi driver vs. normal car driver; extreme athletes vs. casual athletes**). *"The needs uncovered with groups of extreme users are frequently also latent needs in the wider population."* **Power users** are heavy users characterized by high intensity of use.
- **Dimensions printed for finding extremes**: age (child ↔ elderly), development (developing country ↔ sci-fi/future), ability, time, frequency, experience — with average users in the middle.
- **Group size** 2 · **Duration** 30–120 min · **Materials** notepad, pen, smartphone/camera with permission. "It usually takes a lot of time and persistence to seek out the lead user. Extreme users are easier to find."
- **Two procedures, side by side:**

| Lead user (hard to find, usually very experienced and well connected, willing to invest time and resources, very good insights) | Extreme user (easy to find, useful additional information) |
|---|---|
| 1. Identify trend | 1. Identify extreme users |
| 2. Identification of lead users | 2. Determine observation and interaction (questioning, shadowing, or group interview) |
| 3. Co-creation with lead user | 3. Use findings for persona, sharpen problem statement, or collect initial ideas |
| 4. Reflection and projection of the results onto the problem statement | |

- Always ask lead users for recommendations for other potential lead users. When observing any of these user types, **look specifically for workarounds or previously unknown solution alternatives** — then validate later by testing prototypes with the target users too.
- **Expert (Katja Hölttä-Otto, Aalto University Design Factory)**: interaction with extreme users "is as if someone handed me a magnifying glass." Her wheelchair project insight: these aids should work not only on the road but on uneven surfaces such as lawn or gravel. Work with **groups of 8–12 extreme users** — the interaction within the group yields additional insights; always observe and interview several. Stay in contact through WhatsApp groups or temporary blogs.

## 9. Stakeholder map (p. 83)

***I would like…*** *to get an overview of all stakeholders, that is, organizations and people who have a claim or interest in the problem and a potential solution.*

- **Group size** the design team complemented by a representative of the respective stakeholder (the printed figure did not survive extraction); often the design team drafts first and validates in follow-up discussions · **Duration** 60–240 min depending on complexity — *"often it takes some time before all parties involved speak openly about stakeholders and their needs"* · **Materials** large sheet of paper, pens, large square table or whiteboard, **Lego or Playmobil figurines to assign a character to individual actors**.
- **Procedure.** Step 1 — define the use case (a product, project, or collaboration between departments). Step 2 — list all stakeholders, deepening understanding with use-case-specific questions:
  - Who will benefit from the success? Who has an interest in it being a success?
  - Who do we collaborate with? Who provides us with valuable ideas?
  - How can sales and marketing make a mark?
  - **Who is blocking the idea, and for what reasons? Who benefits from a failure?**
  Step 3 — draw the map, place the stakeholders, then draw the **connections** between them. Reflect and determine next steps, actions, and consequences.
- **Connection notation, as printed:**
  | Symbol | Meaning |
  |---|---|
  | Solid line | Close relationship (frequent contact, exchange of information, coordination, mutual trust, common interests) |
  | Broken line | Weak or informal relationship; a question mark shows an unclear relationship |
  | Double/box line | Institutionally established alliances and cooperative projects |
  | Arrow | Flow direction, e.g. of information and values |
  | Lightning sign | Tensions, conflicts, or dangers between stakeholders; broken or injured relationships |
- **Alternative**: power-interest matrix. **Supports**: 5W+H (71), AEIOU (107), brainstorming (151).
- **Expert (Amber Dubinsky, THES – TauscHaus – EduSpace)**: *"Only when you are able to visualize 'it' have you really understood 'it.'"* Select participants for expert knowledge **plus** fresh perspective; get away from general profiles and be as specific as possible; use different figures (e.g. animals from Lego Fabuland) and cords/pipe cleaners in different colors for connections; communicate results and update throughout the project — a stakeholder map for implementation "can be instrumental for its success."

## 10. Emotional response cards (p. 87)

***I would like…*** *to learn more about a user's feelings when interacting with him to obtain better interview results and insights.*

- **Origin**: based on the **Microsoft Desirability Toolkit** — the "Microsoft reaction cards" first described by **Joey Benedeck and Trish Miner in 2002** in *"Measuring Desirability"*, then on the basis of **118 cards**. *"Fifty emotional response cards have proven useful to us."* (The 50 adjectives themselves are not printed in the book.)
- **Group size** 1–2 (ideally one observes while the other poses questions; works with one interviewer) · **Duration** 15–20 min per run (~5 min preparation; 10–15 min per run) · **Materials** 50 emotional response cards, notepad, pen.
- **Procedure.** Step 1 — print the 50 cards and glue them on thin cardboard. Step 2 — lay the cards on a separate table in any order. Step 3 — after the test, ask the user to **select 3 cards that best describe their experience** with the product; tell them the adjective need not be exact and **may be negative** — it is only a trigger for an emotion. Let them show emotion and talk to themselves; write down anything conspicuous; as each card is picked, set it aside and write the adjective with space for later insights. Step 4 — explore each of the 3 adjectives with "why" questions and supplementary probes such as *"What did you expect instead?"* Step 5 — record statements as **direct quotes**; photograph the map situation or record the stories on video.
- **Two calibrated timings from the expert (a UX consultant/lecturer at Zühlke)**: for **vision clarification with a client** — 5 minutes for product considerations + 5 minutes for "why" questions; for **prototype response** — ~3 minutes for card selection + ~10 minutes for the stories per card. Interviewing the CEO and the product manager reveals *"on which level the interviewees answer (strategy, function, interaction, aesthetics)."*
- **Sequencing warning**: if you query the current situation and then the desired future situation, complete **all** tools on the current situation first — otherwise questions about the current situation get contaminated.

## Anti-patterns

- Using a **structured questionnaire** in an empathy or in-depth interview: "It would only serve the purpose of confirming our assumptions."
- Selling instead of learning: explicitly stated for interview for empathy ("reaching out strictly for research purposes"), the feedback capture grid, and solution interviews.
- Asking "why" only once — hidden problems stay hidden.
- Stopping the stakeholder list at general profiles rather than named, specific actors.
- Skipping the negative form of W+H questions and thereby missing who is *not* affected and when the problem does *not* occur.
- Asking about a desired future situation before finishing the current-situation instruments.

## Worked Example

**Problem statement, Lilly's team (pp. 51–52).** "Lilly describes the problem. With their initial research, the teams supplement the problem. They analyze it by raising different W+H questions. Then the various problems are stated, and a specific actor as well as the context are described. These elements are integrated in a jointly formulated problem statement **in reverse order**. The discussion helps to develop a common language and get an understanding for the target group." The printed template shows the four sheets in play — *What problem? · Who has the problem? Which customer? · When and where does the problem occur? · What is our problem statement?* — with the final sentence forming around "How might we define a [consultancy service]…" and cues visible in the comic for *local culture* and *large corporations*. **Key learnings**: "The development of a clear and concise problem definition frequently requires multiple rounds. The joy in precise formulation and choice of words is a crucial factor here. Create variants and discuss them in the group."

**5W+H, Lilly's team (p. 73).** "For the preparation of in-depth interviews with decision makers in Singapore, the team is divided up. W+H questions aim at a better understanding of the problem. **In the end, the design team has collected more than 40 different W+H questions** that will help them learn more about the digital transformation of companies in Southeast Asia."

**Extreme users, Lilly's team (p. 81).** "A **gamer** is an extreme user. He is at home only in his virtual world and no longer notices many things in the real world. But he has very specific ideas about what should be well-designed virtual worlds." Key learning: "Take all extreme user needs into consideration, even if they don't seem relevant at first glance… Test the requirements later with all types of users and not only with the extreme and lead users."

**Emotional response cards, "Future of Work" (p. 89).** Design teams had designed floor layouts for agile collaboration. After a walk-through in a 3-D model, the team used the cards to test the relationship to the space concept regardless of room size. **The test person selected the adjective "difficult"** and said: *"If I have to imagine working in this room…ouch…. They are not directly conducive to working together effectively, and it is difficult, for example, to hold a stand-up meeting in the narrow corridors."* Key learnings: "People remember emotions! People find it easier to remember if they are given a trigger… Users and customers normally want to be polite. The cards help to lower inhibitions."

## Key Takeaways

- Produce **at least ten** problem definitions before consolidating one.
- Two people per interview is the standing default: one talks, one records emotion and body language.
- Ask why until it gets uncomfortable; verify the root with an if-then reverse question.
- JTBD's payload is the **social, emotional, and personal** jobs, not the functional one — and the workarounds users already built.
- Extreme users are cheap proxies for latent mass-market needs; lead users are expensive and better for co-creation.
- Emotion is easier to elicit with a physical trigger (cards) than with a direct question.
