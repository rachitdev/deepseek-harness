# Chapter 4 — Observe

*(Book pages 91–122; 7 tools)*

## Core Idea

"After we have defined a problem, the next phase in the design thinking process is observation. In this phase, we want to learn as much as possible about a user and his/her needs. This is why we use customer experience chains, personas, and explorative interviews, and simple tools such as the AEIOU framework. These tools help us to collect and document insights."

The rule that motivates the phase, from Chapter 1: *"Only reality can show whether our assumptions, for example, presented in a persona, will be confirmed. This is why we have to go to the place where our potential users are located."*

---

## 1. Empathy map (p. 93)

***I would like…*** *to understand the customer/user better, perceive his/her feelings, and empathize with his/her actions.*

- **What the book states.** An empathy map is a tool for **empathetic target group analysis** used to identify feelings, thoughts, and attitudes of existing or potential users and understand their needs. The aim is in-depth insight **by means of W+H questions**. Compared with a customer journey map or a persona, *"the empathy map focuses more on the emotional state of potential customers."* Used mainly in the **understand, observe, define point of view, and test** phases. The book also recommends speaking to experts who know the user well and doing what the user does — *"Walk in the shoes of the user!"*
- **What it produces.** Documentation of insights from observation or testing; understanding of where the user has **pains** and potential **gains**; inference of the user's **jobs to be done**; collected findings that feed a persona; concise summaries of observations and unexpected insights.
- **Alternatives**: interview for empathy (57), feedback capture grid in the test phase (217), value proposition canvas. **Supports**: customer journey (103), persona/user profile (97), jobs to be done (75).
- **⚠ Extraction gap.** The empathy map's own **template graphic, quadrant labels, timing box (group size / duration / materials) and expert page — pages 94–95 — did not survive PDF text extraction.** Nothing beyond the prose above is recoverable from this edition's text layer. Do not invent quadrant names for it; the book's own quadrants are not stated in text anywhere in the volume.
- **Key learnings the book does print (p. 96):** *"Empathy is the ability to understand other people, their lives, their situation, their work, and solve problems from their perspective. Building empathy means meeting the user."* · ***"Use verbs, not nouns. Needs are verbs (activities with which the user needs help). Nouns are usually solutions."*** · "Review and question assumptions and derive insights from it."

## 2. Persona / user profile (p. 97)

*(including variations: freestyle persona, p. 99; future user, p. 100)*

***I would like…*** *to learn more about the user/customer and a possible solution.*

- **Definition.** A fictitious character created to represent a user or customer type, which puts a potential new solution (website, brand, product, service) into the context of the respective needs and jobs to be done. It should have **a name, gender, and basic demographic data (age, profession, hobbies)**, plus personality and characteristics, from which goals, needs, and fears are inferred; a biography may support conclusions about purchasing behavior from the social milieu. *"The team should be able to meet the persona like a real person."*
- **Group size** the entire design team, 2–5 · **Duration** 20–40 min for a plain customer profile; more for photo-based detail; **a separate profile or persona per problem statement**; two or more people can constitute one target group · **Materials** template or flip chart with the structure drawn in, pens, Post-its, photos from observation of potential customers in their natural environment.
- **Template — the eight-step persona/user profile canvas:**
  1. **Describe the persona** — name, gender, age; add social milieu, family, hobbies.
  2. **Task (job)** — what is the task the user does? Where can he be helped?
  3. **Use cases in the context of the problem statement** — where? what? how? Where does the user make use of our proposal? What happens before and after?
  4. **Difficulties and problems** — unsolved problems, or difficulties with existing products and offers.
  5. **Gains (possibilities, benefits) and pains (problems, challenges).**
  6. **Sketch** visualizing the customer (optional) — or supplement with photos or magazine clippings, like a designer's mood board.
  7. **Influencers** — who influences the persona (family, children, stakeholders…)?
  8. **Trends** — which general trends (megatrends, market trends, technology trends) influence the persona?
- **Variation — freestyle persona (p. 99).** **Group size** 2–5 · **Duration** 20–60 min (plus time to gather information and verify) · **Materials** a **2×1 metre sheet of paper**, pens, Post-its, markers, magazines, newspapers, adhesive tape, glue, internet pictures, photos. Emerges ad hoc from memories of a user you encountered. Step 1 — create a **life-size model**: spread the sheet on the floor, one person lies down in the desired position, another draws the outline. Step 2 — depict the user in the context of the problem or of his actions; complete the drawing with typical elements.
- **Variation — future user persona (p. 100).** **Group size** 2–5 · **Duration** 45–60 min (+15–30 min to collect information, plus interpretation time) · Based on the *Playbook for Strategic Foresight and Innovation* by Tamara Carleton and William Cockayne, 2013 (`www.innovation.io/playbook`). Makes sense for innovation projects with a **>5-year** time line. Steps: (1) describe the target customer; (2) discuss what lifestyle and values this customer had **12 years ago** and the decisions made then — how has it changed?; (3) from this generational research, describe the future user in the here and now; (4) compare the two people **at age 22** and understand what changed and what stayed the same by age 34; (5) extrapolate the future target customer, **the same age as the current target customer**; (6) ask what insights emerge and what will be more important in the future.
  *Printed example*: **Lucas, 34** — life changes: married at 28, MBA degree, father of two daughters, two years abroad in London. **Similarities at age 22** between the two personas: both want an MBA degree, both use public transport, both single.
- **Expert (Agile Digitization Consultant, Mimacom AG)**: use **persona twins** for interacting systems (a married couple, or "Robona" in human/robot interaction); **avoid general and gender-neutral personas relevant across all age categories**; if you feel uncertain about a persona, go look for a real person matching it — *"if we don't find anybody, our assumptions were wrong"*; enrich the persona with meaningful customer quotes; **create only as many personas as the team can remember, including their characteristics.**

## 3. Customer journey map (p. 103)

***I would like…*** *to know in detail what [customers] experience when they interact with our company, our products, or services.*

- Unlike a process map, which maps internal company processes, the customer journey map is **geared to the human being and his/her needs**, and covers actions not directly associated with the product or service: *informing, waiting, ordering, delivery, installing, customer service, disposing of*. Used in the **understand, observe, and prototype** phases; also the base for a service blueprint.
- **Group size** mixed teams of experts and people with little experience in the process, ideally **4–6 per group** · **Duration** 120–240 min — *"an initial draft can emerge after 120 minutes"*; variations are often needed for specific customer groups and events · **Materials** Post-its, pens, markers, large whiteboard, wall space for pictures of customers, locations, activities.
- **Template — the eight-step journey canvas:**
  1. **Choose a persona** and share its story with the design team.
  2. **Choose a scenario or job to be done** — what does the persona do, and what is the context? End-to-end or a part.
  3. **Define BEFORE, DURING, and AFTER** the actual experience; mark all experience steps on Post-its. *"It is easier to compile an overview on the meta-level before expanding and elaborating."*
  4. **Assign interactions** — the template gives space for the typical journey and the respective actions.
  5. **What the persona thinks.**
  6. **The emotion he/she feels** — capture positive/negative emotional status of each step with **colored glue dots or emoticons**.
  7. **Potential areas of improvement.**
  8. **People responsible** for the action/process within the organization.
- **Expert (a South African customer experience designer)**: *"Customer journey maps trigger 'EUREKA' moments for innovation teams because the process is looked at from a different perspective."* Think like an investigative journalist. **Context matters**: "The purchase of a house is an entirely different journey for newlyweds who buy their first home than the purchase of a holiday home is for a millionaire." Useful journeys: all actions a customer undertakes; the context of the activity; thinking about a decision; the feeling after the purchase; possibilities for improving the interaction. **A mapped journey can itself be a first prototype** — test the initial contact of a customer interaction. Improvement levers named: removing emotions or offering solutions across the physical and digital world, intensifying positive experiences, removing negative ones, or **changing the sequence**.

## 4. AEIOU (p. 107)

***I would like…*** *to learn more about the problem, the user/customer, and his environment.*

- **Origin**: developed by **Rick Robinson, Ilya Prokopoff, John Cain, and Julie Pokorny in 1991**. In design thinking it is used in field observation and as a visualization technique for new insights. Mainly **understand and observe**; can also inspire new ideas.
- **Group size** 1–2 per observation (depending on the situation, everyone observes and documents, or one interacts while the other documents) · **Duration** **60 min – 24 hrs** ("An observation can take from 1 hour to one whole day") · **Materials** the AEIOU questionnaire printed A4, glued to firm cardboard or on a clipboard so it's easy to write on; pens.
- **Template — the five categories with their printed questions:**

| Letter | Category | Printed questions |
|---|---|---|
| **A** | **Activities** — *What happens?* | What are the persons doing? What is their task? What activities do they carry out? What happens before and after? |
| **E** | **Environment** | What does the environment look like? What is the nature and function of the space? |
| **I** | **Interaction** | How do the individual systems interact with one another? Are there any interfaces? How do the users interact among one another? How does the operation work? |
| **O** | **Objects** | What objects and devices are used? Who uses the objects and in which environment? |
| **U** | **Users** | Who are the users? What role do the users play? Who influences them? |

- **Procedure.** Step 1 — research where the user can be found, at what times, how to contact him. Step 2 — be where the user/customer currently is in the context of the problem statement. Step 3 — work with the template; hand each team member a questionnaire; use a smartphone for photos and video. Then: collect notes, photos, videos, interviews, field observations; **lend structure to the records after the observation**, operating within the structure of the headings; supplement with photos/short videos; finally **cluster and sort the findings into thematic blocks with summarizing headings so you can identify a pattern.**
- **Alternatives printed** (other observation frameworks): **Spradley's 9 dimensions** — space, actors, activities, objectives, acts, events, time, goals, feelings; **A(×4)** — atmosphere, actors, activities, artifacts; **Sotirin** — territory, people, stuff, talk; **POSTA** — person, objects, situation, time, activity.
- **Expert (a Design Thinking Coach at relevate)**: *"AEIOU is not a rigid framework — it simply provides categories that have proved to be useful."* Adapt the questions to your specific needs; work with **sub-categories** when events happen in chronological sequence; the categories are strongly connected, so establish a mental link between them. AEIOU is also used inside "Design your future" (p. 289) as a reflection on records from an **energy journal**.
- **Why it is recommended for beginners**: "It is particularly suitable for inexperienced design teams because it provides structure and a framework, and the results can be quickly consolidated later."

## 5. Analysis questions builder (p. 111)

***I would like…*** *to make it possible to gain insights from big data analysis that are useful in various phases of the design thinking process.*

- **Rationale.** *"Data yields many answers, provided the right questions are asked."* A structured procedure creates freedom for the creativity needed to identify relevant factors of influence, "because diversion by creativity killers is avoided." Mainly **understand and observe**.
- **Group size** interdisciplinary team of **2–5**; **prior knowledge of data analytics is not necessary** · **Duration** 30–60 min per question · **Materials** multiple large flip chart sheets and a whiteboard, pens.
- **Template — a tree/ramification structure. Procedure:**
  1. **Define the center** of the builder — a new product, offer, or process. The needs the product must meet (what it must be able to do) are defined as relevant factors of influence; e.g. *life cycle assessment (power consumption, resources for production)*. Ramify repeatedly until everything relevant is listed.
  2. **Define the most relevant factors of influence** — either relevant data is already on hand, or ask a focus group, or check whether the team already holds the knowledge.
  3. **Determine the questions** using W+H questions for the **three to five most relevant factors of influence.** Printed example — *the aroma of toast*: "Who likes toasted bread? How dark is the bread allowed to be so it doesn't taste burned? What constitutes the flavor of toast? When does it have to taste [good]?"
- **Expert (Esther Cahn, Signifikant Solutions AG)**: "aspects and factors of influence cropped up that nobody had on their radar. Once the questions are defined, the subsequent analysis process is considerably more efficient." Especially valuable on interdisciplinary teams so no factors are forgotten. Don't be put off by data that seems hard to obtain. This is the book's **hybrid model** — combining design thinking with data analytics, using data insights and human insights as the situation requires throughout the cycle.
- **Alternatives**: extreme users/lead users (79), context mapping (133).

## 6. Peers observing peers (p. 115)

***I would like…*** *to understand at eye level what is really going on.*

- **When it wins.** Especially helpful where **interviews are not possible**, or where no direct interviews are to be conducted in an initial phase. Also yields far more profound insights in the **test** phase, e.g. of prototypes or MVPs. Mainly **understand and observe**.
- **Group size** 3–7 overall, but **no more than 3 people should carry out a single observation**; optimally one peer observes another · **Duration** 60–240 min (plus time for documentation and exchange with other observers) · **Materials** notepad, pen, cell phone or video camera if the observed people agree, sketches or photos.
- **The discipline that defines the tool:** ***"Always make a distinction between observation and interpretation. Peers observing peers is about what we see, not about what we think. This is why we use the template and only describe what we really see."***
- **Template fields:** *What do you see?* · *Where did the observation take place?* · *Scope of observation in hours* · *What was observed* · **two parallel columns: Observations | Comments & interpretation** · *What have we learned?*
- **Procedure.** Step 1 — describe what is seen; if the context is already known and you want frequency, define additional categories to count how often something happens. Step 2 — write down **all** of the steps the peer makes; ensure subjects behave as normally as possible. Step 3 — discuss with other observers how they perceived the process. Typical questions: *"What came as a surprise to you? What have you learned?"* Step 4 — interpret the behavior. Step 5 — infer relevant conclusions.
- **Expert (Ina Goller, Bern University of Applied Sciences / Skillsgarden)**: define a clear focus (What is to be observed? Who? What is to be learned?); **training is essential** — observation without interpretation can be learned, as can how to handle observers' findings; watch for **shortcuts and workarounds** where the user uses homemade aids or uses things for purposes they were not made for; provide quantifiable information; and **if possible, do it yourself** — "Slip into the shoes of other people and imitate their actions. Say out loud what is being done, while the other people on the team take notes."

## 7. Trend analysis (p. 119)

***I would like…*** *to recognize trends at an early stage and integrate them in the problem definition and find a solution.*

- **Goal**: identify and quantify trends; explore causes and influence on the project; identify opportunities and risks and infer options for action. *"When looking for individual phenomenological characteristics and overlaps of the trends, we proceed in small iterative steps."*
- **Group size** 2–5; more people can be involved concurrently or one after the other depending on subject focus and complexity · **Duration** 120–240 min — *"A first trend analysis with a matching affinity chart can be done in 2 hours"* · **Materials** large piece of paper or cardboard, **colored cords (subway lines)**, pins, small clothespins, pens, Post-its in different colors.
- **Template — trend tube mapping, then an affinity chart:**
  1. **Focus** — a product, service, or development, written on a Post-it.
  2. **Megatrend cords** — cords in different colors represent megatrends such as **urbanization, digitization, sustainability**. Phenomena or manifestations of each megatrend hang from the cords; they are determined beforehand in a workshop or focus group.
  3. **Intersections** — search for connections and overlaps, *"just as they can be seen on a subway line map"*, then locate where the product or service sits — **ideally at the intersection of several megatrends**.
  4. **Affinity chart (trend topics)** — a grouping of matching elements that visualizes typical patterns, used for the structured output. Examine the elements at the crossing points and search for possible characteristics and directions (printed example: *hiking → city hiking*). Complete the picture with small cards matching industry, consumer, marketing, and technology trends. *Note: cards can be used in multiple ways by simply duplicating them.*
- **Expert (Thomas Duschlbauer, KompeTrend)**: the physical trend analysis "is a struggle for insights, and the visualizations help to get a grip on the complexity." Because the tool gives a **purely qualitative perspective**, substantiate and verify assumptions with statistical data. The more megatrends mapped, the more complex the process and the more space needed; to locate an already developed product, a smaller selection of trends and their manifestations suffices (e.g. digitization → IoT, Big Data, AI, VR, AR). **Combinations of trends can be the key for disruptive innovations** (e.g. artificial intelligence + blockchain). Step 4 is especially valuable for specific questions about target group behavior or new technologies. Trend analysis also helps **detect niches or identify opposing trends**.
- **Named free tool**: Google Trends, "an important (and free) online tool, for the detection and visualization of trends," alongside trend reports.

## Anti-patterns

- **Mixing observation with interpretation** in peers observing peers — the tool's whole value depends on separating the two columns.
- **Interpreting too much, too fast** — from Lilly's empathy-map case: *"We need to be careful and not interpret too much. What exactly did he say?"*
- **Writing needs as nouns.** Nouns are usually solutions; needs are verbs.
- **General, gender-neutral personas** relevant across all age categories — they produce stereotypes based on false assumptions.
- **Too many personas** — more than the team can remember with their characteristics.
- **Running analytics tools before defining questions** — Lilly's explicit instruction to her teams.
- **Treating trend analysis as quantitative** — it is qualitative until backed with statistical data.
- **Deriving the future linearly from the past** (see vision cone, Ch. 5, for the same warning).

## Worked Example

**Empathy map, Lilly's team (p. 96).** "After the interview, all participants summarize their key findings and enter them on the empathy map. Since Lilly's team has recorded the entire interview with the extreme user on video, key passages can be viewed again. In the end, the **pains and gains** as well as the **customer tasks (jobs to be done)** are determined. Then the team transfers these findings to the user profile, later to a persona that conforms to these findings." The team starts with the extreme user because "his use case is exemplary for the younger generation."

**Persona, Lilly's team (p. 101).** The team works with a **user profile canvas for several types of customer**, describing background, information, behavior, pains, gains, and enriching each persona with quotes. They end with **four user profiles** and decide to add a **future persona**, because "the human/robot relationship is becoming more and more important in the future, and Lilly's team would like to see how people integrate that fact into their teams."

**Customer journey map, Lilly's team (p. 106).** "Lilly's team divides the customer journey map into main phases and looks at each phase separately… On the basis of the customer journey map and the **emotion curve**, the team determines the major problem areas… New insights emerge from the discussion, for example, **that the scaling via franchising has not been optimally set up**."

**Analysis questions builder, Lilly's team (p. 113).** For a **virtual assistant**, one of Lilly's design teams "considered what features make a good assistant, which attributes are really important, and finally how these questions can be answered with data analytics." Key learnings: "Starting with specific questions in a data analysis process makes it far more efficient. **Data and tools are usually not the real problem.** Be creative, even when it comes to big data analytics."

**Peers observing peers, Lilly's team (p. 117).** "In many observations that they had made themselves, they came away with the impression that **the people did not behave authentically**. With peers observing peers, they want to choose a different approach. They instruct employees in which way they should observe their environment and their colleagues. The team gets a completely new perspective!"

## Key Takeaways

- Observation is where persona assumptions are killed or confirmed; go to the user's location.
- Every observation instrument in the phase forces structure before interpretation: AEIOU's five categories, peers observing peers' two-column split, the journey's before/during/after.
- Personas are living artifacts — develop them across the entire design cycle, adding insights from every interaction and test.
- The customer journey's emotion curve is what surfaces the "moments of misery."
- Data analytics belongs in the observe phase, but only after the questions are built.
