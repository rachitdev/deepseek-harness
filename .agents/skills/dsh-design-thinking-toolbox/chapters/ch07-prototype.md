# Chapter 7 — Prototype

*(Book pages 185–210; 7 prototype kinds + 4 tools)*

## Core Idea

"Building prototypes makes the selected ideas tangible and perceptible. Prototypes range from a simple critical function prototype to the final prototype. To build a prototype, we use simple materials that are good enough to test a function or an experience. The 'prototype' phase is closely connected to the following 'test' phase. The feedback from the tests is used to learn more about the user and to improve or discard the current prototype. This procedure is reflected in the design thinking motto: **love it! Change it! Or leave it!** Early failure gives us the opportunity of learning and building a better prototype in the next iteration."

Two rules govern the whole phase. **Resolution rises over time** — "As a rule, prototypes get a higher resolution and become more specific over time; initial ideas are usually still just simple sketches." And **resolution is inversely proportional to honesty** — "The higher the resolution of a prototype, the less feedback we receive regarding desirability and functionality. The lower the resolution, the more honest the answer."

## Frameworks Introduced

### The seven-prototype ladder (pp. 187–194)

The book plots the seven kinds on a single arc from *first ideas & prototypes* to *final prototype*, with the **groan zone** — the divergent→convergent transition — sitting in the middle and the **vision prototype** placed as the bridge across it. Terminology is taken from *The Design Thinking Playbook*, "being fully aware that there are different terms for the variants." Which prototype you use, how many you build, and how many micro-cycles you run before a final prototype "depends on the context of the problem statement."

| # | Prototype | Space | Question it answers |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Critical experience (CEP) / critical function (CFP) | Problem | Is this single experience / single function even real? |
| 2 | Dark horse | Problem | What if the unthinkable were true? |
| 3 | Funky | Problem | Do the findings combine into something? |
| 4 | Vision | Groan zone | What is the whole solution, in one sentence? |
| 5 | Functional (system) | Solution | Can the main function actually be built? |
| 6 | "X is finished" | Solution | Do the essential sub-functions work in detail? |
| 7 | Final | Solution | Problem/solution fit — is it elegant, minimal, and still on-need? |

Two structural notes the book adds: **"Several prototypes of the same type are to be made in each case. People can switch from one prototype to any other prototype"** — the ladder is not strictly sequential. And the **exploration map (p. 195)** exists precisely to keep orientation about which prototypes have already been made.

Level of detail runs from **low resolution (low fidelity, lo-fi)** to **high resolution (high fidelity, hi-fi)**.

## 1. Critical experience prototype (CEP) & critical function prototype (CFP) (p. 188)

***I would like…*** *to learn more about the user and his problem through experiments.*

- **When.** "Carried out at an early stage in a project, when the first steps of the 'understand' and 'observe' phases have been completed, or when initial contacts have been made in the form of interviews and the design team wants to learn even more about the user." They can be performed **several times over the design cycle**, "especially if the whole problem is not yet understood," and they make sense "when critical elements for experience or function are still unclear or need to be questioned."
- **What they are.** *"CEPs/CFPs are little prototypes that allow us to deal with the user on a more in-depth level."* Direct exchange with the user about individual decisive elements reveals deeper needs and **"helps, among other things, to prevent misinterpretations of interviews."**
- **What you can do with it.** Investigate and clarify elements critical to the project in little experiments; create an experience critical for the overall design; simulate a function critical for the overall design; arrive at a more profound understanding of needs; **"provoke emotions in the user that are not accessible with a simple questionnaire."**
- **Expert tips.** ***"It's not about solving the problem completely. Instead, the aim is to question elements of a possible solution."*** The experiments should be created in a very short period of time. **The critical items diagram (p. 145) is the input** — its four critical experiences and four critical functions are exactly what CEP/CFP test. Build with the team and integrate ideas that emerge during realization — *"building can also be understood as 'thinking with one's hands.'"* ***"Many little experiments often yield more findings than one big experiment."***

## 2. Dark horse prototype (p. 189)

***I would like…*** *to learn even more about the user and his problem through peculiar experiments.*

- **The name.** *"'Dark horse' is a term used in athletic competitions or in politics. It describes the unexpected winner, who was not given a chance to win at the beginning, or the participant who is completely unknown."*
- **When.** Early phases. "With a dark horse prototype, you can test the reaction of the user to unusual approaches to a solution." It tests ideas that "entail a high risk and have not been in use for the proposed application or have not (yet) been technically feasible until now."
- **How to provoke it.** Take a peek into the future — *"What will the solution look like in 30 years?"* — or turn previous assumptions around. **"The central questions often start with 'What if…?'"**
- **Implementation — the Wizard of Oz experiment.** "It is usually impossible in this phase to create a functioning prototype. This is why a **Wizard of Oz experiment** or a video of it is a good form of implementation. In a Wizard of Oz experiment (**also referred to as mechanical Turk**), the test person pretends to be communicating with a technical system. In reality, of course, another person is hidden and creates the responses from the system. For example: A person keeps refilling the water for the water infusion."
- **Expert tips.** Carry out this risky experiment early, "since then the failure of an experiment is allowed, **if not encouraged**." *"If there's a risk that the design team gets stuck mentally, the building of a dark horse prototype also helps to generate radical ideas."* Goals: try out unorthodox ways, consciously get off the well-beaten path, **"cast light into the most distant dark corners of the problem space,"** dare to move out of your comfort zone as far as possible.

## 3. Funky prototype (p. 190)

***I would like…*** *to combine the findings of initial experiments in order to complete the exploration of the problem space.*

- **What it merges.** "The findings and ideas from various previous brainstorming sessions and prototypes (e.g. CEP, CFP, or dark horse) are merged."
- **Its job.** Remove "any remaining uncertainties about the elements that are critical for the solution." ***"The main objective is still the collection of findings in the problem space. Finding a solution to the problem is still of secondary importance."*** First final functionalities are implemented "in the most simple way," from simple materials or on top of existing prototypes/solutions.
- **Expert tips.** Keep it short and simple — "they continue to be simple prototypes." **Use a morphological box** to create an overview of the results of all experiments plus the findings from *understand* and *observe*: "This allows for better combinations to be found faster." Then re-check against the **critical items diagram** to see whether open questions about the critical elements remain.

## 4. Vision prototype (p. 191)

***I would like…*** *to create a vision of the later solution based on all previous findings and test it.*

- **What it is.** *"The vision prototype is the first concept that attempts to solve ALL identified needs and problems of the user. The sketched vision usually has a rather distant time horizon that can be reached through a series of solutions in the form of products and/or services."*
- **Its structural role.** ***"The vision prototype is designed to help the team overcome the 'groan zone' — the transition of the divergent phase of problem exploration to the convergent phase of problem solution."***
- **The vision statement test.** *"A vision statement should describe in a single sentence the status aimed for. If this statement gets the attention and kindles the interest of the user, the path taken is promising."*
- **Expert tips.** It is critical to identify the needs to be satisfied, the target group, and the benefit provided. **Extreme/lead users (p. 79) "are excellent references when testing a vision prototype."** *"A vision prototype has likewise a very low resolution. It can show the eventual solution scenario in a sketch or in a video."* Do several iterations to refine the vision. Must be tested and verified with users — "in this step, it is quite common that new insights about the user and his or her behavior emerge."

## 5. Functional (system) prototype (p. 192)

***I would like…*** *to develop a first working prototype.*

- **What it is.** "One part of the previously emerged vision is implemented. The focus is on the part of the vision that can be achieved **early on and easily**. With a system of multiple functionalities to be implemented, **it is common first to realize the main function** of the later product."
- **Purpose.** "Making the main function of the later product tangible and perceptible. This main function should be implemented technically with simple means. **The main function should be checked for its technical feasibility.**" With reduced functionalities, "a minimum viable product (MVP) can already be achieved at this point."
- **Expert tips.** ***"The basic functionality to be implemented should be kept as simple as possible and be reduced to its very core. Other functionalities will be automatically added later."*** Warning about the transition: "After the phase of problem exploration with low-resolution prototypes and very fast iterations, the step toward technical implementation is often a barrier, since failure is likely. **This is why this step needs some courage.**" And: ***"There is no failure, there's only learning."***

## 6. "X is finished" (p. 193)

***I would like…*** *to implement the important elements of the desired solution and go into details.*

- **What it is.** "The phase in which the elements or sub-functions required for the overall functionality are implemented and realized. The goal is to have a **functional overall system** on hand after the conclusion of the phase whose main functions have been specified to the greatest possible degree."
- **Contrast with the two previous prototypes.** "While both the vision prototype and the functional (system) prototype focus on the solution of the **overall problem**, more emphasis is put on the **detailed solution for the most important elements** in the 'X is finished' phase." For systems with multiple sub-functions this is "a crucial step toward a final prototype."
- **Expert tips.** ***"The technical requirements and possible development costs are estimated for the first time within this phase."*** It may help to **integrate third-party know-how of partners or potential suppliers**. The sub-functions must be checked and tested for **user suitability** as well; "the 'X is finished' solution usually also requires several iterations until the sub-system works really well."

## 7. Final prototype (p. 194)

***I would like…*** *to come to an end and put the finishing touches on the solution.*

- **What it is.** "The final prototype concludes the phase of problem solving." ***"The final prototype shows the 'problem/solution fit.'"*** At the latest in this step, the **MVP (p. 207)** should be achieved.
- **The risk it manages.** *"When looking so closely at individual elements, you easily lose sight of the big picture. When the individual sub-functions are developed, there is a risk that they will diverge, become too large, and that the elegance and simplicity of the overall solution will be lost."* So the step also re-checks "whether the proposed solution still matches the needs originally determined and the problem of the target group."
- **Expert tips.** ***"The reduction to the essence and the smart combination of individual elements and functionalities ensures elegance."*** Take another look at the defined CEP/CFP elements (p. 188) and examine whether the ultimate solution matches them **"and which parts might be dispensed with."** "Make sure that only those functionalities that are really necessary are implemented and **no overloaded solutions emerge**." Explicit goals: exit the prototype phase and thus the early innovation phase, **avoid overfulfillment of needs**, make the first marketable step toward implementation, and *"try to convince the decision-makers of your final prototype, because this is the only way they can actually understand what the solution will look like and which needs are met with it."*

## 8. Exploration map (p. 195)

***I would like…*** *to know what experiments I have done so far and how I can classify them.*

- **What it is.** *"The exploration map helps to keep track of all the experiments and prototypes already carried out. It normally has an **experience** and a **function** axis. The two axes symbolize **known or existing** as well as **new or unexpected** behaviors and functions."* User/customer feedback on each experiment can be entered too, "so it can be determined whether the expected user behavior conforms to real-life experience." At the end of the cycle it shows **the path the team took** to reach the ultimate solution.
- **Group size** design thinking core team; **ideal are groups of 4–6** · **Duration** **10–45 min** — depends on the number of prototypes to be entered and the intensity of the resultant discussion · **Materials** a large sheet of paper, Post-its, pens, markers, "a lot of creativity and fun to realize something tangible."
- **Template — the two-axis map (known/existing ↔ new/unexpected on both experience and function), with a comfort zone at the centre and a risk/"dark" zone at the edges. Procedure:**
  1. **Step 1** — Enter the experiments already carried out; they might have to be repositioned. Record each experiment **with a name and an image** (e.g. of the prototype and the testing).
  2. **Step 2** — Discuss the positioning on the team. ***"Have we really left our comfort zone?"*** Based on previous exploration, define the goal for a new experiment.
  3. **Step 3** — After a prototype has been built and the **expectation regarding the result** formulated, enter and position those on the map too.
  4. **Step 4** — After the tests, capture the reaction of the users and the findings. "The critical discussion of the feedback may change the position of the experiment on the exploration map."
- **Alternative.** Experiments can be documented conventionally without a map — "in this case, it won't become clear, however, whether really risky or unusual experiments were carried out, how creative the teams were, and how far they dared to go."
- **Expert — this is Larry Leifer's favorite tool** (Professor of Mechanical Engineering, Stanford; Founding Director HPI & Stanford Center for Design Research; ME310). *"Keep up the design thinking mindset and start hunting for the next big opportunity."* Why: "it is often difficult for teams to check whether they have really explored all aspects of the problem space." Tips:
  - Do it with **the entire core team** so a common understanding of the course of experiments emerges. Supplement it **after each experimenting phase**; tandem groups can read off each other's results.
  - When repositioning an experiment, **also record the original expectation. "The difference stands for the misinterpretation of the needs. The following applies: The greater the difference, the greater the gain in knowledge."**
  - **Keep two parallel maps** if the number of experiments is large — one for expectations, one for the reactions from the tests.
  - Display as a **huge poster**; use visualizations or photos of the realized prototypes.
  - ***"The exploration map is not a precise measuring device. It provides rough orientation. The time for the positioning of the individual experiments should therefore be kept very short."***

## 9. Prototype to test (p. 199)

***I would like…*** *to assess whether the needs of the user were met with the implemented ideas.*

- **Positioning.** *"With its focus on direct user interaction and feedback, the prototype to be tested is a fundamental concept of design thinking."* "Choose the kind that best matches the phase in the macro-cycle, the idea, and the hypotheses to be reviewed." **The potential user should interact with the prototype and experience it.**
- **Group size** **1–many** — "a prototype can be created by one person or by a larger team. Experts and additional team members help to check that everything has been taken into account" · **Duration** **30 min – many days** — "Low-resolution prototypes can be built within 30 minutes. A final prototype may require days or even weeks." · **Materials** design thinking material, paper, Post-its, pens, "whatever material it takes to create the prototype."
- **Template and procedure — four steps:**
  1. **Step 1** — Before prototyping, ask **what kinds of insights we want to gain and why we want to make an experiment**. Formulate the **assumptions to be tested** and how the experiment is to be carried out.
  2. **Step 2** — Think about how interacting with the prototype **will become an exciting experience** for the test person and how the test will result in new insights.
  3. **Step 3** — Determine the **level of resolution** and what exactly is to be done. Define different prototypes to be built. **"Often it makes sense to think in alternatives and then opt for one."**
  4. **Step 4** — Choose a variant and outline the experiment. ***Low-resolution prototypes focus on insights about needs, practicality and functionality and are mostly used in the divergent phase. High-resolution prototypes concentrate on feasibility and profitability.***
- **Forms named:** mock-ups, storyboards, scenography, Wizard of Oz prototypes. (A fuller list with resolutions is in *The Design Thinking Playbook*, pp. 111–112.)
- **Alternative.** Interviews or online surveys, "but be careful: These methods do not guarantee the same findings **since direct user interaction is lacking**."
- **Expert (Patrick Deininger, Senior Consultant, delta Karlsruhe GmbH / KIT)** — the lo-fi/hi-fi rules, verbatim:
  - **"The higher the resolution of a prototype, the less feedback we receive regarding desirability and functionality. The lower the resolution, the more honest the answer. So build prototypes from simple materials."**
  - *"Unfortunately, we all tend to fall in love with a prototype we created. When that happens, we will hold on to an idea, even though there might be better ones. This is why we keep searching, **even if we already received perfect feedback**. We dig deeper and find out whether the feedback was really honest."*
  - "We **never invest too much time** in prototyping since that automatically increases the resolution or overloads the prototype with functions."
  - **"We try to implement no more than one or two functions in each low-resolution prototype."**
  - ***"The right time to test a low-resolution prototype is when we are still a little embarrassed to show this particular prototype."***
  - Although we should not explain a prototype, it is often necessary to convey the **context**; **"separate the context explanation from the test itself in terms of time"** to keep the *show, don't tell* approach intact.

## 10. Service blueprint (p. 203)

***I would like…*** *to acquire a common understanding of the interaction and the processes that have an impact on customer satisfaction, target achievement, and efficiency.*

- **What it is.** "A comprehensive tool that helps to define or improve the interaction with a customer and the interfaces at a company for a product or service." It is **"the advanced visualization of a customer journey map"** — but because it also takes in back-office processes, supporting processes, new regulations and technologies, "it is possible to **activate and motivate the entire organization**." It can carry IT architectures, data layers, digital customer channels, and AI-driven digital actions (e.g. chat bots), and it is where **KPIs in terms of quality and time of the interactions** get defined.
- **Group size** **3–6** — *"In the best case, take the relevant experts and process owners on board."* · **Duration** **120–240 min** (about **4 hours** as a workshop format); beforehand it must be determined **which specific service creation process is to be optimized or designed and where the process limits are** · **Materials** large wall or whiteboard, paper, pens, Post-its.
- **Template — five horizontal bands, split by the visibility line:**

| Band | Content |
|---|---|
| 1) **Actions** | the customer's chronological steps |
| 2) **Touch points** | where the customer meets the organization |
| 3) **Actors** | who is involved (compare against the stakeholder map) |
| 4) **Visible activities** | front stage — above the **visibility line** |
| 5) **Invisible activities** | back stage and supporting processes — below the line |

- **Procedure.**
  1. **Step 1** — Large wall, long sheet of paper. Draw the lines (e.g. the visibility line) and fill in steps and processes with Post-its. **Start with the large blocks: actions and touch points.**
  2. **Step 2** — Include the actual state of existing services; create a rough process model for new processes. **Problems and errors are identified with color dots or Post-its.**
  3. **Step 3** — Search for solutions as a team to eliminate sources of error, streamline processes, actively shape customer experiences. Use videos, images, sketches, Post-its.
  4. **Step 4** — Distribute the open items to group work / working in private. **"By working in tandem with the time boxing method, results are achieved faster."**
  5. **Step 5** — Integrate the partial results back onto the blueprint. Once sufficiently refined, **test, improve and finally implement** both the individual elements and the end-to-end perspective.
- **Expert (Beat Knüsel, CEO Trihow AG, Switzerland)**: *"Design thinking can crack the three biggest problems in organizations: silo mentality, hierarchical hurdles, and lack of customer orientation."*
  - **Extensibility** — extend with attributes such as KPIs regarding **time, quality, and finance**; document points of view with **photos and videos**.
  - **"Service staging"** — *"A service can be made tangible and perceptible as 'theater' … to test a service blueprint prototype."*
  - **Typical actions** to look for: *attracting attention, informing, deciding, buying, planning, installing, using, maintaining, disposing.*
  - **Trigger questions**: "How do we imagine an ideal process? What are the process steps and interfaces that can be omitted? Where can tasks be simplified or processed in tandem? How and where can the customer's perception be improved?"
  - **Stumbling blocks**: *"Service blueprints need space. It is best to reserve a room for several days and leave the information there."* And **delineate the goal clearly at the start** — "otherwise, there's a risk of getting lost in the processes. Therefore, begin with the big picture."

## 11. MVP — minimum viable product (p. 207)

***I would like…*** *to translate user needs into a simple, functional product and test whether the offer will be successful on the market.*

- **Definition and boundary.** The book prints the distinction as a pair of captions: ***"This IS an MVP: You develop and test iteratively." / "This is NOT an MVP: Finished products or individual parts are not MVPs."*** The aim is "to find out as quickly as possible (and with little effort) in an iterative process whether the solution satisfies the user needs in any meaningful way." The process is "a permanent alternation between the holistic solution of the problem (**functional prototype**) and the solution of individual details (**X is finished**)."
- **Resolution.** "Typically, MVPs are prototypes of an **already higher resolution** and constitute the basis for launching a product or service on the market on a step-by-step basis." The test results decide whether the MVP is **implemented, adapted, or eliminated**.
- **Group size** **ideally 2–4**, usually interdisciplinary teams — *"Not only the team size counts but the skills of the individual employees do as well"* · **Duration** **days to months**, depending on the product or service · **Materials** vary by MVP.
- **Template and procedure — seven steps:**
  1. **Step 1** — Always focus on **one** MVP (not several simultaneously) and describe the initial situation: the **persona**, the **top three problems and challenges**, the **customer journey**, and relevant **use cases**.
  2. **Step 2** — Ensure the team is very clear about the **product vision and functional scope**. Prioritize and focus on the **core functionality**; expand functional width and depth step by step (**T-shaped MVP**).
  3. **Step 3** — Define the **top three features** to be tested in the next iterations.
  4. **Step 4** — Plan the build, watching **costs and schedule**. If the plan optimizes learning, **define the measurement criteria**, then realize the MVP.
  5. **Step 5** — **Test on potential users/customers in a real context** and collect as much feedback as necessary. **"The results should be measurable."**
  6. **Step 6** — Summarize the learnings together and improve step by step. *"If the MVP flops, don't be disappointed. We can learn something from every iteration."*
  7. **Step 7** — Summarize the overarching findings from the iterations. ***"Must this vision or strategy be adapted?"***
- **Expert (Esther Moosauer, Consultant in Technology Advisory, EY Zurich)**: *"I like most the **FAIL FAST, FAIL EARLY, FAIL CHEAP** mindset since this attitude fosters pragmatism and keeps perfectionism in check."* The MVP makes it possible to find out whether we **"are doing the right thing before we do it right."**
  - **The 80/20 rule as the yardstick**: *"80% of the proposition can already be presented to the user with 20% of the development effort."*
  - *"First, a good solution should be found for **standard cases** before turning to complex individual cases."*
  - **Preliminary test**: show the proposition to colleagues who don't know it. ***"If everybody comes up with the same questions, the value proposition is not yet as clear as it should be."***
  - **"T-shaped teams tend to develop a T-shaped MVP."**
  - The printed escalation of "minimums": ***minimum viable → earliest testable → usable → lovable → sellable.***
- **Variation — minimum viable ecosystem (MVE)** (Lewrick et al. 2018). "Digital products and solutions require an appropriate business model and a business ecosystem to create as much value as possible." The MVE "uses the MVP mindset to design a business ecosystem … that ensures clearly defined goals and the efficient cooperation among the actors in the system," used with new technologies such as **DLT (distributed ledger technologies)**, where "a multitude of actors create a value proposition together, for example, as a data community or cooperative."

## Mental Models

- **Resolution is a dial with a cost.** Low resolution buys honest feedback about desirability; high resolution buys evidence about feasibility and profitability. Turning the dial early is the single most common prototyping mistake.
- **Embarrassment as a timing signal.** If you are not slightly ashamed to show the lo-fi prototype, you have already invested too much.
- **Thinking with one's hands.** Building is a mode of ideation, not just a mode of validation — new ideas are supposed to appear during construction.
- **The groan zone needs a bridge, not a decision.** The vision prototype is the artifact that carries a team from divergent exploration into convergent solving.
- **The delta is the learning.** On the exploration map, the distance between where you expected an experiment to land and where it actually landed *is* the measured gain in knowledge.
- **Many small beats one big.** Multiple little CEP/CFP experiments outperform a single large one.
- **Never fall in love.** Even perfect feedback is a reason to dig further, not to stop.

## Anti-patterns

- **Overloading a lo-fi prototype** — more than one or two functions in a low-resolution prototype.
- **Investing too much time in a prototype**, which "automatically increases the resolution."
- **Falling in love with the prototype**, and holding an idea past the point where a better one exists.
- **Explaining the prototype during the test** — the *show, don't tell* rule; separate the context explanation in time.
- **Skipping the functional prototype step because failure is likely** — "this step needs some courage."
- **Losing the big picture in the sub-functions** — the divergence that the final prototype exists to correct.
- **Overfulfillment of needs** — overloaded solutions with functionalities nobody requires.
- **Treating the exploration map as a measuring device** — it is rough orientation; spend little time positioning.
- **Substituting interviews or online surveys for prototype testing** — "direct user interaction is lacking."
- **Calling a finished product or an individual part an MVP.**
- **Running several MVPs at once** instead of one at a time.
- **Starting a service blueprint without a delineated goal** — "there's a risk of getting lost in the processes."

## Worked Example

**The book's running prototype example — the water challenge (pp. 187–194).** One design challenge is carried across all seven prototype kinds so the change in focus is visible:

> **Design challenge: HOW MUCH WATER TO CARRY?** — *"What is the optimum solution for hikers who depend on fluids during a mountain hike and want to transport the daily requirement of water easily and safely?"*

- **CEP/CFP stage** — the illustrated experiments are labelled **critical function F2 "OPEN"** and **critical experience E4 "DESIGN"**: one element of the eventual solution each, tested in isolation.
- **Dark horse stage** — the printed Wizard of Oz example for this challenge: *"A person keeps refilling the water for the water infusion"* — the hidden human simulating a system that does not exist.
- **Vision stage** — the printed vision statement, in one sentence: ***"Water, my constant companion: lightweight, always available, cool."***
- **Functional (system) stage** — the caption reads: *"The prototype for the functions 'always with me' and 'lightweight.'"* Two of the vision's promises, made real; the rest deferred.

**Exploration map, Lilly's team (p. 198).** In the comic: *"Let's plan and position the next iteration."* — *"At the beginning, we kept quite close to the comfort zone. Now we should really enter the risk zone."* — *"That's right. The positions before and after the test are close to each other. The team is able to assess the situation very well."*
The narrative: "With the initial iterations, Lilly's team had the goal of getting a real in-depth understanding of the problem and the customers… Now they want to **pull the user out of the comfort zone** in the tests and try out more radical approaches. With a **dark horse prototype**, they plan to sound out the limits of what's feasible and **dare to enter the 'dark zone'** of the exploration map." Key learnings: the map "helps to keep track of our experiments and see the point we have reached on our journey of exploration toward the next market opportunity" and "visualizes the intention of our experiments and shows how the results change over time."

**Prototype to test, Lilly's team (p. 202).** "First, Lilly makes sure that her team first tests the **right assumptions**; next, she wants them to learn as much as possible as quickly as possible and would rather do several iterations… She motivates the team **to think in variants instead of building away like mad**, and to think critically about the prototype only *after* the testing. Thinking in variants helped her to simplify many prototypes and test iterations, thus speeding up the project." Key learnings, verbatim: *"Show, don't tell! Never fall in love with a prototype! Try to build prototypes with the lowest possible resolution. Test, even though you are embarrassed to show the prototype! **If you aren't ashamed of your prototype, you have invested too much time in it.** It's better to make many experiments with low-resolution prototypes than to test an ultimate solution that nobody needs or wants."*

**Service blueprint, Lilly's team (p. 206).** The team had already analyzed the customer journey in detail on a large wall and understood the main phases, actions and touch points. "Together, they discuss the emotions and, above all, the **pains** along the journey. They ensure that all actors are mentioned and **compare them to the stakeholder map**. In the next step, the team completes the blueprint… All **visible and invisible activities** are defined." Key learnings: "By identifying, eliminating, or improving pain points, the first prototype of a blueprint emerges. A blueprint can help uncover **silos or lack of transparency** and integrate new technologies. **A blueprint is a highly complex thing. It makes sense to divide the overall system into sub-systems and optimize the latter.**"

## Key Takeaways

- Seven prototype kinds, not one: CEP/CFP → dark horse → funky → **vision (groan zone)** → functional → "X is finished" → final. Build several of each type and move freely between them.
- Low resolution first, always; one or two functions per lo-fi prototype; embarrassment is the go signal.
- The critical items diagram feeds CEP/CFP directly, and the final prototype checks back against it.
- The exploration map's value is the recorded **delta** between expectation and result, not the position itself.
- Every prototype to test starts with a written assumption and a stated reason for the experiment.
- Service blueprint = customer journey + actors + visible/invisible activities across the visibility line, ~4 hours, with process owners in the room.
- MVP: one at a time, 80/20 scope, three named features, measurable test results — and it is *not* a finished product or a part.
