# Chapter 6 — Ideate

*(Book pages 149–184; 7 tools)*

## Core Idea

"The classic way of ideating is brainstorming. Brainstorming is applied in the 'ideate' phase in a variety of ways. It is primarily about generating as many ideas (ideation) as possible before they are sorted, combined, or clustered. The selection of preferred ideas usually takes place within the framework of an evaluation and vote on the team. For this, tools such as dot voting and the decision matrix are used. **The selection of ideas is one of the most difficult elements in the design cycle, since the earlier phases are characterized by a high level of uncertainty.**"

The phase therefore has two halves: **generation** (brainstorming, 6-3-5, special brainstorming, analogies) and **selection** (2x2 matrix, dot voting), plus two tools that **package** a chosen idea for feedback (NABC, blue ocean / buyer utility map).

---

## 1. Brainstorming (p. 151)

***I would like…*** *to ideate quickly — quantity is more important than quality.*

- **Positioning.** *"Good brainstorming sessions stimulate creativity and allow all participants, regardless of their hierarchical level, to contribute their ideas."* Before actual ideation it is often used as a **"brain dump"** so everybody can get their existing solutions out and clear their heads; later sessions then focus on the problem statement. *"Brainstorming has no limits — all ideas are welcome!"*
- **Group size** most effective in groups of **4–6**; for large groups or groups with hierarchical differences, make sure everybody contributes · **Duration** **5–15 min** — *"After a certain amount of time, creativity declines and must be rekindled by new incentives (e.g. other methods and questions)."* · **Materials** Post-its, pens, wall or whiteboard.
- **Procedure.**
  1. Prepare a **clear HMW question** for the session ("How might we…" or "What possibilities are there…", p. 125).
  2. **Repeat the brainstorming rules** before starting. Motivate the group to give more ideas and build on others'. Ensure all are heard and all ideas written down. **One idea per Post-it**, clear and legible; small sketches may replace words.
  3. **Cluster and assess** the ideas with the team at regular intervals.
  4. Judge whether more creativity is needed (e.g. wilder ideas), or start a session in areas where more ideas are wanted.
- **Variant — structured brainstorming.** All participants write ideas on Post-its privately. After a period, one person starts sticking his own on a flip chart and explaining them; a similar Post-it gets glued next to it. **During the explanations, new ideas are generated and written on new Post-its.** The result is a clustered collection ready for evaluation.
- **Supporting tool — the parking lot for ideas.** *"A parking lot for ideas serves the purpose of parking ideas that sound exciting but do not contribute at present anything toward solving the problem."*
- **⚠ Extraction gap — the eight brainstorming rules graphic (p. 152)** survived only partially. Recoverable: **#1 Creative confidence · #2 Quantity before quality · "Build on the ideas of others" · "Only one person speaks at a time — always" · #7 No prejudices · #8 Continue to…** (text lost). Rules 3–6 and the completion of #8 are not recoverable from this edition's text layer.
- **Expert (Małgorzata Waszkiewicz, Warsaw University of Technology / Warsaw Design Factory)**: ***"We never criticize an idea, because we might need it again later!"*** Over the cycle "we come back to ideas that seemed not doable before." Use brainstorming not only in *ideate* but at the start of a project, before a sprint, and in redefining the problem. **Photograph the walls with the Post-its — "they easily get lost in the heat of the moment."** Linking ideas often produces very good solutions. Run a session **at the start of the day**, or after a break or warm-up. Skip general introductions announcing who plays which role.

## 2. 2x2 matrix (p. 155)

***I would like…*** *to categorize and prioritize ideas or identify strategic opportunities and patterns.*

- **Positioning.** *"The matrix is highly modifiable, because any type of meaningful axis attribute may be used."* It can also shift thinking "from a 100% idea orientation toward the recognition of unfulfilled user needs and strategic opportunities," and is used in all phases, not just prioritization.
- **Group size** **2–8** — "The smaller the group, the shorter the discussion. This allows for quick evaluation." For groups over 8, dot voting procedures can help · **Duration** **15–45 min** — about **30–60 seconds per idea** including short discussion; open discussion and evaluation usually needs more (e.g. 45 min) · **Materials** whiteboard or large sheet as template, flip chart, Post-its, pens, markers, **Post-its already written from a brainstorming session or the clustering of ideas**.
- **Template — the recovered axis example (p. 157), VALUE × EFFORT:**

| | **much EFFORT** | **little EFFORT** |
|---|---|---|
| **high VALUE** | Develop / park ideas | **Pursue / implement** |
| **low VALUE** | **Reject** | Develop / park ideas |

  The book's other named axis pair, used in very early phases: **"cool" × "doable."**
- **Procedure.**
  1. Draw the template and designate the axes; use **"high"/"low"** or opposite attributes. *Tip: when evaluating ideas, focus more on the benefits for the user and on feasibility, and use measurable and tangible criteria for the opportunity analysis.*
  2. Position by **reading the ideas aloud** on the team: start with a broad classification (which quadrant?), then place each idea **in relation to the other ideas**, seeking consensus. Alternatively evaluate one axis first, then the second. Repeat until all ideas are placed.
  3. Select for further processing: **if several ideas sit top-right, take the top 3** for discussion; **if fewer than 3 sit top-right, check the development fields** for implementable ideas; **also check for empty quadrants — "they signify potential for further opportunities and unfulfilled needs."**
- **Expert (Vesa Lindroos, independent consultant, Finland)**: the matrix is "as versatile as a Swiss Army knife." Use it for **strategic planning** — focus on current uses and opportunities rather than new ideas, to identify uncovered areas. **Make the axes SMART** (specific, measurable, achievable, realistic, timely) — which also makes it valuable in the *prototype* phase. Limiting factors can act at different levels: technology, project scope, time frame, resources. For complex problems, **break the idea down into its components**. The "cool"/"doable" pair provokes the useful question: ***"Is there any way that this cool idea might be realized?"*** In later phases, quantify as a **cost/yield chart** — "investors are happy to use such a chart as a basis for decision making."

## 3. Dot voting (p. 159)

***I would like…*** *to make a clear decision on what options should be pursued in the form of ideas or concepts.*

- **Why it works.** *"This tool makes it possible for decisions to be heard and made on the basis of reflection, the difficulty of the design challenge, and how well it meets objectives (and **NOT on the basis of power, position, or how extroverted somebody is**)."* It bestows personal responsibility and a clear understanding of the decision process. Used throughout the entire cycle.
- **Group size** ideal for teams of **5–10**; larger groups should be split · **Duration** **5–20 min** — "one of the fastest procedures for capturing the mood and coming to a decision" · **Materials** glue dots or monochromatic color markings, a large surface with the ideas on Post-its or in clusters.
- **Procedure.** *Initial situation: ideas already collected on Post-its.*
  1. **Clarify the criteria before the vote.** The book's printed criteria examples: *best suitability for long-term goals · will delight the customer/user · supports the vision · biggest opportunity for competitive advantages · adherence to deadlines · greatest impact on customer satisfaction.*
  2. Put the Post-its where everybody can see them. Give each participant a set number of votes — ***usually between 3 and 5 dots***. **In private**, each casts votes on the Post-its that best meet the criteria. Let participants choose whether to stack several votes on one Post-it or distribute them.
  3. **Rearrange and regroup** the ideas with the most dots, make a transparent decision on those priorities, determine next steps.
- **Eight printed variations (expert: Ingunn Aursnes, Senior Manager Business Design, Sopra Steria, Norway):**
  - **Present before the vote** — a brief explanation ensures deeper understanding first.
  - **Equal number of dots?** — run a second vote on the winners, or use the 2x2 matrix.
  - **Color markings** instead of glue dots — each participant draws small circles with a pen.
  - **Limit the number of choices** — combine similar ideas; bundle topics, vote first on main topics, then on individual ideas.
  - **Avoid the bandwagon effect** — decide in private, vote all at the same time; ***if there is a risk that an individual is too heavily influenced by one person (e.g. the boss), that person should turn in his/her assessment last.***
  - **Parking lot for ideas** — save ideas for future projects.
  - **Crowd voting** — involve customers or colleagues to set priorities.
  - **Color code** — different colors for different evaluating groups (users/customers vs. internal people).
  - **Heat map** — use dots to highlight individual *parts* of an idea or concept.
  - **Detailed evaluation** — rate ideas on a scale from **0 (do not implement) to 10 (definitely implement)**.

## 4. Brainwriting / 6-3-5 method (p. 163)

***I would like…*** *to generate many ideas quickly and in a structured way in the group.*

- **The arithmetic.** *"The name '6-3-5' is derived from an optimal team size of **6 participants** who each produce **3 ideas** for a problem in the first round. Each of these ideas will then be further developed by the other participants in the next **5 rounds**."* **In a group of 6, up to 108 ideas are collected in under 30 minutes.**
- **Why it wins over open brainstorming.** It captures "the creative potential of people who are more reserved in open group situations by allowing them to work quietly in private," and fosters offbeat ideas "through the explicit separation of ideation and the evaluation of ideas."
- **Group size** groups of **6** are ideal; **4–6** with adaptations to the template and sequence; split large groups · **Duration** **30–40 min** — **3 to 5 minutes per round for 6 rounds**, plus time for the subsequent selection · **Materials** sheets of paper (or printed form), pens, Post-its, glue dots if selection is by dot voting.
- **Template — a grid of 3 columns × 6 rows = 18 boxes**, headed by the **Problem** and columned **Idea 1 · Idea 2 · Idea 3**; cells are numbered 1.1–6.3. *Variant: a sheet of **6 columns × 6 rows = 36 boxes**, which "allows for the development of completely new ideas."*
- **Procedure.**
  1. Form groups of 6 and explain the problem.
  2. Hand out the grid (or have participants draw it).
  3. Each participant writes **three ideas in the first row** within 3–5 minutes — ***the whole thing is done without speaking.*** *Variant: write on Post-its instead of directly on the sheet.*
  4. When time is up, **pass the sheet clockwise**.
  5. Give time to read the existing ideas, then complete **the next row** in the same time box. Ideally develop existing ideas further; ideas **may but need not** build on others'.
  6. Repeat until all rows are filled.
  7. **Cluster and evaluate** with the team; agree next steps.
- **Expert (Philipp Guggisberg-Gibel, Business Design Consultant at mm1, Switzerland)**: the HMW question "has a great impact on the ideation" — discuss the problem statement in the group first, **hang it up in the room**, and ask participants to write it on their own sheet. **Show, don't tell** — sketch ideas rather than describing them laboriously; if sketching, use **A3** paper. For selection: evaluate the ideas on every sheet, intuitively or against criteria, and highlight a defined number via dot voting — or **cut the ideas out and stick them on a 2x2 matrix to illustrate the idea space**.

## 5. Special brainstorming (p. 167)

***I would like…*** *to generate a large number of unusual ideas in a limited period of time.*

- **When to reach for it.** *"They are especially useful when a group gets stuck during ideation or when similar ideas are repeatedly generated."* Which type to use "depends heavily on the problem statement, the participants, and the goals."
- **Group size** typically **2–10** — "Smaller groups are particularly suitable if the group members are very diverse or practiced in adopting different perspectives"; larger groups split into sub-groups working in tandem · **Duration** **10–20 min per technique** · **Materials** Post-its, pens, **a selection of pictures of luminaries** (for figuring storming).
- **The three techniques, as printed:**

| Technique | What it does | Book's own example |
|---|---|---|
| **Negative brainstorming** | Combines traditional brainstorming with the **reversal method**: instead of finding a solution, participants focus on **anything that might make the problem worse**. Results are then reviewed for new starting points, or for aspects that usually exacerbate the problem and could be eliminated. | *Instead of finding approaches for improving a traffic situation, the group concentrates on **maximizing the traffic jam** on the respective road.* |
| **Figuring storming** | Brainstorming **from the point of view of a third party**. Ask: ***"How would 'X' solve the problem?"*** | *Albert Einstein or the president of the United States; also people from everyday life (partners, family members, the boss) and **personas defined within the design thinking process**.* |
| **Bodystorming** | Places test persons **physically** in a particular situation; the scenario is imitated as accurately as possible with relevant environment, artifacts, and persons, so subjects infer new ideas by **physical trial and error**. | *A team developing products for elderly people applies **Vaseline on the lenses of glasses** to perceive the world through the eyes of seniors.* One variant: be constantly in motion and **write down ideas while walking**. |

- **Expert (Miriam Hartmann, University of Applied Sciences of the Grisons / F. Hoffmann-La Roche)**: in traditional brainstorming the focus is often so broad that many ideas are no longer associated with the actual problem, and groups hit their limits on abstract or complex issues. **Be in a creative environment** — comfortable chairs and cushions, large flexible writing surfaces. **Time boxing** — short **10-minute** sequences clocked with a timer. ***"There are no 'bad' ideas because any idea, and be it ever so outlandish an idea, may lead to a good solution when further developed."***

## 6. Analogies & benchmarking as inspiration (p. 171)

***I would like…*** *to find inspiration for ideas and approaches by exploring "worlds" that seem to exist separately in the context of the problem statement.*

- **Positioning.** *"Another industry, animals, persons, or organizations can serve as a benchmark or analogy."* Useful for open, poorly structured problems — **ill-defined and wicked problems**. Signature prompt printed with the tool: ***"How would an elephant solve the problem?"***
- **Group size** optimally **3–6**; groups over 8 split · **Duration** **30–120 min** — *"Working with analogies can be time-consuming. Especially the research entailed in it and the identification of characteristics and attributes as well as discussions with experts often require several hours."* · **Materials** whiteboard or flip charts, Post-its, pens, markers, internet and printer.
- **Procedure — the five-step analogy transfer:**
  1. **List the critical experiences or biggest PAINS** of the problem statement. *Book's example, for shoes, the PAIN: "low status."*
  2. Use brainstorming or brainwriting to search for **scenarios, systems, places, or objects that also entail those PAINS but seem to have solved them.** In the printed example the PAIN of *status* was solved in the **automotive industry**, and the PAIN of *uncomfortable* in the case of a **sofa**. Search questions: ***"What do other industries do? How does nature solve the problem? Why is it not a problem in other countries?"***
  3. **Interview experts** who know the comparable area well. Create an **"analogies inspiration board."**
  4. **List the solutions** — e.g. how "status" in the automotive industry was solved by a *"high-quality lifestyle magazine."*
  5. **Transfer the analogy's solution back to the original problem.** "Some solutions from the analogy can be transferred almost 1:1; others require a bit more creativity when applied."
- **Variation — find analogies with sketch notes (p. 173).** **Group size 1–12** or more, "almost scalable at will" · **Duration 30–60 min**, several rounds of about 30 minutes each · **Materials** one flip chart per person; **A4 or A3** for private work, **A2 or A1** for group work; pens and Post-its; optionally internet image search.
  **Template — a large sheet divided into three columns:**

  | Attributes | Analogies | Ideas |
  |---|---|---|
  | Attribute 1 | Analogy 1 | Idea 1 |
  | Attribute 2 | Analogy 2 | Idea 2 |
  | Attribute 3 | Analogy 3 | Idea 3 |
  | Attribute 4 | Analogy 4 | Idea 4 |

  Steps: list the attributes of the problem statement (p. 49) → ask the team to find analogies for each attribute → **solve the problem from the point of view of the analogy in the form of sketch notes** → develop original and viable ideas.
- **Sketch-note vocabulary printed on p. 174** ("visual basic building blocks"): **Forms** — "nearly everything can be represented with dots, lines, and geometrical forms." **Font** — proportions given as 1/5 · 3/5 · 1/5; *"write letters rather close to one another."* **Containers** — different shapes for *thoughts · speech · factual statement · emotional things*. **Faces** — "emotions through the form of the eyes and mouth"; line of vision indicated by the position. **People** — "the simplest form for a person… and a group"; "from a star… to a figure"; diversify by professions. **Highlighting** — ***gray outside = 2-D, i.e. flat; gray inside = 3-D, i.e. volume.*** Motto printed alongside: *"If you say 'I can't draw!', then this is the right thing for you."*
- **Expert (Julia Gumula, Senior Manager Communications, B. Braun Melsungen AG)**: **concentrate on the critical experiences and pains** when searching. ***"Also look at absurd industries and concepts"*** — analogies far from your industry "have often proven quite helpful." **If nothing comes up, just Google** — an image search for "fast" and "comfortable" returns thousands of objects with those characteristics. **Practice daily** to train the power of association. **Copying from others is allowed.** Use it spontaneously mid-brainstorm: *"How is the customer service solved in a 5-star hotel?"*

## 7. NABC — need, approach, benefit, competition (p. 177)

***I would like…*** *to capture the core of an idea within a very short time and share it with others in a targeted way.*

- **Positioning.** *"The NABC is the minimal version of a business idea structuring method."* Applicable in many phases — ideation, validation of prototypes, better understanding of users — and often used for documentation and for **presenting business ideas to feedback providers**. **The NABC normally fits on one page.**
- **Group size** **1–6** — "usually it is enough for one person to create the first draft of the formulation. More people review content and comprehensibility." · **Duration** **20–40 min** — "you can describe an NABC in sufficient detail and in an understandable manner in 20 to 40 minutes" · **Materials** the **NABC cross** printed on A4/A3 or electronic, Post-its, pens, markers.
- **Template — the NABC cross, four quadrants with their printed sub-fields:**

| Quadrant | Sub-fields printed on the cross | What to write |
|---|---|---|
| **N — Need** | Customer · Problem · Situation | the problem the customer has; the typical customer who has it; the typical everyday situation in which it crops up; **the need that results from it** |
| **A — Approach** | Solution · Product · Service · Business model · Process | how to solve the problem — the approach / **performance promise**; the product, service, or process; how the business model earns money |
| **B — Benefit** | Customer benefit · Business benefit · Value proposition · Benefits · Advantages | formulate **in terms of both quality and quantity**: the benefit for the customer, and the benefits for you / your company |
| **C — Competition** | Alternatives · Competition · Unique Selling Proposition | the alternatives and competitors **existing today and in the future**, plus the unique selling points of the solution |

- **Supporting method — the watering hole with green and red feedback.** *Green feedback: What are the strengths of the idea? What should be retained? Red feedback: What are the weaknesses? What should be improved?*
- **Variation — NABC and the elevator pitch (p. 179).** A three-part pitch with NABC in the middle:
  - **Entry point (hook)** — "attract attention with a statement, a keyword, a headline, a question. **Provoke!** Whet the appetite, kindle interest. Be different, unique, surprising."
  - **Middle part (core)** — **NABC**.
  - **Conclusion (close)** — "What comes next? What is to be achieved? Invitation and next steps."
- **Expert (Mathias Strazza, Head Future Banking, PFLab / PostFinance Ltd.)**: ***"The NABC begins with the customer and his problem — and not with the solution."*** It conveys the core of an idea in a **5-minute pitch**. Always add visualizations, sketches, drawings; storytelling helps convey NABC content. It is not imperative to keep the cross in a presentation — with PowerPoint, show the sections one per slide. **Once the problem is described and a solution is known, intentionally look for different, new solutions**, particularly when hunting radical change.

## 8. Blue ocean tool & buyer utility map (p. 181)

***I would like…*** *to differentiate a product or service from the competition and open up new market opportunities.*

- **Origin.** Blue ocean and blue ocean shift, **invented by W. Chan Kim and Renée Mauborgne**, help define a unique value proposition. *"With a buyer utility map, the focus is put on the user/customer. His experience with services and products usually can be seen as a cycle that is broken down into **six phases**."*
- **The six buyer-experience phases:** **purchase · delivery · use · accessories · maintenance · disposal.**
- **Group size** **4–6** — "It is easier to develop suggestions for the buyer utility map on smaller design teams than on large teams"; split large teams · **Duration** **30–120 min** — completing the buyer utility map usually takes **30 minutes**; the complete implementation including a comprehensive competitor analysis takes **several days** · **Materials** sheets of paper, pens, Post-its, markers, **print templates in A0**.
- **Template — the four actions framework, with its printed questions:**

| Action | Printed question |
|---|---|
| **Raise** | What factors might be **raised far above** the previous industry standard? |
| **Reduce** | What factors might be **reduced far below** the previous industry standard? |
| **Eliminate** | What factors are defined in the industry and might be **eliminated**? |
| **Create** | What factors that the industry has **not offered so far** might be created? |

- **Procedure.**
  1. Start with the four actions framework. Focus on the **strategic factors** that direct or alternative competitors — or the industry as a whole — concentrate on (e.g. *productivity, price, guarantee*).
  2. Determine which can be raised, reduced, eliminated, or newly created; choose the **most critical** factors.
  3. **Arrange these critical factors in the buyer utility matrix** (critical factors × the six experience phases). First define the decision factors critical to the customer with respect to **the offer known today**.
  4. Think about what can be reduced or eliminated. *"Now comes the creative part"* — brainstorm with the team to reach the **unused factors** and identify additional value ranges the offer could cover.
  5. Define the new **"blue ocean" value proposition** from the result.
- **The three oceans (printed table, p. 183):**

| | **Red ocean** | **Blue ocean** | **Black ocean** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Market | Existing markets | Non-competitive markets | **Ecosystem-driven markets** |
| Competition | Strong competition | Few competitors | **Without competition** |

- **Variation — black ocean / minimum viable ecosystem (MVE).** Cross-referenced to *The Design Thinking Playbook* (Lewrick et al., 2018, p. 240 ff.). *"The black ocean strategy aims at forming a system of actors in which the other competitors have no chance of competing in the long term."* The procedure is iterative: an **MVE evolves across four design loops** and "serves as a basis for the optimization of the value streams and offers of each individual actor in such a way that competitors could not survive." **In defining the offer, select initially those offers that have the greatest market penetration, the fewest functionalities, and the greatest customer benefit.**
- **Expert (Alice Froissac, co-founder Openers, ME310 alumna d.school Paris)**: *"With a change in perspective, the offer of the competitor is neither scary nor do we have to copy it. It serves as an inspiration."* Expand the buyer utility map and non-customers map individually — her printed prompts: *How did the user become aware of the product? Did he compare products prior to purchase? What type of delivery does he prefer? Does information on the handling of returns have an impact? Were ongoing expenses an issue? Do the comments in social media influence the purchasing process?* For use, attributes like **simplicity, convenience, and risk minimization** can be crucial.

## Mental Models

- **Quantity before quality.** The whole generation half is optimized for volume; criticism is deferred by design.
- **Separate ideation from evaluation.** Stated as a key learning for both dot voting and 6-3-5 — the tools are structurally built to keep the two apart.
- **Silence as a levelling device.** 6-3-5 works "without speaking" precisely so hierarchy and extroversion cannot dominate; dot voting has the boss vote last for the same reason.
- **Empty quadrants are information.** In the 2x2 matrix an empty quadrant "signifies potential for further opportunities and unfulfilled needs."
- **The analogy is a pain-transfer machine.** Find someone who already solved *your pain* somewhere else, then port their solution back.
- **Start with the customer's problem, not the solution** — the reason NABC is ordered N-A-B-C.
- **Differentiate by subtraction as much as by addition** — the four actions framework gives *eliminate* and *reduce* equal weight with *raise* and *create*.

## Anti-patterns

- **Criticizing ideas during generation** — "we might need it again later."
- **Losing the Post-it walls.** Photograph them; "they easily get lost in the heat of the moment."
- **Complex ideas on the matrix** — *"Keep the ideas as simple as possible — complexity means confusion on the matrix."* Rewrite the Post-it or split the idea.
- **Assuming any axis pair works.** *"Not all combinations of axes yield target-oriented results. Experiment with different possibilities and adapt the axes to the problem statement."*
- **The bandwagon effect in voting** — public, sequential voting, or the boss going first.
- **Voting without stated criteria** — the criteria must be explained before the process starts.
- **Discarding losing ideas** — park them; "if the team does not make good headway, the parking lot is a good place to get inspiration."
- **Talking ideas to death** — the explicit advantage 6-3-5 claims over open brainstorming.
- **Going in circles** in ideation — the trigger to switch to special brainstorming.
- **Looking only at obvious competitors** in the NABC's C — Lilly's standing reminder.

## Worked Example

**The 2x2 matrix, Lilly's team (p. 158) — the axis-selection dialogue, verbatim.**
*"Should we take 'impact' and 'implementation expenditure' as axes or 'cool' and 'doable'?"* — *"In our case, I think the first two are more suitable because [we have] already advanced quite a bit in the project."* Then, positioning: *"This idea has little impact, and the realization will be definitely difficult."* And the outcome: *"We have three interesting area[s] that we will pursue."*
The narrative: "In the very early phases, Lilly likes using the 'cool' and 'doable' axes. But because they are already on the brink of the **problem/solution fit**, they'd rather use the 'impact' and 'expenditure' axes… **The ideas in the orange area are put in the parking lot for ideas.**"

**Dot voting, Lilly's team (p. 162) — the anti-bandwagon protocol in practice.**
*"We just go and fetch glue dots so we can quickly make a selection with Lilly and the team."* — *"**Lilly, you should be last with gluing your dots on the ideas — otherwise the team tends to follow your opinion.**"* — all together: *"My last glue dot ☺"*.
Narrative: "Everybody on the team ponders where he/she wants to put the dots. Then **all team members glue the dots on the ideas at the same time.** They make sure that Lilly places her dots after everybody else… There is one idea that everybody seems to like. The other dots are distributed all over. Now the team discusses once more the ideas that have dots, **combines a number of ideas, and goes into another evaluation round to get an alternative to the first idea**."

**6-3-5, Lilly's sub-team (p. 166) — the run, with its numbers.**
"A sub-team of Lilly's team, consisting of **6 people**, wants to generate many initial ideas. To do so, all team members **draw a 3×6 grid** on a sheet of paper and write down/sketch the first **3 ideas on Post-its within 4 minutes**, working in private, and put them into the grid. Then they pass the sheet on to the next person and write down 3 more ideas, some of which build upon the previous ones, until the sheet is full. **In this way, up to 108 ideas are collected in less than half an hour.** Again in six rounds, the ideas are then evaluated in the groups according to previously defined criteria and subsequently presented."

**Special brainstorming, Lilly's teams (p. 170).** "Some teams felt relatively early on that they were **going in circles** and couldn't move forward. Other techniques, for example, **negative brainstorming**, helped the team to broaden their mental horizon… In the subsequent **figuring storming**, well-known enterprises such as **Apple and Google** and people like **Elon Musk** were used. This way, more potential approaches to a solution could be identified."

**Analogies, Lilly's team (p. 176).** "After an intense brainstorming session about analogies, thoughts come up about **zoos, aliens, strange planets, off-road expeditions through Africa**, and much more." In the comic: *"Off-road expedition. Trip!"* — *"Exactly! Expedition trip! With the right tools and a map, you are prepared for many situations, 'local experts' know their way around on site."* — *"Inspiring ideas have arisen from the brainstorming! Let's see if anything can be inferred from zoos…."* The team's sketch note is labelled **STATUS**. "Finally, decisive stimuli arise from the analogies regarding the topics of **culture, the natural environment, and comprehension of new circumstances**."

**Blue ocean, Lilly's team (p. 184).** "Lilly is in a **mature market**. The teams have found that **most competitors focus on boosting productivity through digital transformation.** This gives Lilly's team the opportunity to address **other levers** for increasing the benefit for the customer." Key learnings: interview users regularly while defining a blue ocean strategy; get experts from your own or other industries involved; fill out the buyer utility map as a team; explore black-ocean opportunities.

## Key Takeaways

- One clear HMW question per brainstorming session; 5–15 minutes, then change the incentive.
- Structured brainstorming and 6-3-5 both buy fairness with silence; use them where hierarchy or reticence would distort the room.
- 108 ideas in under 30 minutes is the 6-3-5 benchmark to hold yourself to.
- 2x2 axes must be SMART, and the *empty* quadrant is often the most valuable read.
- Dot voting: state criteria first, 3–5 dots, private, simultaneous, boss last.
- Special brainstorming is the unsticking move, not the default.
- Analogies work by transferring pains, not features; absurd distance is a feature.
- NABC is the one-page package for feedback; the buyer utility map is the differentiation package.
