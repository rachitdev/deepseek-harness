# Chapter 2 — Warm-Ups That Fit the Setting

*(Book pages 39–46)*

## Core Idea

"In sports, warm-ups are used to heighten the performance of muscles. For musicians, warming up means preparing the voice for singing. In motor racing, warm-ups help to fine-tune technical details. In design thinking, we use warm-ups not only at the beginning of the workshop but also after a break or when we realize that the group is no longer working efficiently."

The book is unusually cautious about them: **"But they can also have the opposite effect. If the context doesn't fit or the culture of the organization is not taken into consideration, an exercise can end up fostering nervousness and irritation. So here is our advice: If you want to perform a warm-up, make sure you have chosen it very carefully."**

## Frameworks Introduced

### Why do we do warm-ups? (the book's eight reasons)

Create a positive group atmosphere · give workshop participants the chance to get to know one another · remove social barriers · reduce pressure to succeed · activate and release positive energy · distract the group a little so concentration is improved afterward · prepare the team for a certain mindset or way of working · have fun and share a laugh.

### The Warm-up Finder

The book prints a six-axis selector for choosing a warm-up: **little time · lots of time · focus (two focus dimensions) · handling · visually**. (The individual axis labels are partially garbled in extraction; the six selector categories are as listed.)

## Key Concepts

Each warm-up page carries the same four fields: *I would like…*, **Learning objective**, work instruction (numbered steps), and Group size / Typical duration / Materials needed. Only some warm-ups print an explicit group size and duration; where the book leaves the box blank, this chapter says so rather than inventing a number.

## Worked Example

### Marshmallow challenge — the full six-step instruction as printed

1. The challenge is to build, as a group, a **free-standing sculpture that is as high as possible, with the marshmallow on top**.
2. Each group gets a specified range of building materials.
3. The marshmallow is the measuring point, so it must be placed at the very top of the structure.
4. The structure is only allowed to be connected to the floor or the table surface. It is not allowed to be fastened to the ceiling.
5. The sculpture must be standing free at the latest when the specified building time has expired, and it must not collapse in less than 10 seconds.
6. Each group has **15–20 minutes**. If a group finishes earlier, the moderator can be summoned at any time to determine the height.

**Tip (the book's):** *"The reflection afterward is important. Talk about action prejudices, 'Fail early, fail often!,' the number of iterations tests and the teamwork."*

### Clapping game — the escalation as printed

1. Each participant chooses a partner.
2. Together they count up to "three," repeating it with increasing speed.
3. To increase complexity, the number "two" is replaced by a clap — you get a *numberclap* rhythm.
4. Next, "one" is replaced by a finger click; the numberclap rhythm is supplemented with a finger click, "and there will be lots of laughing participants."

**Variation:** to emphasize acceptance of mistakes, integrate a reflection on one's own mistakes and other players' mistakes. *"A cheer after every wrong clap, click, or count intensifies the feeling."*

### Bingo — the printed card categories

The book prints a 5×5 sample card. Sample squares: has read 3 English novels · has eaten pizza prosciutto 3 times · has been to the United States at least 3 times · has visited at least 3 other countries · has drunk at least 3 Red Bulls · has been to Australia or New Zealand · has seen 3 James Bond movies in the movie theater · has been to Paris · favorite color is blue · has read Harry Potter · speaks three or more languages · has a parent from another country · has siblings · can play a musical instrument · loves the Beatles more than the Rolling Stones · wears sneakers rather than leather shoes · likes to vacation at the sea · loves hiking and the mountains · has used car sharing · joined the Boy Scouts · has some design thinking experience · owns *The Design Thinking Playbook* · has bought something at Kickstarter · knows the lean canvas · would like to work in a start-up.

**Two rules, as printed:** (1) Each person is allowed to answer a question with "yes" only once, in order to get into a conversation with as many people as possible. (2) First the field must be offered, only then is the discussion allowed to start.

**Sequence:** distribute pen + prepared card → on the start signal, participants search for people fitting the categories → first to fill every field shouts "Bingo!" → if nobody fills the card, the game ends at the time limit and the participant with the most squares wins → a final reflection of the statements rounds out the warm-up.

**Variation:** prepare cards for a specific topic ("has a whiteboard in his office," "has already worked with design thinking") or unusual questions ("keeps to a vegan diet," "goes skydiving regularly"). For big groups or short time, change the goal to filling one row — horizontal, vertical, or diagonal.

### Stop & go — the command escalation

Everybody moves freely in the room. The moderator calls "stop" (stand still) or "go" (keep walking). Then **the meanings are switched**: keep walking at "stop," stand still at "go." Two more actions are added — "name" (state your name) and "clap" — and their meanings are also switched. Then "jump" and "dance" are added and again swapped. *"If everything is done right, 'name, name, name' triggers a round of applause at the end."*
**Variations:** participants replace the moderator and issue commands; swap jumping for sitting down and clapping for high fives; use arbitrary words (Cola = turn right, Fanta = turn left, Sprite = jump, Red Bull = …); anyone who makes a mistake cheers and claps.

### 30 Circles — with the evaluation criterion that matters

Supply each participant a pen and a sheet with circles drawn on it. Ask them to transform as many circles as possible into recognizable products. On "Go!" the clock starts; after **2 minutes** time is stopped. Then compare results — **and pay attention not only to the number of circles filled but to the individuality of the results**: "a volleyball, a football, and a basketball are more similar to one another than a clock, a ring, and an eye." Also ask: has somebody "broken the rules" and connected two or more circles into one product?
**Variation:** state a word (e.g. pot lid) and challenge participants to write down as many alternative uses as possible — toboggan, frisbee, wheel cover, drums.

### Ninja — the five steps

1. Divide participants into small groups; they stand in a close circle (hands can touch the shoulders of their neighbors).
2. One person shouts "Ninjaaa!" (lengthening the word makes for greater effect); everybody jumps back and freezes in a Ninja pose of their choice.
3. A previously appointed member begins attempting to hit another person's hand with **only one movement and one step**. Anyone touched must freeze in that position; the person attacked is allowed to pull his or her hand away.
4. When your hand is hit you are out and must leave the game.
5. As soon as the move is completed, the next player clockwise starts, based on the original sequence in the circle. The game continues until only one person is left.

## Key Takeaways

- Warm-ups are situational instruments, not decoration — a mismatched warm-up damages the workshop.
- Two of the six (clapping game, stop & go) exist specifically to teach **handling mistakes**; the marshmallow challenge teaches **iteration and thinking with your hands**; 30 circles removes the **white sheet blockade**.
- The debrief after the marshmallow challenge is the point, not the tower.
- Where a duration is printed, it is short: 5–20 minutes.
