# Chapter 1 — Foundations: Mindset, Process & Quick Start

*(Book pages 5–36: Preface, The Toolbox in a nutshell, The global survey, What is design thinking?, Quick start)*

## Core Idea

The Toolbox exists because the authors surveyed **more than 2,500 design thinkers** about **150 candidate tools** and kept only those the community rated as most useful — "just over 50 tools, described by more than 100 experts." Its organizing claim, from Prof. Ulrich Weinberg's (HPI School of Design Thinking) preface: *"the selection of the right tools across the entire design thinking cycle makes a vital contribution to success. The selection depends on the situation, the team, the possibilities, and the respective goal. Design thinking is not a rigid concept."*

The authors are emphatic that this is **not a cookbook**: "What we absolutely didn't want to do is publish a 'cookbook.'" Their operating slogan is **"Love it, change it, or leave it!"** — adapt or discard any tool that does not fit.

## Frameworks Introduced

### The design thinking micro-cycle (six phases + Reflect)

| Phase | Purpose | Named entry tools (page) |
|---|---|---|
| **Understand** | Learn about the potential user, needs, tasks; define the creative framework | Interview for empathy (57), Extreme users (79), 5W+H questions (71) |
| **Observe** | Go where users are; test assumptions against reality | AEIOU (107), Persona (97), Trend analysis (119) |
| **Define point of view** | Evaluate, interpret, weight findings into a synthesis | Context mapping (133), Storytelling (129), Vision cone (141), HMW (125) |
| **Ideate** | Generate solutions | Brainstorming (151), Analogies (171), Dot voting (159) |
| **Prototype** | Make ideas testable quickly and without risk | Prototype kinds (187ff.), Exploration map (195) |
| **Test** | Interact with the potential user; document | Testing sheet (213), A/B tests (233) |
| **Reflect** | Learn from action | Retrospective sailboat (243), I like/I wish/I wonder (239) |

Double diamond mapping: **phases 1–3 = problem space; phases 4–6 = solution space.**

### The point-of-view sentence scheme

> Name of the user/persona: (who)
> needs: (what is needed)
> in order to: (his/her need)
> because: (insight/finding)

### The macro-cycle

Run the micro-cycle repeatedly. Divergent phase = as many wild ideas and prototypes as possible, which sharpens the vision. Convergent phase = more specific, higher-resolution prototypes; the **functional prototype** reviews problem/solution fit for individual elements before maturing into a **final prototype**. Then implementation and market launch — for which many products need a designed business ecosystem based on an **MVP** and a **Minimum Viable Ecosystem (MVE)**.

### The three dimensions

Desirability (customer/user need) × Viability (profitable) × Feasibility (technically implementable). *"Why are the three dimensions important? They reduce the risks associated with the launch of new solutions. They help teams, organizations, and companies learn faster. They lead us to solutions that are innovative and not only incremental."*

### The design brief

The design brief is "the translation of a problem into a structured task" — the problem statement is the minimum requirement, the brief adds detail (at the cost of creative leeway). Its six element groups:

1. **Design space and scope** — which activities, for whom? Who are the users and key stakeholders? What do we want to learn about the user?
2. **Existing approaches** — what exists already, how can it help, what is missing?
3. **Design principles** — important leads for the team; limitations; indispensable core functions; whom to involve and when.
4. **Scenarios** — what does a desirable future/vision look like? Which scenarios are plausible and possible?
5. **Next steps and milestones** — by when should a solution exist? Which steering committee meetings give feedback?
6. **Implementation challenges** — who must be involved early? What is the culture for radical proposals and risk appetite? Budget/time restrictions?

If the client has little design thinking expertise, make creating the brief its own little design thinking project.

## Key Concepts

**The global survey (spring 2018).** Distributed virally, mainly via social media; 2,500+ respondents from **44 countries**. Experience level: **85% had worked with design thinking for more than two years; 23% for more than 7 years.** Sectors: consultancy 30%, digital/IT 18%, education 12%, banks/insurance/services 10%, production/supply chain/logistics 7%, other 23% (pharma/biotech 4%, NGOs 2%). Geography: Europe 65%, North America 16%, South America 7%, Asia 7%, Australia 3%, Africa 2%. The survey measured **Awareness [%]** (know the tool) and **Popularity [%]** (rate it very useful or as their favorite, among those who know it). Finding: *"the better known tools are also the most popular… The simpler and more user-friendly the tools, the more often they are applied."*

**The eight mindset elements** (from the book's mindset wheel): driven by curiosity; focused on people; explore the key to complex systems; visualize and show; experiment and iterate; co-create, grow, and scale; develop process awareness (including a feeling for the groan zone); collaborate in networks (ad-hoc, agile, T-shaped people, U-shaped teams); reflect on actions.

**Harvey-ball notation.** Each tool page and the cover Quickfinder marks one **full or half Harvey ball per process step** to signal where the tool yields great benefit. Only guidance — "Every moderator of design thinking workshops should develop his own sense of how and when the individual methods and tools are used."

**"Drippy T" model.** Horizontal axis = general experience/breadth; vertical axis = depth of expert knowledge. The "drippy T" extends the T-shape to highlight the additional disciplines, departments, and subject fields a team member carries.

**Team size rule.** 3–7 members; **5 is ideal**; over 10 coordination becomes complex and effectiveness dwindles. For wicked problems add an "extended team" of experts outside the core team; for more people, run several teams concurrently (team-of-teams).

**Workshop logistics.** Preparation takes as much time as the workshop itself. Plan long breaks — "It is often underestimated how exhausting 'creative work' is." Use warm-ups at the start, after lunch, or when energy drops. Documentation: fastest is photos of flip charts, personas, Post-it walls, feedback capture grids, and finished prototypes; send a log of results the same day if possible. Schedule cleanup time.

**Space.** A good space stimulates, inspires creativity, supports reflection and collaboration, and fits the purpose. **At least 15 m² per group of four.** Flexible furniture; high tables improvised by placing a table on four chairs (test stability); walls covered with paper as work surfaces; whiteboards and flip charts; prototyping material within reach; water, coffee, juice, fruit, chocolate, cookies.

**Materials.** *Prototyping*: aluminum foil, pipe cleaners, adhesive tape, cardboard, strings, Styrofoam, foam rubber, plastic folders, tracing paper, glue pens, white glue, Legos, figures, Play-Doh, felt and fabric, paper in various shapes and sizes. *Moderation*: flip chart paper, Sharpies, A4/A3 paper, Post-its in various colors and sizes, one prepared moderation box per group (glue pens, scissors, adhesive points, tapes, Post-its, pens). *Technical*: projector, speakers, camera, multiple sockets, extension cords, adapters.

## Mental Models

- **Tetris, not assembly line.** "It is like working on a puzzle, only dynamically… Like with each Tetris level, the arrangement, the speed, and the sequence change with each design challenge… If we do not agilely adapt the methods and tools in the workshop, there will be a quick 'game over' for our project."
- **The beginner's mind** (Shunryu Suzuki: *"In the beginner's mind, there are many possibilities, but in the expert's mind there are few."*) — ask questions as though you had no idea of the answer; like an alien landing on Earth.
- **The facilitator is a neutral instrument.** He chooses methods and tools, owns time boxing and planning, and must be willing to make ad-hoc changes — but must not make active contributions in terms of content.
- **The end weighs as much as the beginning.** "An abrupt end triggers dissatisfaction… The conclusion must be planned in just as meticulous detail as the entry point." Define clear next steps and allocate tasks.

## Anti-patterns

- **Publishing a cookbook agenda.** "A detailed agenda without any flexibility and the wrong methods at the wrong time not only turned the workshop into a lousy experience for participants but, far worse, there was no viable solution in the end, nor had the actual problem been dealt with." Consequence: the team does design thinking exactly once and then defends against it.
- **Starting with solutions.** Asking "does somebody have a good idea?" yields solution proposals resting on an assumed understanding of needs.
- **Over-specifying the challenge.** Naming the technology (e.g. "solve it with AI") in the problem statement restricts the idea space and may lose market opportunities.
- **Narrow design briefs.** More detail accelerates solution finding but leaves little leeway for creativity.
- **Conference mode.** Sitting down invites coffee and endless PowerPoint — "relaxing but of little help when it comes to shaping the next big market opportunity."
- **Assuming a bad workshop was the group's fault.** "From our experience, something good comes out of most workshops, and if not, the workshop was usually poorly moderated."

## Reference Tables

### Facilitator's checklist (book's own list)

| Item | Rule from the book |
|---|---|
| Preparation time | = implementation time |
| Team size | 3–7, ideal 5, split above 10 |
| Space per 4 people | ≥ 15 m² |
| Agenda | Rough agenda to participants; detailed plan for inexperienced facilitators |
| Warm-ups | Start, after lunch, or when efficiency drops |
| Music | Quiet background during brainstorming |
| Feedback | "I like, I wish, I wonder" (p. 239) or a mood flasher, at two levels: (1) facilitation & team dynamics, (2) contents, goals, problem solution |
| Documentation | Photos of flip charts / personas / Post-it walls / feedback capture grids / prototypes; log sent same day |
| Wrap-up | Clear next steps and allocated tasks |

### Tips for the facilitator (verbatim list, p. 32)

Be open to dynamics and unexpected situations · be sensitive to the mood of the participants · laughter is important, especially about yourself · steer discussions actively in order to offer a "parking lot for ideas" · clear instructions and time lines for the participants · willingness to help with the performance of tasks · motivate participants to pursue the current goal (substantial ideas, increased depth of findings).

## Worked Example

### Lilly — the persona who carries every tool page

Lilly, **28**, is a design thinking and start-up coach at **Singapore University of Technology & Design (SUTD)**. She studied Enterprise Management at Zhejiang University School of Management, spent her Master's year at Ecole des Ponts ParisTech (d.school), worked with **THALES** as a corporate partner on an **ME310** project in collaboration with Stanford (visiting Stanford three times), and is doing a doctorate with MIT on "Design of Powerful Business Ecosystems in a Digitized World." She uses the **HBDI (Herrmann Brain Dominance Instrument)** to build productive groups of four to five, uniting all thinking modes; her own preference sits clearly in the right brain hemisphere.

**Her design challenge**: found a consultancy for digital transformation in Singapore with her friend Jonny, and find the unique offer versus traditional consultancies. Supporting evidence she cites: a **McKinsey study finding companies applying design thinking had 32% higher sales growth over a 5-year period**. She refuses to segment for now; her differentiator is cultural — she has watched the European/American design thinking mindset fail in an Asian context, and wants to integrate the attitude of an anthropologist, the acceptance of copying competitors, and the penchant for marketing services quickly rather than observing the market for a long time.

**Her problem statement, as printed:**

> *"How might we define a consultancy service for digital transformation in Asia, taking into account local cultural needs for large companies?"*

**Her team**: former students who volunteered, working the challenge over **14 weeks**, intending to use the full range of tools plus warm-ups as needed. The photo-comic of Lilly's team appears on the last page of every tool, beginning on page 49.

### The workshop canvas — the book's own 3-day agenda

| Day 1 | Day 2 | Day 3 |
|---|---|---|
| **Kick-off & warm-up** — introduce team and skills via T-shaped profiles; present the design thinking mindset | **Persona** — create a persona from observations and the POV | **Build and test prototypes** — create the final prototype |
| **Scoping** — present the design challenge; define the problem statement | **Ideate** — different types of brainstorming; cluster and connect ideas; prioritize ideas | **Validation** — test the prototype, lean canvas |
| **Brain dump** — brainstorming | **Build and test prototypes** — build and test critical experience prototype; build various prototypes; test; improve | **Presentation** — present the solution |
| **Research** — explore existing solutions; observe and understand users | | **Wrap-up** — next steps; reflection/feedback; clean up |
| **Synthesis** — exchange observations and storytelling; point of view | | |

The **workshop canvas** itself (download: `www.dt-toolbook.com/agenda-en`) is organized as **Planning → Implementation → Follow-up**, with planning fields for design challenge, participants, space, material, facilitator, catering, invite users, invite participants, detailed agenda per day; follow-up fields for record results (photo), clean room, logging, defined next steps; and a feedback field "What is to be improved?".

## Key Takeaways

- Tool choice is situational; the book's ranking comes from popularity among 2,500+ practitioners, not from evidence of effectiveness.
- The micro-cycle's first three phases are the problem space — spend real time there before ideating.
- Two hard constraints on any problem statement: broad enough for creativity, narrow enough for your resources.
- Five-person T-shaped teams, 15 m² per four people, standing work, and preparation time equal to workshop time are the book's default operating parameters.
- The failure mode the authors most fear is a rigid agenda producing a bad first experience that inoculates the organization against design thinking.
