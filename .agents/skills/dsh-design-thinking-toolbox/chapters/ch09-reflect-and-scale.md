# Chapter 9 — Reflect, Scale & Close

*(Book pages 236–295; 7 reflection/implementation tools + 6 application programs + closing words)*

## Core Idea

The last stretch of the Toolbox does three jobs in sequence.

**First, reflect.** Design thinking adds a seventh phase to the six-phase micro-cycle for a reason: *"We need feedback throughout the entire design thinking process for the improvement of prototypes, stories, and business models."* The reflection tools (I like/I wish/I wonder, retrospective sailboat, lessons learned) are all built around the same discipline — feedback that is structured, positive, written down, and acted on. **Feedback is a gift.**

**Second, carry the result outward.** A validated prototype is worthless inside the team. The pitch, the lean canvas, the road map for implementation and the innovation funnel are the tools that move a design thinking result into a business decision. The funnel is where the book's larger frame appears: **problem identification → problem/solution fit → product/market fit → scale**, with **business ecosystem design** and the **MVE (minimum viable ecosystem)** sitting at the scaling end.

**Third, scale the mindset itself.** The final six entries are not tools but **programs** — "selected applications that cover the entire breadth of design thinking. The initiatives range from programs at universities, such as the legendary ME310, to intrapreneurship programs that give employees the opportunity of realizing their own ideas. Moreover, design thinking can be used in the context of 'design your future,' that is, for the design of one's own life planning."

The closing sentence of the whole book states the stance: ***"Do it and don't just talk about it, because design thinking lives from the application!"***

# Part A — Reflection tools

## 1. "I like, I wish, I wonder" [IL/IW/IW] (p. 239)

***I would like…*** *to provide constructive feedback and keep a positive mood.*

- **Positioning.** *"'I like, I wish' is particularly suitable for **sensitive projects**. By maintaining a positive mood, a relationship that is based on partnership evolves between the feedback provider and the feedback recipient."* It works both on collaboration and on a specific result. The book's own two examples: ***"I like how you motivated us to conduct another customer survey"*** and ***"I wish that the prototype were tested in other cultures as well."***
- What it delivers: a **feedback ritual** in which "only 'I like, I wish,' supplemented with 'I wonder' (in short: IL/IW/IW), is permitted as feedback" · a way to **celebrate small successes** achieved in an iteration, with a prototype or in a test · a part of reflection *and* ideation, expandable with **"What if…"** and a parking lot for ideas · written and spoken feedback.
- **Group size** **3–5**; ideally feedback is given by several people, but it also works with **2** (provider and receiver) · **Duration** **15–90 min** — *"can be deployed after small iterations and in large feedback sessions with multiple participants"* · **Materials** Post-its and pens, large sheet of paper.
- **Alternatives** red and green feedback (adhesive dots — **red = attention, green = all ok**), lessons learned (p. 255), feedback capture grid (p. 217), retrospective sailboat (p. 243). **Supporting tools** design thinking mindset (p. 6), brainstorming (p. 151).
- **Template — the five-column grid (p. 240), reproduced:**

| TEAM/PROTOTYPE | I LIKE… | I WISH… | I WONDER… | WHAT IF…? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Team X — Prototype 1 | | | | |
| Team Y — Prototype 2 | | | | |
| Team Z — Prototype 3 | | | | |

- **Procedure.**
  1. Take a large sheet of paper and draw the five-column table. Enter the names of the teams and their prototypes in the rows.
  2. To obtain feedback on a presented prototype, **give each participant at least three Post-its**; each completes the sentence stem on the X-axis ("I like," "I wish," "I wonder").
  3. ***"For an interaction to emerge, each participant should read aloud what's written on the Post-its before sticking them on the grid."*** Then everybody places their Post-its.
  4. Once all Post-its are on the sheet, **reflect on the findings** and ask whether there are insights important for the next iteration.
  5. ***"Starting a discussion as the recipient of the feedback should be avoided. It would change the mood, and the positive attitude is lost."*** The tool aims at **avoiding ad hominem criticism** and maintaining a positive mood.
  6. ***"The feedback should be seen as a gift by the feedback recipient."***
- **Expert (Lena Papasz, self-employed design thinking coach and marketing consultant, Germany)**: *"Albert Einstein has aptly put it: 'Problems can never be solved with the same way of thinking that created them.'"* Her tips:
  - **Replace "I wish, I wonder" with "How to…"** in order to generate not only ideas but **initial solution proposals**.
  - Integrate the tool in **1:1 feedback interviews**.
  - **Conduct weekly team meetings with IL/IW/IW** — "so as to involve all team members, ensure that tasks are taken seriously, and **recognize concerns early on**."
  - **Feedback is a gift.** As a provider, always show respect and ask: *"How would we feel about this feedback?"* When giving personal feedback, **look the other person in the eye**. As a receiver, listen carefully, ask again if necessary, and ***always say thank you for feedback***.
  - **Add "I learned"** at the end of a project so personal key learnings can be recorded for upcoming projects (see lessons learned, p. 255).
  - *"We can start our day with 'I like, I wish, I wonder' and 'What if…,' thus setting ourselves daily goals"* — the bridge to *design thinking life* (p. 289).

## 2. Retrospective "sailboat" (p. 243)

***I would like…*** *to reflect on the procedure and learn something new to improve myself (or the procedure) for the next iteration, at the end of each iteration, and at the end of the project.*

- **Positioning.** *"The 'sailboat' retrospective is frequently used with the **SCRUM** method in the context of the reflection on a sprint. It can also be drawn as a retrospective board with four quadrants."* It suits any design thinking phase or a completed project, and works **with two people only** — for example as a **"post-mortem" after the joint moderation of a workshop**. *"The tool responds to **accelerating, inhibitory, and environmental factors**. It also promotes the **'failure mindset'** in which mistakes are not seen as failures but as an opportunity to change and learn."*
- **Group size** **ideal with teams of 4–6**; large teams can be divided into sub-groups · **Duration** **60–120 min** — *"Depending on the objectives and the need for discussion, the retrospective can be kept shorter."* · **Materials** flip chart or whiteboard, Post-its, pens, markers.
- **Alternatives** feedback capture grid (p. 217), IL/IW/IW (p. 239), **"water cooler chats" with colleagues**. **Supporting tools** ask 5x why (p. 67), feedback capture grid (p. 217), lessons learned (p. 255), sketch notes (p. 173).
- **Template — the sailboat's four metaphor fields, with the book's own questions:**

| Field | Question |
|---|---|
| **WIND** | *"What was good? What gave us some wind at our backs?"* |
| **ANCHOR** | *"What didn't work so well on the team and slowed us down?"* |
| **CLIFFS** | *"What are the hazards and risks against which the team has no power?"* (e.g. market, new technologies, competitors) |
| **ISLAND** | *"What shared common vision and motivation does the team have?"* |

- **Alternative template — the retrospective board with four quadrants (p. 245):**

| | |
|---|---|
| **Things we continue to do in the same way** | **Things we will do differently next time** |
| **Things that are not relevant** | **Things we want to try out** |

- **Procedure — five steps.**
  1. **Opening:** introduce the objectives and the sequence of the session.
  2. **Collect information** on Post-its stuck to the corresponding fields. **Everybody reads his or her Post-its aloud.**
  3. **Cluster and prioritize findings:** select and deepen the most important topics. ***"Get to the bottom of things, that is, identify causes, so that more than just the symptoms are tackled."*** The aim is also **to address unpleasant issues** and create the basis for improvements.
  4. **Define measures:** *"we document precisely anything to be changed or tried out in a following iteration."*
  5. **Conclude the retro:** everybody gives brief feedback on the retro itself, e.g. with a feedback capture grid. ***"Ultimately, the group should part with good feelings."***
- **Expert (Achim Schmidt, design thinking coach & sketch note trainer, Germany — the book's own illustrator)**: *"We do design thinking every Tuesday from 2:00–4:00 p.m.! — I have actually heard somebody say that in a company. To me, design thinking is a mindset that views things holistically and is related to many areas and life as such!"* His tips:
  - **Create a positive mood.** Always **start with positive feedback and insights**. ***"No blame game!"*** Everybody must be heard out and be able to contribute.
  - **A major retro** (at key milestones or in difficult situations) should be conducted by **an external moderator or an employee who is not on the team**.
  - **Change the location:** *"Get out of the everyday working environment and meet at a coffee place or in a public park."*
  - **Build trust.** Especially with sensitive issues or hierarchical differences — **anonymity can help**. Explain the ***"Las Vegas principle": "What happens in this room stays between us for now!"*** Ask whether there are **"unspeakable" things or taboos** holding the team back.
  - ***"Don't skip it."*** "If the team members know one another well, the retrospective is often omitted. That's a pity."
  - **Implement small changes and measures; they frequently trigger big things.**
  - **A meta-opener:** *"You can start the retrospective with the question: 'Why do we conduct a retrospective?' Write down all the answers and make the findings visible to all. **This retro already offers a huge potential for change!**"*

# Part B — Carrying the result outward

## 3. Create a pitch (p. 247)

***I would like…*** *to share the results and insights with the team at the end of an iteration, and also at regular intervals with the stakeholders.*

- **Positioning.** *"The term 'pitch' actually comes from the advertising industry… The term also became established in the start-up scene… In design thinking, we use 'pitch' for the **brief presentation of the results before the teams or the stakeholders**."* Types differ by length: **the elevator pitch is the shortest** — *"a summarizing and informative outline of the idea within a very short period of time (often no more than approx. **30 seconds or 1 minute**)."* *"Usually, only a few PowerPoint slides are used, or none at all. **In many pitch presentations, real prototypes are shown**, making the presentation more vivid. Depending on the project phase, the focus is on different questions."*
- **Group size** **4–6** — the entire team, or one person presents and the others support and add details · **Duration** **60–120 min** preparation — *"The more impressive and short the pitch, the longer the preparation time. **A short preparation time of about 1 hour suffices for an intermediate presentation.** For the final presentations or an investor pitch, considerably more time is needed."* · **Materials** Post-its and pens, prototypes, **videos and photos of tests, customer feedback, and testimonials**.
- **Alternative** NABC (p. 177). **Supporting tools** lean canvas (p. 251), stakeholder map (p. 83), road map for implementation (p. 259), storytelling (p. 129).
- **Template — the pitch structure (p. 248), reproduced with its own prompts:**

| Block | Prompts |
|---|---|
| **Entry point (story)** | Attracts attention with a story |
| **Problem** | What is the greatest problem? Why is it a problem? Who is affected by the problem? |
| **Customers** | For whom is it a problem? (your persona) · What is the extent of the problem? (How great is the potential for a solution?) · **Who are the early adopters? With whom can we do co-creation?** |
| **Solution/idea** | What is our solution? (prototype, demo, including test feedback) · What is the value proposition? What makes us unique? · Why are we better than the existing alternatives? · **Why can only we implement the solution?** |
| **Business model** | How can we make money? · What are the challenges and risks? |
| **Next steps** | What are we going to do next? · What do we need for the next steps? · **Why is it worthwhile to solve the problem? Why now?** |
| **Summary** | Repeat the key messages · possibly introduce the team at external pitches |

- **Procedure — three steps.**
  1. **Rough planning.** *"Who are the listeners? What do they know already? Where can I meet them? What do they want to know? What's the framework? How much time do we have? What are the options for the presentation? What is my goal? What is my message?"* Then plan the rough sequence (e.g. with Post-its): define content, form, and **who does or says what**.
  2. **Break down into details** over several iterations. **Use stories and arouse emotions.** Follow **KISS (keep it short and simple) with a maximum of 10 slides**. ***"Use key figures. Figures say more than words! Pictures say more than words, and videos more than pictures."*** ***"Standard is boring! Don't use PowerPoint, if possible."*** **Show the prototype**, give a demonstration. **Repeat the key messages at the end** — *"Usually, a listener cannot remember more than 2 or 3 facts."*
  3. **Test, practice, and improve.** Test the pitch, practice the sequence, improve it iteratively. *"After the pitch, the team should be prepared for all sorts of questions."*
- **Expert (Patrick Link, Professor of Product Innovation, Lucerne University of Applied Sciences and Arts; co-founder Trihow AG — one of the book's authors)**: *"It is incredibly difficult to present a radically new business idea in 1 minute and instill enthusiasm for the idea in the audience… **Pitching must be practiced.** Use every opportunity to practice — be it after work with friends in a bar or at events. A good pitch deck is much more than just colorful PowerPoint slides."* His tips:
  - ***"The shorter the pitch, the more difficult it is to prepare, because in this case we have no chance to catch up or add something later."***
  - If you must show slides, observe **Guy Kawasaki's "10, 20, 30" rule for good pitches: 10 slides, 20-minute presentations, and font size 30.**
  - *"The audience can listen or read but not both at the same time. Don't use animations that distract."*
  - **Role-playing**, in which the prototype or the persona takes center stage, "has proven valuable here." Short videos or customer statements can also be intriguing — **test the sound and video and have backup scenarios** ready (projector fails, no sound).
  - **Be passionate.** *"It is not only about the idea but about **the team's ability to implement the solution** as well."*
  - The **lean canvas yields valuable clues for relevant content**; prepare additional information as a backup.

## 4. Lean canvas (p. 251)

***I would like…*** *to translate a problem into a solution that takes into consideration both the customer needs and my own business context.*

- **Positioning.** *"The completed lean canvas documents the ultimate **'problem/solution fit.'** The blocks lead through a logical sequence, **from customer problems to unfair advantages**. The canvas is **better suited for designing the solution than for exploring a problem**."* Its main job: *"to review the 'problem/solution fit' and adjust it if necessary… the collected data is compared to the best solution that fits the behavior and challenges of the customers."*
- **Lean canvas vs. business model canvas — the book's explicit decision rule.** *"We advise working with the lean canvas **first** since it takes greater account of the **validation of the solution as a minimum viable product** (see p. 207) and is comparably **less inside-out oriented** than the business model canvas. **Once the lean canvas is valid and optimization of the cost structure becomes more important, we switch to the business model canvas.**"*
- **Group size** **1–4** — *"Ideally, no more than 3–4 people work on a canvas. With larger groups, people should be assigned to several canvasses; later, the results are consolidated."* · **Duration** **60–120 min** — *"The initial creation takes approx. 60 minutes. In most cases, the focus will then be on **steps 1 through 5**. In iterations, the lean canvas is supplemented and advanced step by step with the new findings. **An update takes 10–15 minutes.**"* · **Materials** the canvas printed on **A0**, pens and markers, **Post-its in various colors and sizes (e.g. different colors per customer segment/stakeholder)**.
- **Alternative** business model canvas. **Supporting tools** NABC (p. 177), problem to growth & scale innovation funnel (p. 263), MVP (p. 207), persona/user profile (p. 97).
- **Template — the lean canvas blocks (p. 252), adapted by the book from A. Maurya, *Running Lean* (2013):**

| | | | | |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **① Problem** | **④ Solution** | **③ Unique value proposition** | **⑨ Unfair advantage** | **② Customer segments** |
| *(with)* **Existing alternatives** | **⑧ Key figures** | *(with)* **High-level concept** | **⑤ Channels** | *(with)* **Early adopters** |
| **⑦ Cost structure** | | | **⑥ Revenue streams** | |

- **Procedure — three steps.**
  1. **Fill in the canvas step by step.** *"In the early phases, the focus is on **steps 1 through 5** to review the 'problem/solution fit' (**problem, customer segments, value proposition, solution, and existing alternatives**). **Tip: First, iterate these five steps until a stable image has emerged.**"*
  2. **Complete the other steps in any order.** *"Tip: Depending on preferences, use Post-its of different colors for different customer segments or **according to risks (e.g. pink = high risk, must be tested quickly; yellow = medium risk; green = already tested or low risk)**."*
  3. ***"Identify the most risky assumptions and test them in experiments."***
- **Expert (Jens Springmann, Innovation Coach at creaffective, Germany)**: *"Many good ideas do not fail because we cannot implement them. They fail because we invest a lot of time, money, and effort to develop good solutions for the wrong questions."* His tips:
  - **Don't lose track of what's what.** ***"3 to 4 Post-its per field with one statement each."*** *"**Too many Post-its on a field indicates a lack of focus.** If the field is empty or almost empty, it should be worked on further."* (The book's own labels for the failure modes: **TOO THIN / TOO FAT**.)
  - **Record the content after each iteration** — document progress and record changes regularly (e.g. with photos). *"At a later point, when fewer major changes (**pivots**) are made, an electronic version can be used."*
  - **Use the KPIs common in the relevant industry** — *"we recommend using **Dave McClure's AARRR (pirate metrics)**."*
  - **Get feedback from outside.** Once the canvas is valid and needs no more change, transfer and expand it into the business model canvas.
  - **Customize the canvas** — add customer profiles and experiment reports.

## 5. Lessons learned (p. 255)

***I would like…*** *to reflect on and record the insights gained during and at the end of a design thinking project.*

- **Positioning.** *"The term 'lessons learned' originates from project management. Lessons learned means the **written recording and systematic collection and appraisal of positive and negative experiences, developments, findings, mistakes, and risks** during a project."* Different levels are considered: **technical, content, emotional, social, and process-related**. *"Design thinking projects usually produce a wide range of insights since the methods are always applied in the context of the problem statement, so **each design challenge takes a different course**."* For more extensive projects, use it **during** the project, not only at the end.
- **Group size** the **core team** and, if necessary, other stakeholders for reflection at project level; **reflection at the meta level is done by participants working individually** · **Duration** **40–60 min** for the group exercise, depending on project length — *"The longer the project, the more time should be planned for reflection. **The more time is scheduled for group work, the easier the individual work.**"* · **Materials** a **log book kept by each participant during the project in preparation for the workshop**, template or flip chart, Post-its and pens.
- **Alternatives** feedback capture grid (p. 217), retrospective sailboat (p. 243). **Supporting tools** lean canvas (p. 251), create a pitch (p. 247), IL/IW/IW (p. 239).
- **Template — the two reflection levels and their questions, reproduced:**

| **Step 1 — Project level** | **Step 2 — Meta level** |
|---|---|
| What went well in the project and what did not? | What does design thinking mean to me? What are the pros and cons? Challenges? |
| What methods did we apply? How? Why? | How is design thinking different from other approaches? |
| What should be done differently in future project work? What should or must change? | How can design thinking be combined with other approaches? |
| What are the key learnings from the project (positive and negative)? | How could design thinking be applied in my environment/my company? Which use cases might be of interest? |

  *"The most common method is that **all relevant project stakeholders jointly review the project in the form of a workshop exercise**."*
- **Expert (Stefanie Gerken, design thinking program lead, workshop specialist & coach, HPI School of Design Thinking, Germany)**: *"For me, design thinking is a holistic approach, a matter of attitude, of how to deal with your environment and not just a methodology used for single projects."* Her tips:
  - **Preparation questions for the workshop:** *"What specifically do we want to look at, and when? Where is the focus? What is the goal of the client? How will coordination with the client take place? Who will moderate the workshop? Which project participants should be invited?"*
  - ***"Wait with holding a lessons learned session if the mood after the project is rather bad. It has often worked for us to **wait 6 weeks** in such a case; seen from a greater distance, many issues can be looked at without stirring up too many emotions."***
  - **Lessons learned should be part of the project plan** — held after completion as part of the project completion documentation, *and* collected **during** the project (halfway in, or at crucial milestones) so they feed the next phases.
  - ***"Less is more when it comes to defined actions."*** Emphasize what proved successful and what failed; define actions based on that. ***"Do not focus only on the negative aspects and don't get personal!"***
  - Lessons learned is a good foundation for **defining the design principles** (p. 53), or as part of **"define success"** (p. 137).

## 6. Road map for implementation (p. 259)

***I would like…*** *to put the focus on the successful implementation of potential market opportunities right from the onset.*

- **Positioning.** *"The 'road map for implementation' is, as it were, **the road to success**. With the open approach of a complex problem statement, it is crucial to gain clarity about the context as quickly as possible. The tool helps determine the **critical factors** relevant to the planning of the implementation project. The aim is to get a **holistic view of the subsequent implementation as early in the process as possible**, to identify the **critical path and possible risks**."* It is *"created as early as possible, reviewed after each step, and adjusted if necessary. **It is our compass, so to speak, for realigning the project again and again to an initially vague and moving target.**"* The actual execution comes later, using the organization's normal project management methods.
- What it delivers: a guide from **current position (A)** to **destination (B)** — *"Where should the journey take us?"* · a check that the right target area and target group are still in view · a shared road to the destination · a **checklist basis with the most important factors of success** · *"Ensure that an uncertain path from A to B becomes clear over the course of the journey."*
- **Group size** *"Ideally, the **project manager, a designer, business or product managers, representatives of the users or customers, investor or sponsor, developer and challenger**, if appropriate."* · **Duration** **60–120 min per meeting** — *"The road map is created step by step; it can be done in portions over several days. Determine **several meetings of 60 to 120 minutes** for the iterative and incremental creation."* · **Materials** large sheet of paper or **A0 template**, pens, Post-its, **camera to capture the road map**.
- **Alternatives** scenario techniques, game plan, reality check. **Supporting tools** define success (p. 137), stakeholder map (p. 83), NABC (p. 177).
- **Template — the road map's axes and named fields (p. 260):** the horizontal process axis runs **Design concept → Prototype & test → Concept acceptance → Market launch of product/service**; the vertical bands hold **Define goals · Fields of action · Stakeholder**, and the printed row labels include **Market segment/customer group · Product · Value proposition · Process · Industrial ecosystem/goal unit cost · Market potential / market share / target price**, with a final band for the **assignment of the persons implementing the solution**.
- **Procedure — six steps.**
  1. **Define goals and build a control system;** if possible **quantify relevant decision-making criteria (KPIs)**; determine budget, schedule, milestones.
  2. **Draw up an overview with all participants.** The **stakeholder map** (p. 83) is useful for checking the goals for their suitability for implementation and, above all, for **putting together the right team**.
  3. **Define the structure, the main fields of design, and perspectives.** *"The 'standard perspectives' are **desirability, feasibility, and viability** (see p. 20). Depending on the problem statement, they can be further specified, for example, by dimensions such as **sustainability and environmental compatibility**."*
  4. **Describe the process steps or phases** — e.g. concept design, prototyping and testing (iteratively), and concept acceptance.
  5. **Fill in all fields with tasks, first in terms of quality;** then enter the results of the individual tasks — for example by means of a **customer journey** (p. 103) for concept design usefulness.
  6. ***"Position on the map all people implementing the solution or needed for the implementation, since it might be necessary to win them over later."***
- **Expert (Markus Durstewitz, Head of Method Development, Innovation and Design Thinking at Airbus — Strategy, New Business Models and Services, Germany)**: ***"In the end, the only thing that counts is the result (deliver or die). Design thinking is not a workshop format but a holistic approach to problem solving."*** His tips:
  - **The "who" question comes first:** *"**Who needs the solution? Who is the user/customer? Who will build it? Whom do we need for the implementation? Who pays? Who is funding the project? Who pays for the solution?**"*
  - ***"If we are not yet able to name all those involved, it usually indicates that our goal is not really clear yet."***
  - *"It is always important to choose the right method for every step. **The tool must fit the context.**"*
  - **Three coordinated cases:** *"The road map helps us to cover all areas, starting with the **use case**, to the **industrial case**, on up to the **business case**. They must be coordinated with one another."*
  - **Know the sponsors and adapt the pitch to them** — *"it is vital to know the needs of the decision makers and what kinds of 'stories' are 'in' at the moment."* **A structure according to NABC (p. 177) helps with the preparation.**

## 7. Problem to growth & scale innovation funnel (p. 263)

***I would like…*** *to make the growth initiatives transparent in a "funnel."*

- **Positioning.** *"The 'problem to growth & scale framework' constitutes the basis of a **modern, contemporary innovation funnel**. It is **not based on many different ideas that are filtered through gates** (as with classical innovation approaches); instead, it starts with the **'problem identification mode.'**"*
- **The framework's own axis (p. 262).** The funnel runs left to right through the stages **Problem → Solution → Problem/solution fit → Test & validate → Product/market fit → Business ecosystem design → Scale**. Two inputs feed the problem identification mode:
  - **Today's markets** — *"extrapolation from existing customers and technologies."*
  - **Future markets** — *"opening to new ecosystems, technologies, market roles,"* reached by **retropolation**.
  *"The problem identification mode is controlled by two factors. First, by the **exploration of existing customers and technologies**, and second, by **retropolation, a way of looking at future ecosystems, technologies, and market roles**."*
- **The discontinued-initiative rule.** *"Initiatives that are discontinued **remain in the funnel**. At regular intervals, the causes are examined in the context of a retrospective, for instance (see p. 243)."*
- **Group size** **1–2** maintaining it; *"The inputs for the topic and the current status come from the **strategy, design, and implementation teams** in an organization."* · **Duration** **30–120 min** — *"Gathering the information initially requires some effort. **The updates should be no longer than 30–60 minutes.**"* · **Materials** the funnel as an **A0 poster in a work room** or kept electronically in a PowerPoint presentation; possibly collaboration tools such as **Trello, Teams, OneNote**.
- **Alternative** road map for implementation (p. 259). **Supporting tools** trend analysis (p. 111), lean canvas (p. 251), vision cone (p. 141), stakeholder map (p. 83), retrospective sailboat (p. 243), **"pictures of the future" (by Siemens)**, **minimum viable ecosystem (MVE)** — referred to *The Design Thinking Playbook*, p. 240 ff.
- **Procedure — four steps.**
  1. **Name every project and establish a scale** for its size or contribution to company success — the book's own example brackets: ***< 5 million · 5–50 million · > 50 million***. *"The things being measured are adapted to the value system of the respective organization (e.g. **top line vs. bottom line**)."*
  2. **Place the current projects on the time axis**, differentiating **(A) existing business** from **(B) future topics**. *"This differentiation shows us **how much activity is invested in the development of the sustainable future** of an enterprise."*
  3. **Trace discontinued projects** so the reasons can be examined at regular intervals.
  4. **Update the funnel through regular control (e.g. monthly)**, and discuss on that basis **resource allocation and sales and profit targets**.
- **Expert (Michael Lewrick, best-selling author, innovation and digitization expert, Switzerland — one of the book's authors)**: *"The mapping of initiatives, including the corresponding development levels, allows for a quick overview of the innovation and growth portfolio."* His three-part method:
  - **How do we extrapolate?** *"We extrapolate from the world of today; our starting point is the daily business of our company. We look at the trends, from which we extrapolate how the near future of our company may look. This takes place by the analysis of data and information from different sources, for example, **industry reports and interviews with experts**."* Fastest route: internal trend reports and freely available market analyses.
  - **How does strategic visioning work?** *"We choose a **positive, constructive, and profitable scenario** and ask ourselves: '**How might our company make a maximum contribution to this scenario? What would we have to do and offer?**' **We stay in the future in our thoughts and don't allow the processes and structures of our company today to influence us.**"*
  - **How does retropolation from tomorrow's world take place?** *"With retropolation, we **draw conclusions for the present from the 'well-known' facts of the future scenario**. We juxtapose the results from the two other activities, combine them, and infer what it means, in very specific terms, for the alignment and direction of our company today. **In which directions should we innovate and do research? What skills need to be built?**"*
  - **"Pictures of the future"** can be used for both extrapolation and, especially, retropolation.

## 8. ME310 at Stanford University (p. 269)

***I would like…*** *to have a complex problem for my company solved by a group of international students.*

- **What it is.** *"An international team of students takes on a design challenge from a corporate partner over a period of **9 months**. ## 13. "Design thinking life" (DTL) (p. 289)

***I would like…*** *to use the design thinking mindset to strengthen my self-efficacy and to redesign my life.*

- **What it is.** *"'Design thinking life' (DTL) runs through all phases of the design thinking process. **Additional components consist of the phase of accepting facts and a comprehensive self-reflection, which is the basis of self-efficacy.**"* It *"adapts this mindset, together with strategies from **coaching and systemic psychotherapy**, to the shaping of one's life. **When you are able to recognize solvable problems and experiment with ideas, you will find out more quickly what makes you happy. - **Group size** **1–5**; *"Larger groups have the advantage that the participants can support one another. **Often, the life concepts are mutually inspiring.**"* · **Duration** **3 days – 2 months**; *"Usually **6–8 weeks** are needed for a cycle."* · **Materials** Post-its, pens, markers, large sheet of paper, **inspiring rooms**.

  1. **Start by exploring personal values, thinking preferences, environmental factors, and identified flow activities.**
  2. **Draw up hypotheses about a personal career plan.**
  3. **Design different paths and possibilities.**
  4. **Test and explore the possibilities.**
  5. **Evaluate the options** and reflect on whether they are consistent with your ideas and possible consequences.
  6. **Plan the implementation in small steps.**
  7. **Reflect regularly** on whether the current tasks still satisfy you; otherwise, optimize your life plan or define new options.
- **Expert (Michael Lewrick, Switzerland)**: *"Companies are surprised that the transformation into an agile enterprise **does not happen with top-down-driven change management programs**.   - **Self-efficacy is the key strength of the future.** DTL is *"a targeted starting point to strengthen the capacity of employees to act,"* and has proven very valuable **for major transitions at companies**.
  - **For HR managers**, DTL is *"an ideal strategy and technique that aims at **strengthening the skills and talents of employees instead of matching people with existing patterns and job descriptions**."*
  - **Life is a constant process of change** — a development tool from pupils choosing a course of studies to students choosing the next career stage.
  - ***"In particularly difficult business or private situations of a participant, it is important that the moderator can deal well with emotional outbursts or is prepared for them."***
  - ***"Everybody should initiate small changes on his/her own to achieve great things."***
- **Key learnings from the use case:** *"Design thinking aims to solve complex problems with creative ease — and where are there more complex questions than in our lives? ## Mental Models

- **Feedback is a gift.** The receiver's job is to say thank you, not to defend. Starting a discussion "would change the mood, and the positive attitude is lost."
- **Positive framing is a technique, not politeness.** "I like / I wish" exists to make criticism sayable in sensitive projects and across hierarchies without becoming personal.
- **Retrospect on causes, not symptoms.** "Get to the bottom of things, that is, identify causes, so that more than just the symptoms are tackled."
- **Reflect at two levels.** Project level ("what went well?") *and* meta level ("what does design thinking mean to me, and where else could it be used?"). The meta level is what makes reflection transferable.
- **Wait out a bad mood.** A lessons learned session held in the wrong emotional weather produces heat, not learning — six weeks' distance is the book's own remedy.
- **Riskiest assumption first.** The lean canvas is finished not when the boxes are full but when the highest-risk box has been tested.
- **Extrapolate *and* retropolate.** Trends give you the near future of today's business; a chosen future scenario, reasoned backwards, gives you which capabilities to build now.
- **Failed initiatives stay on the board.** Discontinued projects remain in the funnel so their causes can be examined — the portfolio-level version of the failure mindset.
- **The implementation question is a "who" question.** If you cannot yet name everyone involved, "it usually indicates that our goal is not really clear yet."
- **Scaling the mindset is scaling self-efficacy.** Kickbox, Young Innovators and design thinking life all work by giving individuals resources, freedom and permission to fail rather than by mandating a process.
- **Teams of teams beats hierarchy.** Network thinking disseminates results quickly; hierarchy filters information consciously.

## Anti-patterns

- **Discussing feedback as the recipient** — it changes the mood and forfeits the tool's whole purpose.
- **Ad hominem criticism** — the thing IL/IW/IW is structurally designed to prevent.
- **Forgetting to document the feedback.** Lilly's standing reminder: "It's great that we all give feedback in a positive form. But don't forget to document it!"
- **Skipping the retrospective because the team knows each other well** — "That's a pity."
- **The blame game** in a retro; starting with the negatives instead of the positives.
- **A retro moderated by a deeply involved team member** at a major milestone — use an external moderator.
- **Tackling symptoms** in a retro instead of digging for causes.
- **Holding lessons learned only at the very end** of a long project, and only on the negatives.
- **Getting personal** in lessons learned; defining too many actions ("less is more").
- **A pitch of text-heavy PowerPoint slides** — "Standard is boring!" and "the audience can listen or read but not both."
- **Unpractised pitches** — the shorter the pitch, the more preparation it needs; there is no chance to catch up later.
- **A lean canvas field with too many Post-its** ("lack of focus") or an empty one ("should be worked on further").
- **Using the business model canvas first** — it is more inside-out and less MVP-oriented than the lean canvas.
- **Filtering ideas through gates** as in a classical innovation funnel, rather than starting in problem identification mode.
- **Letting today's processes and structures constrain the future scenario** during strategic visioning.
- **Deleting discontinued initiatives from the funnel** — you lose the ability to examine why.
- **Building a road map without naming the implementers** — you may need to win them over later.
- **Endless deliberation instead of starting** — the kickbox's "No blah blah blah, just do."
- **Assuming adults know what a young target group wants** — the Young Innovators case exists precisely to refute that.
- **Underestimating the visual design of a method set** — it is what makes the methods attractive enough to be used.

**The growth & scale stages**

| Stage | What is being proven | Tool that marks it |
|---|---|---|
| Problem identification | That the problem is real (via extrapolation + retropolation) | Problem statement, trend analysis, vision cone |
| Problem/solution fit | That the solution fits the problem | Final prototype; **lean canvas** |
| Test & validate | That users accept it | Solution interview, MVP |
| Product/market fit | That a market wants it | MVP metrics, A/B testing |
| Business ecosystem design | That the actors can create value together | **MVE (minimum viable ecosystem)** |
| Scale | That growth is fundable and repeatable | Innovation funnel, road map for implementation, GoldBox |

## Worked Example

**IL/IW/IW, Lilly's team (p. 242).** "Lilly and her teams have practiced a positive feedback culture right from the onset. In every interaction, feedback in the form of 'I like, I wish' is provided. **In the beginning the team often fell back into old patterns, but in the meantime everyone has internalized the principle.** Their template also has columns for 'I wonder' and 'What if…' that are used as the situation requires it. Especially when documenting the feedback, this can still yield significant stimuli."
The comic captures a live round on the robot prototype: *"It's great that we all give feedback in a positive form. But **don't forget to document it!**"* — *"What would happen if the robot were even more like a human…"* — *"This is an important point. **Some users wish the robot to have larger eyes.**"*
Key learnings: *"Practice a positive feedback culture… It is important to **write down the feedback and reflect on it later** with the design thinking team. **Feedback is a gift — always say thank you.**"*

**Retrospective sailboat, Lilly's teams (p. 246).** "After each iteration, Lilly's teams **sit down for a moment** and look back on what went well and what wasn't so great. It's important to Lilly that **the chemistry on the team is right** and that new things are tested again and again. In every iteration, she tries out new techniques and methods, **accepting the possibility that not all of them work as desired. If something doesn't work out, the team has learned to laugh about themselves.**"
Key learnings: *"Conduct a short retrospective in each iteration as well. **Everyone writes down something; at a minimum, something he or she wants to try out.** Make sure that the mood is positive and that there is trust. Remember, it's about continuous improvement."*

**Create a pitch, Lilly's team (p. 250).** "To Lilly, presenting the idea well has top priority. She knows that the listeners **do not want any endless PowerPoint presentations**. Her team normally begins with **a little role-playing game that makes the problem clear and tangible** to the listeners. The team plans the presentation story, moving from the rough outline to the details. Whenever possible, Lilly wants to integrate into the pitch **the prototype and the feedback from the users**. **The conclusion is as important as the entry point.** The team plans the entry point and conclusion very carefully."
In the comic: *"For a convincing pitch, we need a good entry point…"* — *"…and the problem should be explained, for example, in a role-playing game."* — *"…it's best to start with answering the 'Why?' question!"* — *"**Sinek! Always start with WHY!**"*
Key learnings: *"Test the pitch deck and get feedback. Standard is boring! Tell a story based on a persona or show a prototype. **Show the enthusiasm of the customers/users in the interaction with the solution. The team is crucial here. Enthusiasm and commitment are always a winner.** Repeat the important key messages at the end."*

**Lean canvas, Lilly's team (p. 254).** "Lilly's team uses the lean canvas to **document the problem/solution fit and prepare the pitch**. The lean canvas **shows contradictions and open items very well**. After Lilly's team has completed the canvas, they think about **where the greatest uncertainties and risks lie. Once identified, the team tests them first** because they want to make effective and efficient use of their time. Lilly's team still sees **the greatest uncertainty in the value proposition** and would like to test it again next."
Key learnings: *"It is vital to solve a problem that is relevant to the customer with the product idea. To find the right solution for the right problem and for the right customer is a challenge. **We will hardly discover it the first time around. Therefore: iterate — iterate — iterate!** As soon as the first lean canvas is done, the assumptions can be tested. **Begin with the riskiest one.**"*

**Lessons learned, Lilly's teams (p. 258).** "Lilly's teams reflect on their projects **at regular intervals and at pivotal milestones**. The lessons learned **at the meta level are consolidated with the learning of the other teams**, so that **the design principles can be adjusted**, for instance, or collaboration be improved. The questions on the technical level and in terms of content, at the emotional/social and process-related level, however, **often vary greatly per team and project objective**."
In the comic: *"Let us begin with our project! **What are the tools we applied last week? And were they the right ones…?**"* — *"We should do more iterations with the respective critical experiences in the future…."* — and the meta-level result: ***"One clear-cut learning at the meta level is to carry out market analyses alongside design thinking…."***
Key learnings: *"Use lessons learned on multiple levels… **Hold reflection sessions during the project with the help of a log book** at project level. Include the meta level at the end. **Prepare some key questions** for the reflection session."*

**Road map for implementation, Lilly's core team (p. 262).** "The road map for implementation is created **jointly by Lilly's core team**. Since usually not all people involved are identified from the beginning, they can be brought in at a future time with later adjustments. In addition to the tasks, **the people implementing the solution must be named. Once the road map is complete, it is a good indicator of the project's level of maturity.** Lilly thinks about the implementation **at an early stage**. Only in this way is she able to involve the relevant stakeholders."
In the comic: *"How will we implement this? What is important? **What are the next steps?**"* — *"Slowly, the picture becomes clearer. It will be really good. Don't you agree?"* — *"I'll just finish drawing the target group."*
Key learnings — the step-by-step procedure: *"**Define goals and criteria. Select tasks and methods. Assign people. Adjust road map after each step and each iteration if necessary.**"*

**Innovation funnel, Lilly (p. 266).** "Lilly wants to use a simple tool in order to **depict the initiatives that were worked out over time in a funnel**. The 'problem to growth & scale innovation funnel' offers her the advantage that she can **stay within the methodology of lean start-up and design thinking**. In addition, she quickly realizes **which initiatives were discontinued at what time** and how many growth topics and their market potential are in development."
In the comic: *"With this tool, we track the growth initiatives **from problem identification up to a scalable solution**…"* — *"**Red means > 100 million**"* — with the funnel columns labelled *Funnel · High-level · Strategic · Growth* and the validation band reading *Market validate hypothesis (problem/solution fit)*.
Key learnings: *"**Always think in problems and not in ideas** when creating a funnel. Take **both the problem/solution fit and the problem/market fit** into account. Sub-divide the funnel into **existing business fields and future fields of activity.** For example, use **'pictures of the future'** to explore the topics of the future."*

**ME310, the Design Loft (p. 272).** "For many students, the **ME310 Design Loft at Stanford University** is the place where, for a year, problem statements are understood and ideation and prototyping take place. **The course has been in existence since 1967.** One of the highlights of every program year is the **'paper bike' challenge**. This exercise boosts creativity and finding a solution with students. **The basic idea is to build a vehicle with a limited range of materials that remains functional in changing game conditions.**"
In the comic: *"The design loft at Stanford University is empty today. The students are at the paper bike challenge!"* — *"I get the ball first!"* — *"…back at the loft, the focus is on prototyping & testing."*
Key learnings: *"Use design thinking teams at universities and technical colleges for the solution of complex problem statements. What you get are **simple, high-definition prototypes that help accelerate the product or service development cycle**. **The collaboration with students should also be considered a recruiting tool.**"*

**Co-creation toolbox in use at Siemens (p. 276).** "When preparing a project, the teams at Siemens use the toolbox **to select the relevant methods for the project. The heading and color on the front of each method card help in integrating them in the design thinking process.** A brief description explains the method (what) and what it is used for (why). At the back of the card, the approach is explained step by step, and **an application example is given.**"

**Kickbox in use (p. 280).** "Kickboxes of **any size** can be used in companies; and they work just as well **at universities and for NGOs**. The **decentralized, bottom-up ideation with the RedBox**, in which all employees of a company or an organization can participate, makes this program so powerful. **It inspires the collective spirit of innovation in a company and transforms the corporate culture clear across departments and hierarchies.**"
Key learnings: *"With the kickbox, **a mixture of cultural transformation and business innovation** can emerge. Each and every employee can start their own kickbox project. A lean start-up approach is followed systematically: **begin small, test early, iterate fast!** Many of the resources in the kickbox originate from The Design Thinking Toolbox."* And: *"Supported by professional marketing, the kickbox has significance beyond the boundaries of the company."*

**Digital transformation, Lilly's team (p. 284).** "The cornerstones of digital transformation are communicated. **The evaluation of strategic options takes place in iterations with the building of MVPs and MVEs.** In addition, a change takes place in the minds of management and employees. **It is allowed to fail and act on the basis of self-efficacy.** The rooms offer multiple ways for ad-hoc collaboration and starting experiments together. Lilly's team also helped in **setting up a lab in which co-creation takes place and proofs of concept are implemented.**"
Key learnings: *"Set clear goals… Allow enough time for the procurement of information and analysis. **Be active, start and work iteratively.**"*

**Young Innovators — the Snapchat case (p. 288).** The book's sharpest application anecdote, verbatim:
> *"When adults develop ideas and services for the young target group, **they almost always get it wrong**. A humanitarian organization asked the Superloop Young Innovators **if they should use Snapchat. After all, it's the cool thing to do.** The Young Innovators quickly learned the **opinions and social media behavior of 500 young people, rejected the NGO's thesis**, and provided them, **in full self-organization**, with completely different results than expected. **Between us: No, Snapchat is not always cool.**"

In the comic, the young testers give the kind of feedback the client did not expect: *"The persona is 19 and spends 2 hours a day gaming."* — *"**Why do you have to print it? It should be done with one click. Even better if the process is completely automatic.**"*
Key learnings: *"Use the Young Innovators network to harness the creativity of young people and integrate their skills across the entire design thinking cycle. Building your own community is time-consuming **but it's worth the investment**. The community can also be used as **a recruiting tool** to identify innovative minds and to position yourself as an attractive employer."*

**Design thinking life (p. 292).** "Use the 'design thinking life' mindset to **make your life the way you always wanted it to be**. Get to the bottom of things through self-reflection. **Learn to distinguish between solvable problems and facts.** Try to initiate small changes to make a big difference. Look to the future by writing the script of life itself through self-efficacy or adapting it to the new circumstances."
In the comic: *"My life plan! **Small changes can make a big difference!**"* — *"What possibilities are there to test the life plan?"*

## Key Takeaways

- Reflection is a phase, not an afterthought: IL/IW/IW after every interaction, a short retro every iteration, lessons learned at milestones and at the end.
- The receiver of feedback says thank you and does not argue. Written feedback beats spoken feedback because it survives the session.
- Retro anatomy: wind (accelerators), anchor (inhibitors), cliffs (uncontrollable risks), island (shared vision) — then causes, then **measures**, then a positive close.
- Reflect at both project *and* meta level; the meta level is where design principles get adjusted and where the practice itself improves.
- If the mood is bad, postpone the lessons learned session — six weeks is the book's number.
- Pitch: 7 blocks, ≤10 slides if any, a story to open, key figures over words, prototype over slides, repeat 2–3 key messages at the end, and practise.
- Lean canvas before business model canvas; iterate steps 1–5 until the problem/solution fit stabilizes; 3–4 Post-its per field; colour-code by risk; test the riskiest assumption first.
- The road map for implementation starts with "who" — if you cannot name everyone, the goal is not clear yet. Use desirability/feasibility/viability as the standard perspectives.
- The innovation funnel starts in problem identification mode, not idea filtering; keep discontinued initiatives on the board; split existing (A) vs. future (B) business; extrapolate *and* retropolate.
- Scaling design thinking works through programs that grant resources, autonomy and permission to fail: ME310 (9 months, teams of teams), the Siemens 65-card co-creation toolbox, Swisscom's Red/Blue/Gold kickbox, the five-step digital transformation road map, Young Innovators, and design thinking life.
- The book's last instruction: **do it, don't talk about it — and adapt every tool to the situation in front of you.**
