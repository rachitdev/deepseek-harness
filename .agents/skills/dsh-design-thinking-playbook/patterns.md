# Patterns — The Design Thinking Playbook

The book's reusable moves, each as **When / How / Trade-offs**, grouped under its three parts.
Chapter references point at the file that develops the move in full.

---

## Part 1 — Running the cycle (Ch 1–10)

### Design the deliverable like a product (Ch 1)
- **When** — any output with an audience: a product, a service, a workshop, a book, a memo.
- **How** — determine customer needs, create personas, build empathy, and only then produce. The authors ran a design thinking cycle on their own readers before writing.
- **Trade-offs** — front-loads work before anything visible exists; pays back by killing rewrites that come from designing for an imagined reader.

### Dig one level deeper on the persona (Ch 1)
- **When** — the persona reads as a demographic bracket rather than a person.
- **How** — add way of thinking, social environment, milieu, hobbies, stories, photos; draw it life-size; then validate by interviewing someone who knows such a person.
- **Trade-offs** — costs interview time. Skipping it produces persona twins: the same attribute list fits Prince Charles and Ozzy Osbourne.

### Reframe with jobs-to-be-done (Ch 1)
- **When** — the brief is stated as a target ("increase sales 15%") or as attribute tinkering.
- **How** — ask why the customer buys and what they would buy instead. Write "When ___, I want to ___, so I can ___" for each situation; expect one product to serve opposite jobs.
- **Trade-offs** — may split one product line into two specs; incremental improvements get abandoned.

### Calibrate the challenge on the WHY/HOW ladder (Ch 3)
- **When** — you must set the scope of a design challenge.
- **How** — ask WHY repeatedly to widen (a can opener becomes world hunger), HOW to narrow (a rotating mechanism). Pick the rung that leaves the comfort zone yet still admits a buildable prototype.
- **Trade-offs** — too high and no prototype is possible; too low and you have already assumed the answer. When unsure, start anyway — iteration one corrects the frame.

### Classify the problem before choosing the process (Ch 3, 18)
- **When** — at kick-off, before planning any method.
- **How** — decide whether the problem is well-defined (one solution), ill-defined (many — the normal case), or wicked (the problem is the search for the problem). Wicked problems need "How might we…?" and the why technique; linear analysis will not converge.
- **Trade-offs** — labelling a problem wicked licenses long divergence; get it wrong and you burn budget on a task that had one right answer.

### Trade radicalism for speed in the brief, deliberately (Ch 3)
- **When** — writing a design brief for a team or a sponsor.
- **How** — treat the challenge description as a minimum requirement; every added detail expedites problem solving and shrinks the freedom for a radical solution. Choose the point on that spectrum on purpose and say so.
- **Trade-offs** — the exact trade being made; the failure is making it accidentally and then being surprised the output is incremental.

### Surface and rank critical assumptions (Ch 4)
- **When** — an idea exists and a prototype has been sketched, before any field work.
- **How** — one sentence for the idea, one Post-it per need assumption (tagging need / trigger / blocker), then plot on *incidental ↔ decisive* against *knowing ↔ ignorant*. Review decisive/ignorant first.
- **Trade-offs** — about 30 minutes, best spent with someone who asks tricky questions; the payoff is killing a castle in the air early.

### Integrate needfinding into ordinary life (Ch 4)
- **When** — "we have no time for needfinding."
- **How** — list where and when the target group crosses your own week (shopping, the train, the bus stop), decide in advance the question you would open a stranger with, and build a question map you keep enriching.
- **Trade-offs** — encounters are unsystematic and unrepresentative; they are still evidence rather than assumption, and they cost no day off.

### Run the interview to the very end (Ch 4)
- **When** — any needfinding or test interview.
- **How** — introduction, open beginning, a concrete recent example, a grand tour hunting contradictions, reflection where you summarize from your point of view, then wrap-up — and do not turn off the recorder, because the best material arrives last.
- **Trade-offs** — long interviews; evaluate answers only at the end to avoid filtering as you go.

### Take the apprentice / alien stance (Ch 5)
- **When** — you are the expert and the user is not opening up.
- **How** — behave as an apprentice in a world you have never visited; ask the naive question anyway. The role is unthreatening, so respondents explain more.
- **Trade-offs** — costs status in the room. The pollo effect — fear of looking foolish — is what keeps the important question unasked.

### Introduce the talking stick (Ch 5)
- **When** — meetings where people talk over each other or do not feel understood.
- **How** — one holder speaks until they feel understood; others listen or ask comprehension questions only.
- **Trade-offs** — demonstrably more meeting time until the practice settles; then better listening, more willingness to compromise, and everyone gets to finish.

### Synthesize with a 360° view (Ch 6)
- **When** — the solution must serve many groups and the findings are unweighted.
- **How** — list stakeholders (including noncustomers) and non-stakeholder perspectives — time, money, another planet, another culture, another era — state the *intention* behind each, and ask each one its own question.
- **Trade-offs** — the number of perspectives is infinite; you must stop somewhere. Impossible perspectives are legitimate when chosen for what they yield.

### Ask the reverse question, anonymously (Ch 6)
- **When** — you need the constraints people will not volunteer.
- **How** — ask what the worst version would look like, and collect the answers anonymously in writing or online.
- **Trade-offs** — anonymity is unnecessary for positives and mandatory for negatives; a friendly non-anonymous audience returns only what it thinks you want.

### Present findings as a daisy map (Ch 6)
- **When** — handing insights to a group that will pick from them.
- **How** — draw five to eight petals of equal weight instead of a ranked list.
- **Trade-offs** — no ranking means more discussion; a list would have let position decide the winner.

### Warm up until they laugh, and suppress hierarchy (Ch 7)
- **When** — opening any ideation session.
- **How** — a warmup that makes participants smile at one another; state explicitly that every role can contribute; skip the role introductions; if hierarchy is steep, run an all-trainee team.
- **Trade-offs** — feels unserious to some sponsors. Mood is the precondition, not the decoration.

### Brain-dump before diverging (Ch 7)
- **When** — participants arrive with fixed solutions.
- **How** — give a session purely to unloading existing ideas before searching for new ones.
- **Trade-offs** — spends time producing nothing new; without it, those people generate nothing new all day.

### Police the requirements/ideas line (Ch 7)
- **When** — the wall fills with "ergonomic," "safely," "cheaper."
- **How** — name them as requirements, not solutions, and re-ask the question. If a group is stuck at the question level, make translating into a physical model mandatory.
- **Trade-offs** — feels like criticism during a no-criticism phase; do it about the category, not the person.

### Size the solution space with the brainstorming question (Ch 7)
- **When** — before and during ideation.
- **How** — compare "what could we lay across the ditch?" with "how can one overcome a physical barrier?" and pick the formulation matching the space you want. Adapt it live if yield is wrong.
- **Trade-offs** — a wide question can produce unusable spread; a narrow one caps the count at the outset.

### Run the halftime idea count (Ch 7)
- **When** — mid-ideation, across parallel groups.
- **How** — stop and ask each group for its number.
- **Trade-offs** — competitive pressure pushes laggards to wilder ideas *and* diagnoses the stuck group, which has almost always started evaluating against instructions.

### Cluster to align, not to sort (Ch 8)
- **When** — the wall is full and interpretations differ.
- **How** — argue about which cluster each Post-it belongs in; schemes include matches-the-question, exciting, out of scope, today/tomorrow/future, B2B/B2C/B2B2C, incremental vs. radical.
- **Trade-offs** — slow, and the classification itself is disposable; the shared reading it produces is the deliverable.

### Select in two tiers: dissemination, then implementability (Ch 8)
- **When** — choosing among clusters inside a political organization.
- **How** — first plot speed of dissemination against speed of adaptation, factoring in decision makers and your own influence; then plot survivors on implementability against financial feasibility.
- **Trade-offs** — treats political feasibility as a design dimension, which can suppress a technically superior idea; the second pass yields implementation alternatives rather than one winner.

### Run a submarine project (Ch 8)
- **When** — real corporate selection criteria would kill a good idea before it can be shown.
- **How** — a few dedicated people build quietly; surface only when prototype results can win decision makers over.
- **Trade-offs** — political risk and no official resources until it surfaces. The alternative is arguing against criteria with nothing to show.

### Fix critical functionalities early (Ch 2)
- **When** — the start of a macro cycle, long before convergence.
- **How** — spend one to two hours drafting and ranking 10–20 critical functions in the context of a critical user experience; at the groan zone, match the idea mass against that ranking.
- **Trade-offs** — an early ranking may be wrong; without it, convergence has no objective filter and the loudest advocate decides.

### Escalate creative risk through the macro cycle (Ch 2)
- **When** — a team cannot tear itself away from its original concept.
- **How** — brainstorm → critical functions → cross-industry benchmarks → dark horse (remove something constitutive) → funky prototype (remove all budget limits) → vision prototype → concretize.
- **Trade-offs** — deliberately wastes effort on prototypes destined to be discarded; that waste is what buys distance from the first idea.

### Outsource the vote (Ch 2)
- **When** — the group is about to select from its own ideas.
- **How** — present survivors to other groups and let that community allocate the dots.
- **Trade-offs** — costs a round of exposure and explanation; internal voting is not objective because teams fall in love with their own ideas.

### Build the prototype to answer one question (Ch 9)
- **When** — every prototype, always.
- **How** — name what is to be tested first; build only good enough to make the basic features comprehensible; time-box it; use whatever material is at hand.
- **Trade-offs** — a low-resolution prototype yields less accurate tests. Match maturity to the objective, not to ambition — and never fall in love with it.

### Box it and shelf it (Ch 9)
- **When** — an offer or a whole portfolio needs to become discussable.
- **How** — boxing: design the physical box, front promises and back details, remaining sides answering who / which goals / when / where / why. Shelfing: sort everything onto product, service and solution shelves under the categories customers would search.
- **Trade-offs** — forces the team to construct its own understanding; the shelves expose portfolio gaps and synergies you may not want on the wall.

### Test without explaining (Ch 10)
- **When** — putting a prototype in front of a user.
- **How** — do not describe how it works; observe use *and misuse* without correcting; answer questions with questions ("what do you think this button is for?"); ask why even when you know.
- **Trade-offs** — users may struggle visibly and the session feels like a failure; every early explanation replaces their interpretation with yours.

### Test several variants, in context, in pairs (Ch 10)
- **When** — any qualitative test round.
- **How** — prepare two or three described scenarios so feedback is comparative; go to the user's real environment; split moderator, actor and observer; film if only one observer is available; pilot with one person first.
- **Trade-offs** — more build effort and more people per session; a single solution returns vague feedback.

### Run the experiment grid (Ch 10, 19)
- **When** — an assumption is expensive enough to test formally.
- **How** — hypothesis ("we believe that…"), test ("to verify this we will…"), metric ("and measure…"), criterion ("we are on the right track if…"), carry out with documentation, then learnings.
- **Trade-offs** — formality slows early exploration; it records why each decision was made and gives evidence for investors. Ask how to run it in half the time with half the resources.

## Part 2 — Building the conditions (Ch 11–17)

### Prototype the room (Ch 11)
- **When** — no creative space exists and no budget has been approved.
- **How** — empty a borrowed room, add one or two chairs more than participants, put the tables free-standing and organic, distribute chairs rather than arranging them, keep walls clear, then iterate after each use with "what worked / how do we want to work?"
- **Trade-offs** — a cheap first version invites scepticism from stylish-office cultures; begin with a low profile and watch the reaction before pushing further.

### Co-design the environment with the people in it (Ch 11)
- **When** — a space, or a work environment, is being designed *for* a team.
- **How** — design place, people, process and meaningfulness together with the team as part of team development.
- **Trade-offs** — slower than commissioning a design; employees do not feel comfortable in stylish rooms whose brief never included them.

### Compose T-shaped people on a U-shaped team (Ch 12)
- **When** — assembling any project team.
- **How** — hire for depth plus empathy and interface expertise; have candidates draw their own T profile and present it; use a boot camp so both sides can judge fit; mix hierarchy levels; develop T toward Pi; ensure one strong member per thinking-preference quadrant.
- **Trade-offs** — I profiles are easier to find and collaboration complexity rises; without shared ownership you get a multidisciplinary compromise.

### Swap thinking preferences deliberately (Ch 12)
- **When** — the team keeps producing solutions in one style.
- **How** — assign phase tasks to the people whose preference fits, and rotate; visualize preferences (e.g. HBDI) so the team can see the map.
- **Trade-offs** — people work outside their comfort zone; it also improves how the team communicates concepts to decision makers.

### Think with the pen (Ch 13)
- **When** — anything abstract needs to be shared quickly.
- **How** — text, graphic elements, icons and symbols, figures and emotions, colour — schematic rather than photorealistic, writing first and enclosing after, only a few colours, print letters at least 3–3.5 cm on a flip chart.
- **Trade-offs** — it will not be beautiful. Visualization is a language to practise, not a talent; the point is transport of content.

### Build the arc, and start with WHY (Ch 14)
- **When** — pitching an idea, a product or a transformation.
- **How** — emotionally significant opening, a likable main character, conflicts, visible before/after change, climax with the moral. Communicate from WHY (purpose) through HOW to WHAT.
- **Trade-offs** — narrative time competes with evidence time; flesh the story out with data and facts rather than replacing them.

### Personify the object to reach unspoken needs (Ch 14)
- **When** — direct surveys return only the obvious, idealized answers.
- **How** — the Minsky suitcase, or the "wearable dreams" protocol: give the object a name, age and personality, put that person in trouble, set the scene, and have respondents write the short story without self-censoring.
- **Trade-offs** — output is fiction, not data; it reaches the emotional and social layer that a questionnaire cannot.

### Extrapolate, then retropolate (Ch 14, 17)
- **When** — planning beyond the range of a roadmap.
- **How** — step 1, extrapolate today's trends from industry reports, noting importance, impact and maturity; step 2, design several distant scenarios outside-in, refusing to let today's structures constrain them; step 3, juxtapose the two and infer what to research, build, hire and redesign now.
- **Trade-offs** — step 2 is uncomfortable and easy to sabotage from inside the business; publish scenarios at intervals or the organization falls back into routine.

### Clarify purpose before choosing a method (Ch 15)
- **When** — someone asks for "a workshop."
- **How** — establish what is to be achieved, how and why; run the intent checklist (awareness? problem solutions? relationships? knowledge exchange? vision? conflict? decisions?); then plan process, participants, environment and information.
- **Trade-offs** — clarification takes a conversation the sponsor did not budget for; the more clear-cut the specification, the more successful the implementation.

### Compose the room with ARE IN (Ch 15)
- **When** — a decision must stick after the workshop.
- **How** — ensure participants cover Authority, Resources, Expertise, Information (including the informal kind) and Need.
- **Trade-offs** — a bigger, more senior room is harder to facilitate; missing any one role leaves the dialog structurally unable to reach a sustainable decision.

### Hold the group through emergence (Ch 15)
- **When** — the room feels dysfunctional mid-ideation.
- **How** — name it as the normal period of emergence, keep contributions in exchange rather than monologue, focus on interests instead of positions, and let the group discuss the topic it believes it cannot discuss.
- **Trade-offs** — the facilitator absorbs the discomfort and stays neutral on content; deciding early to relieve the tension is what kills decisions.

### Be resource-oriented, not deficit-oriented (Ch 15)
- **When** — improving something that already works.
- **How** — appreciative inquiry: examine what runs perfectly and build from strengths, on the assumption that expertise already exists in the company.
- **Trade-offs** — inverts the standard consultancy posture; the largest optimization potential hides in processes nobody questions.

### Build a network, not a departmental initiative (Ch 16)
- **When** — spreading design thinking past the first successful project.
- **How** — establish a company-wide network of users and supporters so the approach travels transversally, while still securing decision-maker buy-in; mix hierarchy levels in teams.
- **Trade-offs** — slower than mandating it from one department, which has no lasting effect. Process improvement and operational excellence make silos *more* efficient, not more connected.

### Close the measurement loop (Ch 16)
- **When** — customer centricity or mindset maturity needs to change, not just be known.
- **How** — measure across a representative cross-section, reflect on causes and translate them into written strategies *with* employees, draw up an action plan, monitor progress, re-measure.
- **Trade-offs** — a thin sample gives a patchy score, and knowing the score changes nothing. The dedication that develops from the measurement matters more than the number.

### Understand the past before shaping the future (Ch 17)
- **When** — a foresight or strategy exercise starts by jumping to solutions.
- **How** — run the Perspective stage first: what has happened up to today, and why the surviving options are rated as they are.
- **Trade-offs** — teams experience it as delay; it is what makes future options intelligible instead of arbitrary.

### Combine trends into new ideas (Ch 17)
- **When** — hunting market opportunities rather than fixing a known problem.
- **How** — lay basic technologies, accelerators and future scenarios on a progression curve and combine across the bands; watch weak signals before they become macro trends.
- **Trade-offs** — trend lists date quickly, and futurology's habit of extrapolating past data is exactly what this is not; the discipline is a positive, shapeable vision, not a forecast.

## Part 3 — Designing the future (Ch 18–19)

### Model with feedback loops, and stabilize them (Ch 18)
- **When** — the situation resists a cause/effect explanation.
- **How** — draw A→B→C→A rather than a chain, so the model shows how and why something happens; stabilize each loop by using feedback to close the gap between actual and desired level.
- **Trade-offs** — harder to communicate than a linear story; unstabilized loops amplify in either direction.

### Delimit the system to get your SWOT (Ch 18)
- **When** — beginning any systems analysis.
- **How** — draw the boundary; elements inside are the strengths and weaknesses you can change, elements outside are the opportunities and risks acting on you; list every stakeholder; work from rough to detail.
- **Trade-offs** — the boundary is a choice that determines the answer, so treat the first system picture as a prototype to be tested and improved.

### Think in variants (Ch 18, 9, 19)
- **When** — a solution, a prototype or a business model looks settled.
- **How** — generate several alternatives before evaluating any, then apply the decision criteria formulated from the goals.
- **Trade-offs** — slower and it invites reopening a closed decision; a single option cannot be evaluated, only accepted.

### Switch mindsets when you stagnate (Ch 18)
- **When** — the picture is unclear or progress has stalled.
- **How** — a design thinking practitioner runs the systems problem-solving cycle; a systems practitioner confirms findings once through the design thinking cycle and expands the creative framework.
- **Trade-offs** — costs a full pass in an unfamiliar mode; it is where most of the new insight comes from on wicked problems.

### Fill the lean canvas in order, and check viability (Ch 19)
- **When** — turning design thinking findings into a business model.
- **How** — problem → customer segments → unique value proposition → solution → existing alternatives → channels → unfair advantage → key metrics → revenue → costs → short concept → experiment reports (typical start at 1 or 4), then ask whether you are satisfied with the viability and loop back if not.
- **Trade-offs** — a good business model rarely emerges in a few hours; racing it produces a plan rather than a hypothesis set.

### Enter the canvas by the right door (Ch 19)
- **When** — designing a digital offer.
- **How** — pick the entry point: the problem, the solution (digitize a physical object), a missing digital alternative, an unfair advantage built as an ecosystem, digital channels, or an existing business model not yet present in your market.
- **Trade-offs** — entry via copying is fastest and least defensible; entry via ecosystem is slowest and hardest to copy.

### Reduce risk fit by fit (Ch 19)
- **When** — moving from prototype to business.
- **How** — customer/problem fit through design thinking, problem/solution fit and then product/market fit through lean start-up, each step an experiment that lowers risk and raises project value; use an MVE, not an MVP, when the scenario is an ecosystem.
- **Trade-offs** — the discipline of one uncertainty at a time is slower than building the full offer, and it is what keeps burn low.

### Blueprint the invisible half of a service (Ch 19)
- **When** — designing or repairing a service.
- **How** — lay out customer actions on a timeline, then green touchpoints, blue visible provider activities, invisible activities, orange supporting systems, and finally red for what is critical, error-prone or risky.
- **Trade-offs** — labour-intensive and iterative; the failures hide in exactly the invisible layer a customer journey map omits.

### Validate the value proposition against the ten factors (Ch 19)
- **When** — before committing to a value proposition.
- **How** — check embedding in a business model, importance to most customers, willingness to pay, unresolved problems, few tasks solved extremely well, emotional and social components, the customer's own measure of success, differentiation, superiority in at least one dimension, and difficulty of copying.
- **Trade-offs** — a proposition aimed at many tasks will fail factor 5; the NABC analysis exists to add the competition view the customer profile lacks.
