# Glossary — The Design Thinking Playbook

**360° view** — Deliberate enumeration of stakeholders and non-stakeholder perspectives (time, money, imaginary world, age, culture, geopolitics) before writing a point of view; each perspective gets its own question (Ch 6)
**9-window tool** — Grid of system (subsystem / system / super system) against time (past / today / future), used to see a product from another point of view (Ch 6)
**A/B testing** — Quantitative test of two variants of a simple prototype, changing only one variable, evaluated on predefined criteria; version B shown only to new visitors when run live (Ch 10)
**AEIOU** — Observation framework: Activities, Environment, Interaction, Objects, Users, applied with "what? how? why?" (Ch 1)
**Appreciative inquiry** — Facilitation stance that examines what already works perfectly in a system rather than its deficits (Ch 15)
**ARE IN formula** — The five roles an adequate system for dialog must contain: Authority, Resources, Expertise, Information, Need (Ch 15)
**Art of hosting** — Family of dialog formats in which the facilitator acts as host, ensuring participants feel safe during change (Ch 15)
**Benchmarks from other industries** — Macro-cycle step 3: brainstorm the problem, brainstorm industries and experiences, combine the best three of each into prototypes (Ch 2)
**Black ocean** — Strategy of building an unfair advantage so deep that competitors cannot enter, typically through ecosystem design (Ch 19)
**Bodystorming** — Prototype form in which team members physically act out a specific situation (Ch 9)
**Boxing** — Making an offer tangible by designing its physical box: front promises, back details, remaining sides answering the WH questions (Ch 9)
**Brain dump** — Opening session where participants unload their existing fixed ideas so they become open to new ones (Ch 7)
**Brainstorming rules** — Creative confidence; quantity before quality; no criticism during the phase (Ch 7)
**Buddy check** — Foresight tool for matching the right partners and team members (Ch 17)
**Business model canvas** — Systematic depiction of business contexts and relations, usable as a medium-resolution prototype type (Ch 9, 19)
**Customer Centricity Score (CCScore)** — Measurement of how far customer centricity has spread through an organization, evaluated at different levels of aggregation; low score = "customers are tolerated," high = "customers are in the focus" (Ch 16)
**Change path** — Foresight tool indicating the most important milestones needed to perform a certain action (Ch 17)
**Churchill matrix** — Importance × urgency grid, used for choosing measures rather than products (Ch 8)
**Circle way** — Dialog in a circular arrangement; participants collaborate more actively and take leading roles (Ch 15)
**Circular value creation** — Business model archetype that generates value from a waste product and closes the loop (Ch 19)
**Cluster** — Bundle of related ideas; the argument about which cluster an idea belongs to is what builds shared interpretation (Ch 8)
**Cognitive empathy** — Recognizing what another person feels; enough to describe a persona (Ch 5)
**Communication sheet** — Documentation artifact that makes a selected concept idea tangible and shareable across teams (Ch 8)
**Concept map** — Map that may start from several key concepts and shows cross-connections like a road network; usually needs three restructurings to be good (Ch 8)
**Connect 2 Value** — Lewrick and Link framework connecting knowledge, talent and systems to value, running People → Teams of teams → Ecosystems over Execution (Ch 12)
**Critical assumptions** — The assumptions behind an idea that are both decisive and least known; reviewed first (Ch 4)
**Critical functionalities** — The mandatory user functions identified and ranked in macro-cycle step 2; the objective filter that later kills ideas in the groan zone (Ch 2)
**Crowd clover** — Foresight tool for mapping innovation networks (Ch 17)
**Customer development** — Steve Blank's principle that customers must be developed and won over during the development phase (Ch 19)
**Customer journey** — End-to-end sequence of customer experiences, often prototyped as a storyboard or blueprint (Ch 9, 19)
**Customer/problem fit** — The depth of understanding of customer and problem achieved by design thinking, before lean start-up work begins (Ch 19)
**Daisy map** — Petal diagram showing five to eight equal elements, used instead of a list so the top item does not win by position (Ch 6)
**Dark horse** — Macro-cycle step 4: lift the borders, strive for maximum success and accept maximum risk ("a windshield wiper without a windshield") (Ch 2)
**Design brief** — Structured translation of a problem into a task, covering design space and scope, existing approaches, design principles, scenarios, next steps and implementation challenges (Ch 3)
**Design challenge** — The formulated question a design thinking project answers; ideally open enough to contain no solution in its description (Ch 3, 11)
**Design space / design scope** — The activities to be supported and for whom; the boundary a potential solution must fit (Ch 3)
**Digital storytelling** — Storytelling for channels with no nonverbal feedback: keep it short, keep the narrative linear, show don't tell (Ch 14)
**Divergent / convergent thinking** — The two halves of each diamond: differentiate then integrate, in the problem space and again in the solution space (Ch 2)
**Dot voting** — Placing adhesive dots on ideas; quick and democratic (Ch 8, 6)
**Double diamond** — Problem space then solution space, each with a divergent and a convergent half (Ch 2, 17, 18)
**Dynamic facilitation** — Impulse-driven rather than linear facilitation that appreciates mental leaps, documented on four lists: questions, ideas, concerns, information (Ch 15)
**Early adopter** — The ideal first customer, described with a persona and entered separately from the target segment on the lean canvas (Ch 19)
**Emotional empathy** — Actually feeling what the other person feels; enough to prioritize which experience is critical (Ch 5)
**Empathic design** — Development of products and services based on unspoken customer needs (Ch 14)
**Empathy map** — Canvas covering hearing, thinking and feeling, seeing, speaking and doing, frustration and desire (Ch 1)
**Experiment grid** — Six-step experiment template: hypothesis, test, metrics, criteria, carry out, learnings (Ch 10, 19)
**Experiment reports** — Osterwalder-style paired Experiment N / Learnings N strips that document progress per iteration alongside the lean canvas (Ch 19)
**Extreme user** — A user at the edge of the segment (e.g. seniors doing strenuous sport) whose needs expose what the average hides (Ch 4)
**Fail fast — fail often** — Operating slogan of the test phase: early and frequent failure realizes market opportunities (Ch 10)
**Fake door** — Deliberate fake access to a product that does not yet exist, used to measure demand (Ch 9)
**Feedback-capture grid** — Four quadrants for documenting user feedback: Like, Wishes, Questions, Ideas (Ch 10)
**Foresight Framework** — Five stages of strategic foresight: Perspective, Opportunity, Solution, Team, Vision, each with three tools (Ch 17)
**Frugality** — Business model archetype promoting effective use of resources, e.g. on-demand production with no overproduction (Ch 19)
**Funky prototype** — Macro-cycle step 5: creativity cranked one notch further with all cost and budget restrictions removed (Ch 2)
**Future user** — Method for 10-year horizons: compare today's target customer with today's 22-year-olds at the same age and extrapolate (Ch 1)
**Generational arc** — Foresight tool illustrating demographic transformation across generations (Ch 17)
**Giga map** — "A big messy map of a big messy thing"; holistic but usually only understood by its creators (Ch 8)
**Golden Circle** — Simon Sinek's WHY / HOW / WHAT rings; communicate from the inside out because people buy why you produce something (Ch 14)
**Graphic recording** — Visual documentation of a group's knowledge as it is produced (Ch 13, 15)
**Green and red feedback** — "What I like about the prototype is…" and "I wish that the prototype…" (Ch 9)
**Groan zone** — The transition from divergence to convergence, experienced as unpleasant and often mistaken for dysfunction (Ch 2, 15)
**HBDI (Herrmann Brain Dominance Instrument)** — Whole Brain instrument mapping thinking preferences onto four quadrants; used to compose and diagnose teams (Ch 12)
**Hook canvas** — Nir Eyal's hook model as a canvas: trigger, action, variable reward, investment (Ch 1, 5)
**How might we…?** — Point-of-view question template that opens the ideation phase (Ch 3, 6)
**I profile** — Specialist with depth but no breadth; easier to hire, weaker in interdisciplinary work (Ch 12)
**Ill-defined problem** — Unclear problem with more than one correct remedy; the normal case in design thinking (Ch 3)
**Insight** — Weighted, interpreted finding; step B of the point-of-view procedure condenses the ten most important ones (Ch 6)
**Interdisciplinary team** — Team producing ideas collectively where everybody feels responsible for the overall solution (Ch 12)
**Janus cone** — Foresight tool depicting multiple overlapping and intersecting events in one framework (Ch 17)
**Jobs-to-be-done** — Asking why a customer buys rather than what properties a product should have: "When ___, I want to ___, so I can ___" (Ch 1)
**Killer phrase** — Dismissal such as "That will never work!" issued when decision makers cannot or will not penetrate a solution (Ch 15)
**Lead user** — Eric von Hippel's term for users who have mass-market needs early and build their own interim solutions; source of the mountain bike and the hyperlink (Ch 4)
**Lean canvas** — Maurya's one-page business model with the unique selling proposition at its core, rational reasons on the left and customers, channels and revenue on the right (Ch 19)
**Lean management** — Nine principles and tools (value stream design, CIP, 5S, TPM, kanban, Makigami) added after initial scaling (Ch 19)
**Lean start-up** — Short-iteration mindset continuing design thinking with little capital and validated learning (Ch 19)
**LOVE IT — CHANGE IT — LEAVE IT** — The three verdicts available for any prototype, and the book's stance toward its own advice (Ch 2, 9)
**Macro cycle** — The seven-step arc of a whole innovation project, chaining many micro cycles through escalating creative risk (Ch 2)
**Micro cycle** — Understand, Observe, Define point of view, Ideate, Prototype, Test, Reflect (Ch 2)
**Mind map** — Tree built inside-out from one key concept; brings order but does not show correlations (Ch 8)
**Mindfulness** — Focused, present attention; the substrate empathy grows on, summarized as "full mind or mindful?" (Ch 5)
**Minsky suitcase** — Tool for generating emotional stories from an object and its imagined former owner (Ch 14)
**Mock-up** — Prototype showing the overall impression of a system without necessarily working (Ch 9)
**Morphological box** — Structure for combining partial solutions into variants (Ch 7)
**Multidisciplinary team** — Team of experts each advocating their specialization; the solution is often a compromise (Ch 12)
**Minimum viable ecosystem (MVE)** — Working collaboration on a key functionality between initial ecosystem partners; tests an ecosystem scenario the way an MVP tests a product (Ch 8, 9, 19)
**Minimum viable product (MVP)** — Executable version carrying only the most necessary functions; the functional prototype of macro-cycle step 7A (Ch 2, 9, 19)
**NABC** — Need, Approach, Benefits, Competition; a value proposition tool that unlike the customer profile accounts for competitors (Ch 2, 19)
**Needfinding** — Field discovery of real user needs through observation, interviews and random encounters; cannot be delegated or reconstructed at a desk (Ch 4)
**Needs as verbs** — Rule that a need statement expresses WHAT the user wants to achieve, with the HOW consciously set aside (Ch 4)
**Open space technology** — Format that designs the workshop as free space; recommended for final presentations of design thinking projects (Ch 15)
**Operational concept** — Documented output of a system representation (Ch 18)
**Pathfinder** — Foresight tool showing the best path an idea should take through an organization or innovation network (Ch 17)
**Persona** — Verified, specific description of a user with pains, gains, jobs-to-be-done and use cases; the starting point of the whole method (Ch 1)
**Persona twins** — The thin-persona trap: identical attribute lists describing Prince Charles and Ozzy Osbourne (Ch 1)
**Pi profile** — Adaptive employee with two legs of depth — own specialization plus a second role or leadership (Ch 12)
**Pictures of the Future** — Siemens' three-step method: extrapolate from today, apply strategic visioning outside-in, then retropolate from the future scenario (Ch 14)
**Pinocchio** — Rudimentary, nonfunctioning version of a product; the PalmPilot's original prototype (Ch 9)
**Point of view (PoV)** — The condensed sentence that synthesizes all findings and sets the question for ideation; always a starting point for the next iteration (Ch 6)
**Pollo effect** — The reluctance to ask naive questions for fear of looking foolish; there are no naive questions (Ch 5)
**Pretend to own** — Prototype tactic of renting or leasing what you pretend to own before investing heavily (Ch 9)
**Problem reversal** — Reversing the question ("How would you prevent creativity?") then inverting each negative; good for service processes, not for new product ideas (Ch 7)
**Problem space / solution space** — The first and second halves of the double diamond; in systems thinking, steps 1–4 and 5–7 of the problem-solving cycle (Ch 3, 18)
**Problem/solution fit** — The first fit lean start-up creates, before refining into product/market fit (Ch 19)
**Progression curve** — Foresight tool placing events and life cycles in context, analogous to the S-curve; also the three-band trend map (Ch 17)
**Prototype vision canvas** — Canvas for comparing several visions side by side: vision statement, target group, needs, product, benefit (Ch 2)
**Quick 'n' dirty prototyping** — Building an idea physically as a creativity technique; material diversity determines idea yield (Ch 7)
**Reality distortion field** — Bud Tribble's term for Steve Jobs's ability to exit divergence at exactly the right moment (Ch 2)
**Real theater** — Foresight tool for immersing in tomorrow's world and experiencing user needs in realistic environments (Ch 17)
**Relabel** — Prototype tactic of putting your own brand and packaging on another product (Ch 9)
**Requirements vs. ideas** — The facilitator's core diagnostic: "ergonomic," "safely," "cheaper" are requirements, not solutions (Ch 7)
**Resolution (degree of)** — The maturity axis organizing the prototype catalogue: low (early phase), medium (first solution approaches), high (end solutions) (Ch 9)
**Retrospective board** — Reflect-phase artifact; the retrospective is where personas get updated (Ch 2)
**Reverse point of view** — Asking what the worst version would be, to surface constraints positive questions never reach (Ch 6)
**SCAMPER** — Substitute, Combine, Adapt, Modify, Put to other uses, Eliminate, Rearrange; the full form of the operator matrix (Ch 7, 3)
**Scenario** — A hypothesis about a map of the future written as a captivating story, used to identify risks and opportunities — not a forecast (Ch 14, 17)
**Servitization** — Business model archetype fulfilling needs without the customer owning the physical product, e.g. power by the hour (Ch 19)
**Service blueprint** — Layered map of customer activities, visible and invisible provider activities and supporting systems, separated by the customer interaction line (Ch 19)
**Shelfing** — Sorting a portfolio onto product, service and solution shelves under the categories customers would look for, exposing gaps and synergies (Ch 9)
**Short concept (X-for-Y)** — One-sentence analogy for the value proposition, e.g. "YouTube = Flickr for video" (Ch 19)
**Silo structure** — Specialized teams optimizing their own fitness while transversal collaboration for a consistent customer experience falls by the wayside (Ch 16)
**Stakeholder map** — Map of the parties around a problem; starting point for the 360° view and for implementation planning (Ch 6, 18)
**Storyboard** — End-to-end customer journey as a series of images or sketches (Ch 9, 13)
**Strategic foresight** — Mindset plus methodology for shaping the desired future rather than extrapolating it (Ch 17)
**Submarine project** — Project run quietly by a few dedicated employees, surfacing only once prototype results can win decision makers over (Ch 8)
**Super system / subsystem** — The zoom-out and zoom-in rows of the 9-window tool (Ch 6, 18)
**SWOT** — Strengths and weaknesses inside the system boundary; opportunities and risks outside it (Ch 18)
**Systems map** — Map of actors, stakeholders and elements with their interrelationships and material, energy, money and information flows (Ch 8, 18)
**Systems thinking** — Understanding a whole through its interacting elements and feedback loops rather than cause/effect chains; used synonymously with systems engineering (Ch 18)
**T profile** — Depth of expertise (vertical) plus breadth of empathy and interface expertise (horizontal); Dorothy Leonard-Barton's concept (Ch 12)
**Talking stick** — Speaking token held until the speaker feels understood; costs meeting time before it pays in listening (Ch 5)
**Teams of teams** — Concept for utilizing skills across a larger organization so squads can work on different functionalities in parallel (Ch 12)
**Third-age suit** — Empathy device letting designers experience the physical limitations of seniors in their own body (Ch 14)
**Time boxing** — Creating prototypes under time pressure, because more time does not yield more results (Ch 9, 2)
**Trend (micro / macro / mega)** — Maturity stages of a signal: decentralization of everything as a micro trend, access over ownership as a macro trend, smart cities as a mega trend (Ch 17)
**U procedure** — Method-based reflection turning score results into improvement strategies; a change process in its own right (Ch 16)
**U-shaped team** — Team that returns to equilibrium after disruption and is more stable afterward; you can make a mistake without fearing for your job (Ch 12)
**Unfair advantage** — Something that makes the solution difficult for others to copy, e.g. an entire business ecosystem (Ch 19)
**Unique value proposition** — The simple, clear message explaining why the solution is different and noteworthy; the core of the lean canvas (Ch 19)
**User profile canvas** — Persona canvas with description, jobs-to-be-done, use cases, gains, pains, photos and stories (Ch 1)
**Value excellence** — The successor to operational and experience excellence, achieved through strong customer centricity and proactive collaboration (Ch 19)
**Vision prototype** — Macro-cycle step 6, the groan-zone artifact fusing everything learned; tested with at least three potential users (Ch 2)
**Vision statement** — Clear, concise summary of an idea, used to describe a prototype briefly (Ch 17, 2)
**Visual facilitating** — Visualizing information and dialog in real time on site, transforming complexity into a structured picture (Ch 13, 15)
**Weak signal** — Early, low-confidence indication of change; a foresight input before it becomes a macro trend (Ch 17)
**WH questions** — What, who, why, where, when (and how); the divergent-phase overview tool, also used in boxing (Ch 4, 1, 9)
**White elephant** — A product over-fitted to lead users: high risk, low likelihood of implementation, hard to stop (Ch 4)
**White spot analysis** — Foresight tool giving insight into hidden market opportunities and a wider view of the competitor landscape (Ch 17)
**Whole Brain model** — Four-quadrant model of cerebral/limbic and left/right modes underlying HBDI (Ch 12)
**Wicked problem** — Unknown problem where a partial solution clarifies the problem definition; the problem is the search for the problem (Ch 3, 18)
**Wizard of Oz** — Prototype where the user interacts with an interface whose reactions are simulated by people (Ch 9)
**World café** — Format where participants rotate between tables while table hosts carry the conversation forward (Ch 15)
**WYFIWYG** — "What you foresee is what you get": expectations influence what actually happens (Ch 17)
