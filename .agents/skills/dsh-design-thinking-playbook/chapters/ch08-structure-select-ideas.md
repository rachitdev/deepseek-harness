# Chapter 8 (Book §1.8) — How to Structure and Select Ideas

## Core Idea
Once the mood breaks, "ideas keep cropping up in rapid succession. Often, the screens,
windows, and walls are not big enough to place all the ideas." Then comes **the agony of
choice**, which is hard for two structural reasons:
1. **Each of us interprets the drawings, words, or short texts on the Post-its
   differently.**
2. Some ideas share a basic thought; **others solve a completely different problem than
   the one originally intended.**

The chapter's answer is a four-step arc — **generate → structure → select → refine and
document** — plus eight ways to cluster, two selection matrices, a realpolitik section on
corporate selection criteria, and the map family (concept / mind / systems / giga map).

Its most important line about clustering: **"The way is the goal, and the discussion about
a meaningful classification in itself results in everybody having the same understanding
of the ideas in the end."** Clustering is a shared-understanding device before it is a
sorting device.

## Frameworks Introduced

**The four-step procedure**
1. **Generate ideas.**
2. **Structure the ideas** — bundle into clusters.
3. **Select the ideas** — most important ideas or clusters; select according to the needs
   of the persona or according to actual use.
4. **Refine and document the ideas.**

**Eight clustering schemes (A–H)**

| | Scheme | Use |
|---|---|---|
| A | **Matches the question** | Separates on-brief ideas |
| B | **Exciting** | Keeps novelty visible independent of fit |
| C | **Out of scope** | Parks what solves a different problem |
| D | **Today — Tomorrow — Future** | Time horizon |
| E | **B2B — B2C — B2B2C** | Market model |
| F | **Incremental versus radical** | Degree of innovation (plotted against product/service) |
| G | **Speed of dissemination × implementability** matrix | Two-tier preselection (see below) |
| H | **Churchill matrix: importance × urgency** | Choosing *measures*, not products |

If the range of ideas is very broad and the scope of the question was greatly expanded,
group first into overarching topics and then cluster again.

**The two-tier "speed of dissemination and implementability" selection (G).**
- **Step 1** — focus on the **speed of dissemination** and the **speed of adaptation**.
  Especially in a political environment (which prevails at Peter's company) it is useful
  to consider the decision makers and one's own influence on the potential rollout. *"The
  best ideas are characterized by rapid dissemination and fast adaptation."*
- **Step 2** — investigate **implementability and financial feasibility**. This produces
  implementation alternatives or indications of the functional scope.

**The Churchill matrix (H).** Urgency on the x-axis, importance on the y-axis. Discuss
with the team which measure belongs in which quadrant; once all are allocated, define the
to-dos with responsibilities and milestones. Suitable specifically for deciding **which
measures to use**.

**Communication sheets** for concept ideas — the documentation artifact of step 4.

## Key Concepts
- **Dot voting** — participants place adhesive dots on ideas. "The vote is quick and
  democratic."
- **Selection criteria in large organizations** are real and usually inhibiting — they act
  as guardrails or specify financial goals — "but because they do exist in reality, we
  should discuss them." If the criteria aren't known within a well-defined strategy and
  vision, ask the six key questions (see Reference Tables).
- **Two hard realities when defining criteria.** (a) "The potential market opportunity can
  be as great as it may be, but if the decision makers (e.g. top management) don't cotton
  to it, the idea will fail" — at the latest when financial resources are allocated.
  (b) **Values matter**: "If it goes against the moral grain of a company to use customer
  data from digital channels for other business models or to sell it profitably, such
  ideas will have little success."
- **The upside of early criteria**: "the waste of resources is reduced and effectiveness
  is boosted."
- **Submarine projects** — the countermeasure to restrictive criteria. Projects "initiated
  on the q.t. with a small number of dedicated employees" that **'go to the surface' only
  once the initial results were worked out in prototype form and have won over the
  decision makers.**
- **MVP / MVE** — the book pairs *Minimum Viable Product* with **Minimum Viable Ecosystem
  (MVE)**, introduced here and elaborated in Chapter 9 (book p. 112).
- **Structure as a poster.** Any structure can become a poster — e.g. a simple **pro-and-con
  poster**, to "visualize the collective intelligence of a team or capture a mood." Lilly
  uses it for quick end-of-course feedback without prolonged discussion. For scheduling,
  draw the poster as a **timeline** — as the authors did for the Playbook itself, where
  they needed the **"courage to drop."**

## Mental Models
- **Structuring is alignment.** The argument about which cluster an idea belongs to is
  what creates the shared interpretation the Post-its lacked.
- **Political feasibility is a design dimension.** Speed of dissemination and adaptation
  are ranked *before* implementability, because rollout depends on decision makers.
- **When the criteria block you, go under them, not through them** — build the prototype
  first, surface later.
- **Map choice signals intent** — a mind map is for brainstorming order; a concept map is
  for correlations; a systems map is for actors, flows and influences; a giga map is for a
  holistic conception you will mostly only be able to explain yourself.

## Anti-patterns
- Selecting before establishing a shared reading of the Post-its.
- Letting undiscussed corporate criteria silently kill ideas.
- Pushing an idea that offends the company's values and expecting resources for it.
- Producing a giga map for an audience — "largely owing to its complexity, it is only
  understood by those who created it."

## Reference Tables

**Key questions when the selection criteria are unclear**
- What might the vision be?
- What are the personal preferences at management level?
- What is our enterprise's culture, its values, and its sense of morals?
- Which growth areas have already been defined as part of a strategy discussion?
- What is the financial contribution an idea must yield as a minimum?
- What are the customer needs and trends on the market?

**The map family**

| Map | Structure | Strength | Cost / caveat |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Mind map** | Key concept in the center, built inside-out like a tree | A brainstorming device; brings order to discovered points | **Does not show the correlations among them** |
| **Concept map** | Freer than mind mapping; can start from **several** key concepts; cross-connections between branches like a road network | Graphic depiction of knowledge; excellent for bringing order to thoughts and showing correlations | Takes more time; **"at least three new creations or restructurings are usually necessary to get to a good result"** |
| **Systems map** | Actors, stakeholders, observed elements; interrelationships and influences; **material, energy, money and information flows** | Understand and visualize the problem; supports thinking in variants | Iterative adaptation and detailing; typically top-down, rough to detailed |
| **Giga map** | "A big messy map of a big messy thing" — a stripped-down systems map following the concept-map idea | Draws up a holistic conception of a task; in final form helps communicate the treatise | Usually only understood by those who created it |

**What communication sheets achieve**
- Visualization of the problem and the situation
- Improved understanding of the problem and the idea
- Better understanding of possible influences on customers and users
- Order in our thoughts
- Recognition of approaches to the solution
- Documentation, summary and depiction of our knowledge

They also make ideas **tangible** and minimize misunderstandings when sharing across teams
in major projects and global organizations.

## Worked Example — Selecting inside Peter's political organization

Peter's company is exactly the "political environment" the chapter names, so the selection
runs as the two-tier matrix rather than a single score.

**Step 1 — speed of dissemination × speed of adaptation.** Each surviving cluster is placed
by how fast it would spread and how fast the organization would adopt it. Peter explicitly
factors in *who the decision makers are and what his own influence on the rollout is*. The
ideas landing in the fast/fast quadrant are carried forward — "the best ideas are
characterized by rapid dissemination and fast adaptation."

**Step 2 — implementability × financial feasibility.** The survivors are re-plotted; the
output of this pass is not a single winner but **implementation alternatives and
indications of the functional scope** — i.e. how much of the idea can actually ship.

**The pressures acting on the decision**, as the book diagrams them: *personal preferences
of management* and an *order to the business line* push from above; *customer needs* push
from the user side; *willingness and ability to act* modulates both; and where those
forces block the idea, the **submarine (prototype)** route runs beneath them until an
**MVP** — or, for an ecosystem play, an **MVE (Minimum Viable Ecosystem)** — exists that
can win the decision makers over.

**Documentation.** The selected concept goes onto a **communication sheet**, and the plan
onto a **timeline poster** — the same technique the authors used to plan the Playbook
itself, including the deliberate "courage to drop" and a party at the end that was "quite
motivating for editors and experts."

## Key Takeaways
- Group the ideas and select them systematically — **selection is an important step**, not
  an afterthought.
- Use every possibility for structuring and visualization in order to understand and
  communicate problems or situations.
- Dot voting is fast and democratic; use it to get from clusters to candidates.
- Discuss the organization's real selection criteria openly instead of pretending they
  don't exist; if they block a good idea, run a submarine project.
- Match the map to the job: mind map for order, concept map for correlations, systems map
  for flows, giga map for holistic conception.
- Present the selected idea in a form appropriate for the target group.
