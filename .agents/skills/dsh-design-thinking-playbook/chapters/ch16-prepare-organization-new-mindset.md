# Chapter 16 (Book §2.6) — How to Prepare the Organization for a New Mindset

## Core Idea
Peter has run many design thinking projects and produced innovative, customer-centric
solutions. "His environment, his direct supervisors, and colleagues also know that design
thinking is an asset for the company. **However, Peter notices more and more often that not
all teams back the mindset of design thinking**… many organizations have a hard time
disseminating the approach transversally. **Resentment grows.**"

The chapter's governing metaphor, coined by a cyclist colleague at a **DTP community meeting
in Zurich**:

> **"Design thinking is like a fantastic racing bike and it effectively takes us where we
> haven't been before! But the fact we own a racing bike does not mean we'll be able to
> cross an Alpine pass. We must have the corresponding fitness for it!"**

Peter's diagnosis of his own company: "too many departments that do not share the same
mindset: **Although they have a great racing bike, they have a beer belly and no fitness
whatsoever.**"

And the load-bearing sentence of the whole chapter: **"Design thinking is always only as
effective as the capacity of the organization to implement the result comprehensively and
evenly."**

## Frameworks Introduced

**The three organizational phases**

| Phase | Description | Metaphor |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Pioneer phase** | "An organization built around a leading figure" | **» family** |
| **2. Diversification phase** | "Emergence of a comprehensible and manageable structure"; the company grows and diversifies, "different cultures and silos evolve. This phase is characterized by **efficiency and effectiveness**" | **» machine** |
| **3. Integration phase** | "A holistic structure or system"; "an organism… that ensures that these systems are perfectly matched with one another" | **» organism** |

The pruning rule: "**organizations must be reorganized and rebuilt at regular intervals.**
In nature, an apple tree must be cut and pruned for it to yield a rich harvest again and
again."

The conclusion drawn: **"effective design thinking can optimally unfold only in an
integrated organization."**

**The three elements to pay attention to when enshrining design thinking** (see Reference
Tables): *company management · organizational structure and culture · holistic
implementation of the customer experience.*

**The human-centered culture model** — the chapter's own diagram of what the mindset rests
on: **values (including trust)**, **working atmosphere & incentives**, **collaborating in a
network**, with the **design thinking mindset** at the core, producing **solutions,
products, and services** for **customers and users**.

**The Customer Centricity Score™ (CCScore™)** and the three-step improvement loop (see
Worked Example).

## Key Concepts
- **The silo diagnosis.** "The company has a typical silo structure, which has become
  manifest over the years and allows management to deal with growing complexity and the
  requirement for more efficiency. It is made up of **specialized teams that optimize their
  own fitness.** The preferred tools are **process improvement and operational excellence,
  which make the silos even more efficient. Transversal collaboration for creating a
  consistent customer experience falls by the wayside.**"
- **The lost meaning.** In a diversified organizational form, "the separation of the
  organizational elements can result in the fact that **the overriding meaning of the
  company gets lost on the way**. The business units and departments see the objectives for
  their work exclusively in relation to themselves. Objectives that are meaningful to all
  organizational units are not universally lived. **If they do exist, usually they're only
  the key financial figures of the company — such as profit and EBIT.**" The consequence:
  "human, nonfunctional relationships fall by the wayside due to the lack of motivating
  goals."
- **The shift in value creation.** "The customer experience is becoming **the primary
  product** in many segments owing to the transition from industrial manufacturing toward
  sensitization (alignment to goods and services). **Economic success is determined not by
  the quality of the product but by the fulfillment of our needs across the entire customer
  experience chain.** Customers want an experience — no matter what kind — that they can
  share with others."
- **What successful businesses do differently.** They "gear all their activities and all
  areas as well as their employees toward the customer… **integrate the customer and his
  needs deep in their strategy**, such as by creating an enhanced awareness of **strategic
  foresight**. They put the focus on customer interaction and the design of worlds of
  experience."
- **The leadership change required.** "A **radical change in the usual understanding of
  leadership**, away from the leadership doctrine of an **omnipotent management** toward a
  leadership culture (and a mindset) that **enables the organization to overcome this
  functionally acting structure**" — one where "employees develop a high degree of intrinsic
  motivation but, at the same time, direct their activities toward an overriding goal shared
  by the entire organization."
- **Colleagues are customers too.** "The human-centered approach helps to establish
  orientation toward the customer, which includes **considering colleagues from other
  departments as customers as well.**"
- **The entrepreneurial ideal.** "The ideal is that **all employees see themselves as
  entrepreneurs and act accordingly**… All employees act on their own responsibility, and
  **the work is meaningful to all involved.**"
- **Measurement is not change.** "**Simply to know that we are not comprehensively focused
  on the customer does not yet change the mindset!**"
- **The dedication matters more than the number.** "**Much more important than the
  measurement itself is the dedication that develops from it.** Employees must be actively
  involved in the development of measures. **This is the only way they develop their mindset
  further.**"

## Mental Models
- **Fitness before the bike.** Owning the method is not owning the capability. Examine "its
  excellence (its fitness!) in detail **before beginning**."
- **A network beats a department.** "If we develop design thinking **exclusively at the
  initiative of one single area, it won't have any lasting effect**. From our experience, it
  is promising to establish the basis for effective design thinking through a **company-wide
  network of users and supporters**. This way, design thinking can be disseminated
  transversally. **The buy-in of the decision makers stays as an imperative, though.**"
- **Include everyone in the diagnosis.** "Because customer centricity is a feature of **the
  organization as a whole**, we should include all employees to determine its maturity. **The
  employees usually know best where the organization can be improved.**"
- **Representative sample or noise.** "The measurement of customer centricity is similar to
  a survey on customer satisfaction. **If we involve only a few people, the result is
  patchy.** To get to meaningful information, we must question **a representative
  cross-section of the workforce.**"
- **A closed control loop, not a one-off score.** "In a closed circuit comprising
  measurement and inventory, reflection and the development of measures, as well as the
  subsequent implementation in the organization, **causes and effects of improvement
  measures can be tracked and controlled.**"
- **"Change by design."** The book's phrase for treating customer centricity and design
  thinking as "active, complementary aspects of change management."

## Anti-patterns
- Buying the method without building the organizational fitness to use it.
- Launching design thinking from one department's initiative alone.
- Doubling down on process improvement and operational excellence — the tools that make
  silos *more* efficient and transversal collaboration harder.
- Letting profit and EBIT be the only company-wide objectives.
- Omnipotent management as the leadership doctrine.
- Measuring customer centricity with a small, unrepresentative sample.
- Measuring and stopping — treating the score as the intervention.
- Developing improvement measures *for* employees rather than *with* them.

## Worked Example — Raising customer centricity: the three-step CCScore™ loop

The framing, in the chapter's own metaphor: "For our example of the racing bike, this means:
**We build up our muscles purposefully where we need the strength and endurance for our
Alpine crossing!**"

**Step 1 — Measure the strength.**
"The strength of customer centricity is measured by an **online assessment**. This allows
for a very detailed and differentiated view of **the individual drivers behind the
CCScore™**. It will become quite clear **what the individual factors contribute to the
overall score of the company and where there is potential for improvement.**"

Design conditions carried over from the chapter: the assessment must reach a
**representative cross-section of the workforce**, because customer centricity is a property
of the whole organization and a thin sample yields "a patchy" result.

**Step 2 — Infer specific options for action.**
"In a **method-based reflection**, the causes and drivers of the CCScore™ results are
analyzed, **translated into relevant strategies for improving customer centricity, and
written down.** This so-called **U procedure** is basically **a change process, which
develops far more than just customer centricity; it enables the company to strengthen the
effectiveness of its organization fundamentally and thus lays the cornerstone for effective
design thinking.**"

The non-negotiable here: "Employees must be **actively involved** in the development of
measures. This is the only way they develop their mindset further."

**Step 3 — Define an action plan and implement it.**
"To implement the strategies that have emerged, **an action plan is drawn up for selected
measures.** The implementation of the measures is initiated; **progress is regularly
monitored**; and **goal attainment is checked in the following CCScore™ measurements.** The
development of customer centricity can be thus monitored and controlled."

**The loop closes:** *1. Measurement → 2. Options for action → 3. Implementation →* back to
measurement. "The measurement results **only constitute the starting point** of a
multi-tier process that leads to the targeted improvement of customer centricity."

**Applied to Peter's company.** The silo diagnosis says the low score would come from
departments optimizing their own fitness while transversal collaboration for a consistent
customer experience "falls by the wayside," and from the absence of any company-wide
objective beyond profit and EBIT. The measures that follow are therefore structural —
a **company-wide network of users and supporters** for design thinking, **decision-maker
buy-in**, management naming customer centricity as a strategic theme with a clear vision,
and a basis of trust that lets employees act independently — not another round of process
improvement, which would only make the silos more efficient.

## Key Takeaways
- **Create an organizational structure without silos** — "this is the only way to
  disseminate design thinking transversally in the company."
- Establish a mindset focused on **designing experiences** — positive experiences across the
  entire customer experience chain, or "Wow!" effects when dealing with the product.
- **Put the customer and his needs at the center of any activity. He is the reason the
  company exists.**
- Consider customer centricity and design thinking as **active, complementary aspects of
  change management**. Live the **"change by design"** thought.
- **Measure customer centricity** (e.g. by way of an index) and improve it step by step.
- The transformation into a new mindset encompasses **all levels: management, structure, and
  implementation.**
- Raise the awareness of company management for a new mindset; **create commitment to and
  confidence in the new way of working throughout the organization.**
