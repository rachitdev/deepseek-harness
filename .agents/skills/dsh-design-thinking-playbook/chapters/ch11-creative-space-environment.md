# Chapter 11 (Book §2.1) — How to Design a Creative Space and Environment

## Core Idea
"The premises of most companies and universities were neither planned nor staged as
creative spaces, nor are they suitable for such use. The majority of them are **filled to
the gills with bulky furniture, thus blocking any creative energy.** In particular the
tables prompt people to work individually or else work on their laptop. In the best case,
employees or students sit around a table, which encourages, at most, an exchange of ideas,
but **does not generate any shared common creativity.**"

The good news: **"nearly every room that has plenty of natural light and space (preferably
about 5 m² / 55 sq ft per participant) can be quickly reshaped into a creative space. The
goal is to gain as much freedom as possible for creativity to unfold."** And the method is
design thinking applied to itself: "We do best by starting with redesigning the environment
and **implementing the first prototype of a creative space.**"

The governing principle throughout: **less is more.** "Only in an empty space can something
new evolve."

## Frameworks Introduced

**The three-stage maturity path for a creative space**
1. **The first prototype** — empty the room, minimum furniture, borrowed space, whatever
   materials exist.
2. **Further improvement**, driven by two reflection questions: *(1) What has worked well
   in the application? What would we like to have more of?* and *(2) How do we want to work
   in the future, and what helps our wishes come true?*
3. **The right creative space for my organization** — fit to industry, enterprise type and
   prevailing corporate culture.

**The four elements of a work environment** (the expert tip "Design not only spaces but
also the work environment"): **the place, the people, the process, and the meaningfulness
of the work.** "The work environment has become one of the most important instruments for a
company to retain the best talents and high performers."

**The prototyping-workshop structure for designing a creative space** — input / sequence /
output / resources, run over about one and a half days (see Worked Example).

**Sizing rule.** The design of the space must account for whether it hosts:
- **a project team of 4 to 12 members** working on a project **for weeks or months**, or
- **8 to 25 participants** who sit over a topic **only one or two days**.

For feedback providers, add stools or **textile cubes** ("can also be used as seat stacks
and staged beautifully"). "Feedback sessions last only a couple hours, not days, so simple
seating accommodations are quite reasonable."

## Key Concepts
- **Empty first.** "Start with emptying the room, because less is more in this case."
  Consider how many people are to be creative in it and add **one or two additional
  chairs**, stackable if possible.
- **Flexible beats rigid.** "Flexible and stackable material is better suited than
  inflexible and rigid stuff, because stackable furniture allows you to create even more
  space if the situation calls for it."
- **Tables: organic, free-standing, movable.** "Choose a more organic, stimulating shape
  over a rigid rectangular one. The table should be placed free-standing in the space
  because… all wall surfaces will be included in the creative work."
- **Chairs distributed, not arranged.** "For the creative process, we don't arrange the
  chairs around a table, but instead distribute them freely in the room. When they don't
  sit stiffly at the table, **participants stay more agile, both physically and
  mentally**."
- **Material on chairs, not tables.** "Instead of on the table, we can simply put the
  required material on chairs or stools that are not needed. This uses less room."
- **Keep the walls clear.** Bags and luggage go "on top of or underneath unoccupied
  chairs," not on the floor along the walls — "this is the only way to work on the walls
  free of hindrances and for the results to be presented and seen later." Coatrack: a
  movable stand, or put it outside the room.
- **Never run out.** "Nothing is more annoying and inhibiting to the creative flow than
  when we run out of basic material — this includes **functioning whiteboard markers**."
- **The squared side.** "It goes without saying that the flip chart paper is used on the
  side with the squares."
- **Build XXL surfaces by hand.** "If large paper surfaces are needed and XXL sheets are
  unavailable, we glue together any amount of flip chart pages with the masking tape on the
  back to build huge creative surfaces… creative energy needs room to unfold."
- **Uneven walls:** use several sheets on top of one another, or work with Post-its written
  on before placing.
- **Color has assignments.** "Orange stands for creativity, flexibility, and agility, and
  blue for communication, inspiration, and clarity." Red is also welcome. "Colors and
  patterns on usually barren floors are outstanding for inducing creativity."
- **Music, coffee and brain food.** Active loudspeakers ("soft music playing in the
  background during design sessions"); the caddy can hold a coffee machine and electric
  kettle; **bottles of water, cups, nuts and dried fruits** in the room.
- **Meaningfulness is the underrated motivator.** "Surprisingly, the majority of companies
  have a hard time defining the **'why.'** Especially for the much-cited millennial
  generation, meaningfulness is a key criterion for choosing an employer."

## Mental Models
- **The room is a prototype.** Treat it with LOVE IT / CHANGE IT / LEAVE IT, iterate it
  after each use, and let the team that uses it design it.
- **Co-design produces identification.** "A creative space can be designed jointly as part
  of a team development process. After all, the participants must feel comfortable and
  identify with their space. **By the way, this is why employees often don't feel
  comfortable in stylish rooms: because their wishes and needs have not been taken into
  consideration sufficiently or at all.**"
- **Deliberate disruption is a design option.** Rubber boats, hammocks, shower curtains as
  separation — "such objects can have an inspiring or consciously disruptive effect. This
  'disruptive' function of a creative space that dissolves or destroys what exists is quite
  conceivable in order to put things in motion."
- **Low profile first.** "Begin with a low profile and observe the reactions of the
  environment carefully before you go too far with your creativity."
- **Resistance is data.** "As with any innovation, you'll likely encounter resistance.
  Sometimes, such resistance points at real weaknesses in a concept; sometimes, people are
  simply suspicious. **Any resistance must be taken seriously and accounted for in the
  implementation process.**"
- **Simple materials win.** Lilly's favourite prototyping material is **aluminum foil** —
  "any shapes can be quickly created from it, and pieces can easily be made smaller without
  using scissors… with time, you will realize that **simple materials in particular have
  the potential for great prototypes.**"

## Anti-patterns
- Overloading the environment. "Less is more: Creativity mainly requires a great deal of
  freedom and space."
- Rooms fraught with boring memories. "Avoid spaces that are fraught with boring
  memories."
- A stylish room designed *for* the team rather than *with* it.
- Fixed rectangular tables with chairs arranged around them.
- Going straight to a lavish build without a first, cheap prototype of the space.
- Ignoring industry, enterprise type and corporate culture when choosing how far to push.

## Reference Tables

**Equipment by maturity stage**

| | First prototype | Professional next level |
|---|---|---|
| **Walls** | Smooth walls papered with flip chart sheets and masking tape; nails for individual sheets | **Whiteboards attached to the walls**; magnets, incl. extra-strong magnets for posters and heavier paper; writable walls, window panes, glass walls |
| **Boards** | Flip charts on rollers; large paper rolls as an alternative | **Flexible whiteboard walls (in the HPI design) on rollers**; whiteboards and pin boards on rollers |
| **Tables** | Lightweight, movable, rollers a plus; organic shape; free-standing | **Foldable, on rollers; square measurements** — "the tables used in the Design Space at the Stanford d.school have this shape. Four workshop participants can group around these tables, and enough space is left to sketch something or for prototyping" |
| **Chairs** | One or two more than participants; stackable | Different colors, stackable preferred; stools and textile cubes for feedback providers |
| **Prototyping material** | Playdough, Lego bricks, string, colored sheets of paper, cotton wool, pipe cleaners, masking tape — laid out on the table **at the beginning of the workshop** | Styrofoam, colored wool, wood, balloons, fabrics, cardboard, "the good old extensive collection of Lego bricks" — "everything handicraft shops have on offer" |
| **Storage** | A **caddy on wheels** with multicolored whiteboard markers, Post-its in various colors and sizes, adhesive dots; or **transparent boxes** (advisable if you travel with material or switch rooms) | A treasure chest for materials |
| **Extras** | — | Painted walls (orange, blue, red); floor color/pattern via carpet, PVC, homey wood or paint; active loudspeakers; coffee machine and kettle in the caddy; water, cups, nuts and dried fruits; larger screens **on rollers** instead of a small screen and projector |

**Stacking tip:** "Stack tables efficiently by putting one on top of another, **with the
tabletop to the front**, so it can be used for sticking inputs and results onto it."

## Worked Example — The bank's creative space, from Jonny's borrowed room to a design brief

**The first prototype (Jonny).** At the bank where he works, "Jonny finds meeting rooms
galore, but only very few of them have the necessary flexibility to foster creativity. He
has brought up the need for such a space several times already. In the end, he succeeded in
convincing his boss, **while having lunch together**, to venture the experiment of a
creative space." The room he gets "is not optimal, but the old coding machines that were
stored there had to be disposed of sooner or later anyway."

**The design brief — creative space** (the book's filled-in brief)

> "The pace of change in business and the challenges entailed therein have become
> enormously complex. To cope with these developments, many companies and organizations
> have come to master tasks with more collaborative approaches to innovation.
>
> Especially for creative working, workspace design is decisive for promoting communication
> and creativity. Companies such as **Google, Apple, and Procter & Gamble** have been
> pioneers in creating innovative and inspiring working atmospheres for flexible and
> individual collaboration.
>
> The spatial environment is usually characterized by specific furnishings: **flexible
> furniture, lots of space on the walls, the necessary tools and materials to visualize
> research impressions and new ideas, and suitable places to retreat to, in which ideas can
> be given their initial form.**
>
> We as a traditional banking house want to follow these developments as part of our
> digitization efforts. In a first attempt, we begin with a creative space for our
> co-creation workshops in the **FinTech departments at our office in Singapore**. In
> particular, business models, new business ecosystems, and first prototypes in conjunction
> with technologies such as **blockchain** are to emerge from these workshops."

**Design challenge:** *"How might a creative space look that gives us the flexibility to
begin a cooperative innovation process with various stakeholders (internal/external),
taking into account our values and our brand as a branch of a traditional French financial
institution in Southeast Asia?"*

**The two-day prototyping workshop — "Prototyping workshop, model creative space"**

**Resources:** *Catering, tables, chairs, pin boards, flip charts, blank walls…* ·
*Timer, prototyping material, Post-its, pens…* · *Team, facilitator, jury…*

**How the one and a half days are planned:**
1. As input, the **design brief and pictures of other creative spaces**.
2. Warmup in the morning, then individual brainstorming and several brainstorming sessions
   "in connection with **prompt translation into prototypes**. The testing is done with
   employees in the **cafeteria and coffee corners**. At the end, the final prototype is
   presented to the decision makers."
3. Result: **two to three models of a prototype**, "which we will either continue to
   improve or order to be implemented."
4. "Because the approach contains many elements of a design thinking cycle, **the
   participants become familiar with it**" — the workshop teaches the method while
   producing the room.

The design challenge itself is deliberately shaped: "an initial, roughly formulated design
challenge that is **open enough and does not contain any solution in its description**."

## Key Takeaways
- Don't overload the environment; creativity requires freedom and space.
- Use rolling and stackable material for maximum flexibility.
- Stack tables tabletop-to-front so the surface stays usable for inputs and results.
- Try as many different creative spaces with teams as possible to learn what helps.
- **Conduct an initial workshop with members of management in an external creative space**
  to induce enthusiasm for the impact of an inspiring environment.
- Change space, location and environment as often as needed; avoid rooms with boring
  memories.
- Design the work environment too — the place, the people, the process, and the
  meaningfulness of the work.
