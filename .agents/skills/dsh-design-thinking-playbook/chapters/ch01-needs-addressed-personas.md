# Chapter 1 (Book §1.1) — What Needs Are Addressed in the Playbook?

## Core Idea
The Playbook practices what it preaches: before writing it, the authors ran their own
design thinking cycle on their readers. They built three personas — **Peter**, **Lilly**,
and **Marc** — and wrote the whole book against those personas' pains, gains,
jobs-to-be-done and use cases. The chapter is therefore two things at once: the book's
own problem statement, and a full tutorial on the **Understand** phase (persona,
empathy map, AEIOU, hook canvas, jobs-to-be-done, future user).

The governing rule of the whole book is stated here as three commitments to the reader:
1. **Keep your personality!** — "No one needs to mutate into Karl Lagerfeld just because
   we have creativity and room for development at our disposal."
2. **Love it, change it, or leave it!** — "Use the concepts and tips that you like and
   adapt them to your needs." It would be a shame if all organizations were a clone of
   Google, Spotify, or Uber.
3. **Don't do it alone!** — get the skills, technologies and attitudes on your team and
   think in business ecosystems.

And the framing warning: **"Design thinking is not a structured process!"** The "How
might we…" instructions in the book are indications, not recipes.

## Frameworks Introduced

**The eight-element design thinking mindset** (the book's own manifesto, stated up front):
- Driven by curiosity — ask "WH questions" continuously, change perspective.
- Focus on the people — build empathy, be mindful when exploring needs.
- Accept complexity — complex system problems demand complex solutions.
- Visualize and show — stories, visualizations, simple language.
- Experiment and iterate — build and test prototypes in the context of the user.
- Co-create, grow, and scale — build scalable market opportunities in ecosystems.
- With varying mental states — combine design thinking with data analytics, systems
  thinking, and lean start-up as the situation requires.
- Develop process awareness — know where you stand; feel the "groan zone."
- Networked collaboration — ad hoc, agile, networked, with T-shaped people and
  U-shaped teams across departments and companies.
- Reflect on actions — our thinking, actions and attitudes shape our assumptions.

**User profile canvas** — a persona canvas with fields: Name; Persona description (age,
gender, place of residence, marital status, hobbies, leisure, education, position in the
company, social environment, Sinus-Milieus category, way of thinking); Jobs-to-be-done;
Use cases; Gains; Pains; Photos; Stories. The authors recommend cutting the canvas apart
and gluing the pieces onto a huge poster so the persona can be drawn life-size.

**Empathy map** — areas: hearing; thinking and feeling; seeing; speaking and doing;
frustration; desire. Credo: *"Walk in the shoes of a potential user!"*

**Hook canvas** (Nir Eyal's hook model as a design canvas) — four linked elements:
- **Trigger** — external (a Tinder "Super Like" notification) or internal (opening
  Facebook because you feel lonely). Key question: what are the relevant triggers of an
  action for each persona?
- **Action** — the *minimum* interaction of the service with the user. "What is the
  simplest action that our user must perform to be rewarded?"
- **Variable reward** — the key emotional element; the reward can exceed the original
  goal (you shared information, you got reputation back).
- **Investment** — what the user puts in that reloads the next trigger (following a feed,
  setting a back-in-stock notification).

**Jobs-to-be-done framework** — asks "why would a customer buy my product?" instead of
"what property should the product have?" Format: *"When ___, I want to ___, so I can ___."*

**Future user** — for radical innovation with 10-year horizons. Steps: (1) describe the
target customer today; (2) discuss the lifestyle and values this customer had 12 years
ago and the decisions made then; (3) interview today's 22-year-olds; (4) compare the two
people *at the same age* and see what changed and what did not; (5) extrapolate to
describe the future user who exists today. Cross-referenced to the authors' companion
"Playbook for Strategic Foresight and Innovation."

**AEIOU** — observation framework (Activities, Environment, Interaction, Objects, Users),
applied with "what? how? why?" See the Toolbox skill for mechanics.

## Key Concepts
- **Persona twins** — the trap of thin personas. Two people: born 1948, grew up in
  England, married twice, has children, successful, rich, takes vacations in the Alps,
  likes dogs. One is **Prince Charles**; the other is **Ozzy Osbourne**. Same core
  attributes, utterly different users. Therefore always dig one level deeper.
- **Profiler analogy** — persona work resembles the case analysts in American detective
  series: describe personality and character traits to draw conclusions from behavior.
- **Personas must be verified.** Potential users are first sketched from participants'
  knowledge; that sketch must then be checked against reality via interviews and
  observation, because real users routinely have different needs than assumed.
- **The three personas of the book** (used throughout all 25 sections):
  - **Peter**, 40, Innovation & Co-Creation Manager at a large Swiss ICT company, MSc
    Electrical Engineering (TU Munich). Wants to be a design thinking expert; wants to
    combine design thinking with other methods; needs to bring a new mindset to his team
    and his whole company; wants to know which design criteria digitization demands.
  - **Lilly**, 28, design thinking and lean start-up coach at Singapore University of
    Technology & Design (SUTD); studied Enterprise Management at Zhejiang University,
    a year at École des Ponts ParisTech, came to design thinking via **ME310** with
    THALES as industry partner. Doctoral thesis (with MIT): *"Design of Powerful Business
    Ecosystems in a Digitized World."* Uses **HBDI®** (Herrmann Brain Dominance
    Instrument) to compose groups of four to five. Wants examples from actual practice
    and a global community; wants to adapt the Western mindset to an Asian context.
    Partner: **Jonny**, an expat at a major French bank in Singapore, watching fintech
    and blockchain and asking whether banks as we know them will cease to exist.
  - **Marc**, 27, MSc Computer Science, Stanford, blockchain founder, "the innovator" of
    his start-up team. Grew up in Detroit watching the auto industry lose its luster;
    learned early to "dance" with uncertainty. His team: Beatrice "the brain," Tamara
    "the visionary," Vadim "the mover and shaker," Stephan "the strategist," Alex "the
    salesman." Needs teams-of-teams, faster learning, and an ecosystem design.

## Mental Models
- **Design the book like a product.** Determine customer needs → create personas → build
  empathy → then write. Applies to any deliverable, not just products.
- **Divergent/convergent awareness begins in Understand.** The chapter closes by handing
  off to §1.2: the transition from divergent to convergent is the hard part, and Lilly
  has it easier only because she controls how open the design challenge is.
- **Deliberately over-broaden the frame first.** "Normally, we force ourselves at the
  beginning to leave our comfort zone and define the creative framework broader than we
  actually wish to."

## Anti-patterns
- **Thin personas** — brief descriptions that produce Prince Charles when you meant Ozzy
  Osbourne.
- **Assuming the sketched persona exists.** Never skip validation interviews.
- **Attribute-tinkering instead of jobs-thinking** — asking "another topping? another cup
  size?" yields only incremental innovation (the milkshake trap).
- **Cloning another company's culture.** "Love it, change it, or leave it."
- **Taking photos without asking.** "Not everybody likes to be photographed or filmed!"

**"How might we… develop a persona?" — the 10-step loop**

| # | Step | Question | Methods |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Find the user | Who are the users? How many? What do they do? | Quantitative data collection, AEIOU |
| 2 | Build a hypothesis | What are the differences between the users? | Description of groups of similar users / segmentation |
| 3 | Confirmations | Is there data or evidence that confirms the hypothesis? | Quantitative data collection, empathy map |
| 4 | Patterns | Are the initial group descriptions still correct? Are there other important groups? | Categorization, jobs-to-be-done framework |
| 5 | Creating personas | How can the persona be described? | Categorization, persona |
| 6 | Define situations | What use cases does the persona have? What is the situation? | Searching for situations and needs; user profile canvas / customer profile; customer journey |
| 7 | Validation | Do you know such a person? | Interviews with people who know the persona; reading and commenting on the description |
| 8 | Dissemination of knowledge | How can we present the personas and share them? | Posters, meetings, e-mails, campaigns, events, videos, photos |
| 9 | Creating scenarios | In a given situation with a given objective, what happens when the persona uses the technology? | Narrative scenario — storytelling, situation descriptions; hook canvas |
| 10 | Continuous further development | Is there new information? Must the persona be re-described? | Usability test, continuous revision |

## Worked Example — The Milkshake (jobs-to-be-done)

Problem statement as usually posed: *"How can the sales of milkshakes be increased by 15%?"*
With a conventional mindset you inspect product properties — a different topping, another
flavor, a different cup size — survey customers, find the new properties popular, and ship
an incremental improvement that barely moves the number.

Reframed as jobs-to-be-done — *why* do customers buy this product, and what would they buy
*instead* of the milkshake? — two distinct customers appear:

| | Morning customer | Afternoon customer |
|---|---|---|
| Who | Commuter, drives to work | A mother with a whining child |
| Situation | Needs a breakfast substitute and a diversion while driving | Child wants something to eat; mother wants something healthy |
| Why not coffee | First too hot, then too cold; liquid, spills easily | — |
| Ideal milkshake | Large, nutritious, **thick** | Small, **thin**, liquid, low in calories |
| Job-to-be-done | Breakfast substitute + pleasant diversion on the commute | Satisfy the child and make the mother feel good |

The same product has two opposite specs (thick vs. thin) because it is hired for two
different jobs. The digital counterpart the book gives:

- *When I take a photograph with [a camera], I want to be able to edit it so it looks
  like a professional photographer took it, so I can show perfect pictures.* → Adobe
  Photoshop.
- *When I take a photograph with my cell phone, I want to edit it in an easy and simple
  manner, so I can share it quickly with my friends.* → Instagram.

## Key Takeaways
- Write the persona before you write the solution — including when the "product" is a book.
- Use real names, real properties, life-size drawings, magazine cut-outs for accessories.
- Be specific about age and marital status; get demographic data from the Internet.
- List pains and gains; capture the jobs-to-be-done the product supports; describe the
  experience that is *particularly critical* and prototype exactly that.
- If time is short, standard personas can be used — but the intensity and closeness of
  building one yourself is what creates empathy.
- Iterate the persona; refine and dig deeper step by step.
