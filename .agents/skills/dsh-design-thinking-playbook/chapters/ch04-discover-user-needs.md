# Chapter 4 (Book §1.4) — How to Discover User Needs

## Core Idea
Needfinding cannot be delegated, skipped, or reconstructed at a desk. The chapter's engine
is a fourth persona, **Priya**, an innovation manager at an Internet and technology giant
suddenly assigned "health for seniors" — a segment she knows nothing about, in an office
where nobody has crossed 50, whose Zurich friends are all 30–40, whose parents still work
full time, and whose grandparents have died. Her question: *"How can we carry out a
needfinding when we actually have no time for it? Or better: How do we explain to the boss
that we won't come to work today?"*

The answer: needfinding does not require a day off — it requires **surfacing your
assumptions, ranking the critical ones, and then integrating random encounters into
ordinary life.** Skipping needfinding is not skipping one step; because Understand,
Observe and synthesis (Define point of view) cannot be strictly separated, "ignoring
needfinding would mean omitting no fewer than three steps."

## Frameworks Introduced

**The five-step assumption-clearing exercise** (≈30 minutes; best done with someone who
asks tricky questions). Starting point: an initial simple prototype already exists, so
ideation is concluded for now.

1. **Formulate the idea in one sentence** and visualize it. Priya's: *"Senior walks for
   retired couch potatoes"* — visualized as the "Senior wizard."
2. **Formulate the need assumptions.** Needs are the actual motivations of people; they
   emerge from the desire to make possible something that does not exist (staying healthy)
   or to get rid of something unwanted (losing weight). **In design thinking we define
   needs as verbs. Needs refer to WHAT the user wants to achieve — solution-oriented HOW
   thinking is consciously set aside.** Ask: What does the user want to achieve by
   applying our idea? What motivates the user to use it? What prevents the user from using
   it? Write each assumption on a separate Post-it.
3. **Identify the critical assumptions** — reflect for a few minutes on the assumptions
   themselves. Typically you discover "a wonderful crop of assumptions on which we have
   built our solution."
4. **Be ready for random encounters** — decide in advance what question you would use to
   approach a stranger, and where and when you could meet the target group in *their*
   everyday life (shopping, on a trip, on the train, at the bus stop).
5. **Review the critical assumptions first** — the ones you know least about and that
   matter most. Structure with a four-quadrant grid on the axes **"incidental" vs.
   "decisive"** and **"knowing" vs. "ignorant."**

**The six-part needfinding interview**

| # | Stage | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Introduction** | Introduce yourself, explain the reason for the request and the course of the interview. Emphasize there is no "true" or "false." Ask permission to document (video, photos, audio). Respondents must feel appreciated and that their knowledge is valuable |
| 2 | **Actual beginning** | Let the interviewee introduce themselves; start with a general, open question about the theme; go deeper on the answer with expanding and clarifying questions |
| 3 | **Create reference** | Find a recent example the person remembers well. If depth isn't reached, be patient and ask for more experiences and stories |
| 4 | **Grand tour** | Deepen other critical topics and **search for contradictions**. Get to the bottom of details, tangible and emotional. "We have reached our goal when things that were hidden come to light" |
| 5 | **Reflection** | Pause; express gratitude; summarize the main points *from your point of view* — the interviewee will often add things, point out inconsistencies, emphasize items. Ask the "why" question here; you may move to a general level to discuss explanations or theories |
| 6 | **Wrap-up** | **Don't turn off the recording device yet!** The most intriguing things often occur at the very end. Thank them again, let them ask you questions, then reflect on content *and* approach |

**Lead users as innovators** (term coined by **Eric von Hippel**). Definition: users who
have the needs and requirements *earlier than the mass market* and expect a particularly
high benefit and competitive edge from satisfying that need. They have a strong drive to
solve their own problem, which drives them to build interim solutions and prototypes.
Major innovations that came from lead users: **the mountain bike, the hyperlink structure
of the World Wide Web, and GEOX shoes.**

Three-step approach:
- **Step 1 — Identify needs and trends.** Scan secondary sources (future researchers,
  trend reports, trend scouts, research directions, market experts, technology experts);
  preliminarily determine important early trends and future needs.
- **Step 2 — Search for lead users and lead experts** in the target market; identify
  **analogous markets** by abstracting your own questions and topics.
- **Step 3 — Develop solution concepts.** Turn rudimentary ideas into innovation concepts
  in a large workshop with lead users, lead experts and technicians. Within co-creation,
  keep the lead users in the prototype process.

**The 6 WH questions** — the divergent-phase overview tool.

## Key Concepts
- **"It is an illusion to think that we are familiar with the lifestyles of all the people
  for which we develop innovations."** Over four years as a needfinding expert, Lilly
  would have had to be old, visually impaired, lesbian, a kindergartener, or an illegal
  immigrant — and, for the palliative care ward project, on her deathbed.
- **Reflect that you are not the user.** In the exceptional cases where you are, "we must
  proceed with great caution when transferring our needs onto others."
- **Priya's village fallacy** — she grew up in an Indian village of similar size, so she
  believes she knows how Swiss villagers live. Her experience gives access to *certain
  aspects* of village life but cannot cover the majority of today's needs.
- **The blank-stare test.** Ask an innovation manager what doesn't work in the everyday
  life of the person whose life their idea would improve. If you get a blank stare, they
  have generated ideas not based on real needs — and sending them out is useless, because
  "they don't know what they should see and hear."
- **Confirmation bias in outsourced research.** Consultants who rely on customer
  interviews conducted by a market research institute rather than themselves "choose from
  the interviews only those things that match what he has seen or heard in the reality he
  has developed over a lifetime."
- **Crossing the Chasm caveat.** Lilly, having read Geoffrey Moore, knows there is a gap
  between the needs of lead users, early adopters and the early majority, so she always
  also tries to recognize the needs of "normal" customers.
- **The "white elephant."** Peter has been on projects that focused far too much on lead
  users; the product got that sobriquet. Such projects have **high risk, low likelihood of
  implementation, and are hard to stop** — sometimes only a few real customers exist for a
  solution many lead users found interesting.

## Mental Models
- **Needs are verbs.** Force need statements into the WHAT, and keep the HOW out.
- **"The sooner we recognize it, the better."** If the critical assumptions don't exist in
  everyday life, you built "a mental castle in the air" — and killing it early frees money,
  time and energy to hunt the next big market opportunity.
- **Curiosity as the instrument.** "If we succeed in embodying an attitude of pure
  curiosity in needfinding, we find [insights] that can guide us to new and even more
  human-centered solutions."
- **The tool is not the point.** "Such tools can help us, but ultimately it is all a matter
  of one decisive point: [surfacing] the assumptions you have made in your mind and
  becoming aware of them."

## Anti-patterns
- Validating with friends and colleagues, who "think our solution is good" because they
  like you and don't want to dampen your enthusiasm.
- Asking "Would you like…?" instead of watching the customer search for the function
  unprompted.
- Outsourcing interviews and then cherry-picking the quotes that confirm your worldview.
- Designing only for lead users → the white elephant.
- Evaluating answers as they come in, rather than at the end.

## Reference Tables

**Digging deeper — the five moves behind the scenes**

| Move | Instruction |
|---|---|
| Set aside your own experience | "It is best to forget our own problems and wishes!" |
| Hunt workarounds | Search for the workarounds, remedies and quick fixes of your users |
| Catch contradictions | Recognize contradictions between what interviewees say and what they actually do |
| Separate need from solution | Differentiate between need and solution; if you already have a solution in mind you will read the need through it |
| Map alternatives | Chart alternative solutions against the problem |

**Open-question bank for needfinding interviews**

| Purpose | Questions |
|---|---|
| Tracing behaviors | "Why do you smile when you say that?" · "How did it happen that…?" · "Who has taught you that?" · "How do you know how it works?" · "What works? / What doesn't work?" |
| Gaining clarity | "What exactly do you mean by…?" · "How would you describe it in your own words?" |
| Exploring actively | "You say this is difficult. What exactly was/is difficult about it?" · "A difficult task. Why exactly is it difficult for you?" |
| Asking about sequence (day/week/period of life) | "What is your first memory of…?" · "What happened before/afterward?" · "How did you do it before?" · "When was the first/last time that you…?" |
| Asking for examples | "What was the last app you downloaded?" · "With whom did you discuss it?" |
| Exploring exceptions | "When didn't it work, then?" · "Did you have problems with… before?" |
| Understanding connections | "How do you communicate with…?" · "From whom did you hear that?" · "Who helped you with it?" · "How did you hear of it?" |
| Informing outsiders | "If you had to explain it to an exchange student, what would you say?" · "How would you explain this to your grandparents?" · "How would you describe it to a small child?" |
| Comparing processes | "What is the difference between your home and that of your friend?" · "What is the difference when you do this on the road instead of at home?" |
| Imagining the future | "How do you think you'll do it in 2030?" (What if it were like that already today?) |

**The 6 WH questions grid**

| What | Who | Why | Where | When |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| What is the problem? | Who is involved? | Why is the problem important? | Where does the problem occur? | When did the problem [occur]? |
| What would we like to know? | Who is affected by the situation? | Why does it occur? | Where was it already resolved before? | When do people see results? |
| What are the assumptions being scrutinized? | Who decides? | Why was it not yet solved? | Where did similar situations exist? | When did the problem start? |

Rules for use: (1) create a set of WH questions; (2) list possible sub-questions;
(3) try to answer all of them; (4) skip a WH question that makes no sense in context;
(5) in a problem interview, use probing and repeating questions; (6) **try to find more
than one answer to every question — conflicting answers should be amplified more deeply
together with the user**; (7) **evaluate the answers only at the end** and filter by
relevance to the solution.

**Three-step reflection after needfinding**

| Step | Question | Sub-questions |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Reflect on the user and the need | How do people think and act in everyday life? What is done differently than we imagined? What surprises us ("Eureka!" moments)? Is there a need worth solving? |
| 2 | Is our solution the right one? | Is it really true that we don't have to change anything for our idea to work in everyday life? What would we change so the innovation is used in everyday life? |
| 3 | Were our approach and the kind of questions right? | Did the way we posed our questions come across well? Was our documentation of any use later? What was good, where should we improve, what should we still try? |

## Worked Example — Priya's "Senior walks" assumptions

**Idea in one sentence:** *Senior walks for retired "couch potatoes."*

**Need assumptions, one per Post-it** (note how the book tags each as need, trigger, or
emotional state/blocker):

1. Couch potatoes want to **exercise** (need) in order to **prevent chronic diseases**
   (need).
2. Retirees **don't have the necessary daily structure** (trigger) to exercise on a
   regular basis (need).
3. Senior citizens want to **feel healthy** (need) so they can go on excursions with their
   grandchildren.
4. Senior citizens **feel uncomfortable** (emotional state/blocker) when they exercise at
   the fitness center together with young people.

**Critical-assumption grid.** Post-its are placed on axes *incidental ↔ decisive* and
*knowing ↔ ignorant*. Assumption 4 — the emotional blocker Priya has no direct evidence
for and which decides whether the concept must be age-segregated — lands in
decisive/ignorant and is therefore reviewed first.

**Random-encounter plan.** Priya does not take a day off. She lists where and when she can
meet retirees inside her own week: shopping, on a trip, on the train, at the bus stop —
and prepares the opening question she would use to get a stranger to talk about their
everyday life.

**Question map.** Priya builds an open-interview question map around clusters such as
*changes*, *rhythm*, *what changes?*, *what is difficult?*, *sports in old age*,
*motivation*, *being happy*, *significance* — and keeps enriching and extending it with
supplementary questions she meets over time (e.g. whether seniors living in the country
exercise differently).

**Result.** "Priya's arguments are no longer based on her assumptions but on the things she
has heard and seen… She has a solid idea of how it really feels to be at an advanced age
and wishing for a healthy life."

## Key Takeaways
- Find the persona in real life and interview them.
- Forget all initial assumptions about the product or service and focus on user behavior.
- Observe and listen carefully; document observations exactly so assumptions can be
  corrected.
- Walk in the user's footsteps; accompany them for a day of their daily life.
- **Identify extreme users too** — e.g. seniors who do strenuous sports at a very old age.
- Review your experience with other users iteratively and never stop being curious.
- Plan and prepare the interview diligently; build a question map for an open interview.
