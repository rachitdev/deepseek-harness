# Chapter 9 (Book §1.9) — How to Create a Good Prototype

## Core Idea
"With a prototype, an idea is brought into a form that allows potential users to
experience and evaluate it." The prototype is not a draft of the product; it is a
**question made physical**. Ideas rest on assumptions, and "the task is to question these
assumptions and confirm them in the real world by testing, or to discard them — that is,
disprove them with a counterexample on the basis of observations and an experiment."

The governing constraint: **"At the outset, the prototype must be only good enough to make
the relevant basic features of a future offer comprehensible to a target audience."** And
the governing attitude: "all those involved in the process must keep an open mind so an
idea can be changed or discarded. What is crucial here is the willingness to make radical
changes."

## Frameworks Introduced

**The three principles of prototyping**
1. **First principle: "LOVE IT — CHANGE IT — LEAVE IT."** Each prototype gets one of
   three verdicts.
2. **Second principle: "Never fall in love with your prototype!"** "The simpler, faster,
   and cheaper we produce a prototype, the less it will hurt us when we have to reject
   it."
3. **Third principle: "It's a never-ending story: Prototyping means to iterate, iterate,
   and iterate still again."**

**The maturity rule.** "Over time, the degree of maturity of our prototypes will be higher,
and it will be more elaborate… The more sophisticated our prototype is, the more accurate
and meaningful the tests will be. The degree of maturity of the prototype depends on how
much time and money we can invest in it. Our intention, however, must be to drive the
prototype **only as far as necessary in order to attain our set goals**."

**The three decision criteria the test results feed.** Prototype results are "a basis for
decision making in order to make the right, balanced decisions in terms of **human
desirability, economic feasibility, and technical implementability**. Only once all these
criteria have intersected are we on the right path for generating a market opportunity.
**We always begin with the human being and his needs.**"

**Degree of resolution** — the axis that organizes the whole prototype catalogue:
- **low** = in an early phase
- **medium** = first approaches to a solution
- **high** = more end solutions

**Boxing and shelfing** — two techniques for making an offer and a whole portfolio
tangible and discussable (see Worked Example).

**The seven-step prototyping workshop** (see Reference Tables).

## Key Concepts
- **Materials are the method.** Physical prototypes "can be made from aluminum foil,
  paper, or Lego bricks; in the case of services, they can be expressed in the form of a
  role play." Digital prototypes "can be built, designed as videos, clickable
  presentations, or landing pages." Types combine — "integrating a smartphone into a
  cardboard box so it works as a display and thus serves as the prototype for augmented
  reality glasses."
- **The initial prototype**, defined: "characterized by the fact that it can be made using
  the simplest available materials and as quickly as possible" — cardboard, paper, plastic
  cups, string, tape.
- **Prototyping is possible at any time in the macro cycle** (Ch. 2). "The added value of
  individual functions, a new product, or the result of a customer interaction can be
  tested with a prototype."
- **Green and red feedback.** "What I like about the prototype is…" (green) / "I wish that
  the prototype…" (red). "This helps to maintain a positive basic mood and cultivate
  improvements."
- **Test in pairs.** "One team member can ask the questions, and the other makes
  observations."
- **Time boxing.** "Create prototypes under time pressure. More time does not yield more
  results. Time boxing boosts the pressure to get results."
- **No offer has intrinsic value.** "The value that customers ascribe to the offer is all
  that counts."
- **Operability is the quality bar.** "The simpler the prototype of an offer can be
  operated, the better."

## Mental Models
- **A prototype is a disprovable assumption.** Build it to be killed by a counterexample,
  not to be admired.
- **Resolution should trail the question, not the ambition.** Match maturity to the
  objective; over-building is a cost, not a virtue.
- **Think in variants.** Lilly "detaches herself from one variant and tries out something
  completely different once again — the first idea doesn't always have to be the best!"
- **The only way to build it wrong is to try to get it right first time.** "She can't do
  anything wrong when building her prototype — unless she wants to get it all right the
  very first time, which is impossible."
- **Boxing forces construction of understanding.** "It forces the team to construct their
  own understanding of the product in a very direct and visual manner."

## Anti-patterns
- Falling in love with the prototype — you will refuse to leave it.
- Building at high resolution in an early phase; the resolution scale exists to stop this.
- Prototyping without naming what is to be tested. "Always build prototypes on the basis
  of the question of what is to be tested."
- Treating prototyping as a phase at the end rather than a habit across the whole project:
  "Always schedule enough time for prototyping and testing, **across the entire duration of
  the project**."
- Leaving the eventual implementers out until handover. "Involve at an early stage the
  project team members who will implement the prototype in the end."

**The prototyping workshop — seven steps**

| Step | What happens |
|---|---|
| **1** | From the functions/initial solution scenarios, the team ponders **what functions are absolutely critical to a user** — those get built and tested. "What is important is that we implement something tangible and that an interaction with a potential user can come into being." |
| **2** | The team thinks about **which variant should be built**. |
| **3** | The team builds one or multiple prototypes. **"It is important to provide enough material for building the prototype."** |
| **4** | Prototyping in several groups gives internal feedback first — **green feedback** ("What I like about the prototype is…") and **red feedback** ("I wish that the prototype…"). |
| **5** | Prototypes **and the way of presenting them** are improved. "Concentrate on the essential features and solutions." |
| **6** | Prepare the testing carefully (Ch. 10). **Go out in pairs**; one asks, one observes. On return, all team members document and share findings. |
| **7** | Improve prototypes and/or discard variants. **"If none of the prototypes work, it is useful to obtain more facts and customer needs and adapt the prototypes accordingly."** New variants go back out for testing. |

**Three key questions when building a prototype** (Lilly's)
- What are the basic functions for the user?
- What hasn't she taken into consideration at all yet?
- How has nobody ever done it before?

## Worked Example — Boxing and shelfing IKEA's kitchen offer

**The boxing principle.** "The basic idea behind boxing is to create a physical box, which
can be used, for example, for the marketing of the product. Let's imagine a cereal box."
Each side carries information summarizing the benefits and characteristics of the product
and the brand.

Core questions in boxing:
- **Front** — what is the product name, image, slogan, and two to three promises about the
  product?
- **Back** — which details about the feature, application, and content are important?
- **Remaining sides — the WH questions**, answered by text or visualization:
  - **Who** is the target customer or user?
  - **Which** goals should be achieved? Which problems are solved?
  - **When** is the product available and how can we get it?
  - **Where** and under what circumstances is the product used?
  - **Why** should the user use the product?

Boxing generalizes: besides a product box, "a problem box, a solution box, a project box
(e.g. per project or working package), a process box (e.g. per process step) can be
created." "The added value of boxing is that the situation can be viewed from different
perspectives."

**The shelfing principle.** "When it comes to describing a whole portfolio, often a
structure is missing for the discussion." Sort everything into **three shelves: the
product, service, and solution shelves.** "Above the shelves, we write the categories that
the customer would most likely look for. Afterward we arrange the products, services, and
solutions accordingly." The payoff: "gaps in the offering portfolio, and also synergies,
can be detected quickly." Discussion attributes: attractiveness, novelty, strategy
contribution, differentiation.

**The book's filled-in example — "How does our offer for the perfect kitchen look?"**
IKEA's kitchen portfolio on three shelves:

| 1) Solution portfolio — *"We offer kitchens in…"* | 2) Product portfolio — *"Our products range from…"* | 3) Service portfolio — *"Our services include…"* |
|---|---|---|
| Country-style | Cupboards and tables | Planning, delivery, and installation |
| Urban for singles and families | Products available in stone, wood, and laminate look | Carried out by qualified installation partners or for self-assembly |
| Designs for small spaces | Refrigerators, hotplates, and ventilation equipment complement the offer | |

**And the paper-website prototype.** Lilly and Jonny are still dreaming of their design
thinking consultancy. Lilly outlines **every page of her Web site on paper**, then tests
"the mobile version of her Web site with users in an oversized version" made from paper —
iterating with potential customers "until they like it and are satisfied with its
navigation and the scope of its contents." She refines all the way up to the finished
design, **"which is only programmed at the end."**

**And the sensory mask.** *"How might a sensory augmentation mask for an exhibition look?
Build an initial prototype!"* Perception filter 1, perception filter 2 — "cardboard,
sticks, string, cups, scissors, glue… and we have a prototype that can be discussed."

## Key Takeaways
- When prototyping, start with the **need of the persona and a trend in the market**.
- Always build prototypes on the basis of **the question of what is to be tested**.
- No offer has intrinsic value; only the value customers ascribe to it counts — and make
  sure as many customers as possible ascribe value to it.
- Test in the real world as early as possible; prototypes are assumptions to be
  scrutinized.
- Use the material that is available. Create prototypes under time pressure.
- Match objective and maturity of the prototype.
- Schedule prototyping and testing across the entire project duration.
- Involve the eventual implementers early.
- Apply boxing and shelfing to test a potential portfolio while prototyping.
