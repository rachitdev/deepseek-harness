# Chapter 5 (Book §1.5) — How to Build Empathy with the User

## Core Idea
**Empathy is the ability and willingness to recognize and understand the thoughts,
emotions, motives, and personality traits of another person.** By definition, design
thinking is "an empathetic, optimistic, and creative way of working to shape the future."
The chapter's argument is that most products for a segment fail because the designers hold
a *category stereotype* rather than an *empathetic model* of the people in it — and it
proves the point on the seniors market, then generalizes into mindfulness practice, the
talking stick, and empathy in UX design.

Its evidence about retirees: they do not want to be called "generation 65+," "best agers,"
or a target group in the "silver markets." They do not want to book a trip for seniors or
be invited to "exercises for seniors." **They are not interested in illnesses. They want
to stay healthy and mobile, and in most cases they feel up to fifteen years younger than
they actually are.**

## Frameworks Introduced

**Mindfulness as the key to empathy — the 10 practices**
1. **Dare to change the perspective** — observe the world from the other side.
2. **Give full attention to a topic** — be mindful, present, and precise.
3. **Listen attentively and actively** — use looks, gestures, facial expressions.
4. **Reflect on your own behavior** — how do I come across to other people?
5. **Read signals** — what do the facial expressions, gestures and voice of your
   counterpart say?
6. **Question your readiness for empathy** — is my opinion without prejudice?
7. **Ask open questions** — "How might the future look?"
8. **Explore feelings and needs** — "How do you feel today?"
9. **Express your own feelings and needs** — "I wish…"
10. **Act with empathy** — "How can I help?"

**Types of empathy** — a ladder:
- **Cognitive empathy** — recognizing, in a first step, what another person feels.
- **Emotional empathy / emotional intelligence** — actually *feeling* what the other
  person feels; the ability to perceive others' emotional sensitivities and respond
  adequately. Increasingly important in product design, employee management and human
  relationships.
- **The strongest form** — suffering with the other person mentally and physically.

**The talking stick** — adopted from Northwest American indigenous cultures. In a meeting
the stick is given to one person, who explains their standpoint and keeps the stick *until
they feel the other participants have understood them*. Others listen or ask comprehension
questions; otherwise they remain silent.

Benefits: members improve their ability to listen; being understood boosts willingness to
compromise; ability to change perspective is nurtured; everybody gets a chance to speak
and to finish; only one person speaks at a time, which helps acoustic comprehension.
Trade-off: **initially this demands more time in a meeting** — the empathy gain arrives
once the practice is established.

**Three core properties of empathy in UX design** (derived from Tinder):
1. **Personal bond** — one picture per page and a simple interaction possibility ensures a
   personal, self-contained UX; the process is efficient, callable anywhere; more
   information about a match is one click away.
2. **Motivation** — a match comes about only if *both* searchers signal interest, so users
   quickly undergo moments of happiness; these have a strongly motivating impact and
   ensure long-term customer loyalty (the **hook effect**, Chapter 1).
3. **Trust** — the chat function strengthens users' trust in the app.

## Key Concepts
- **Understand the language of the customer.** Listen actively and ask about words that
  can be read several ways. "Resources" might mean time, material, or people — you can
  only know by having your interlocutor explain it.
- **Interrogate theatrics.** If someone calls a situation "incredibly exciting" while
  rolling their eyes, ask: *"We could see how you rolled your eyes. What did you mean?"*
  Better understanding of the customer's language and personality yields better
  understanding of their need.
- **The "pollo" effect** (*pollo* = Spanish for chicken). Nobody wants to ask naive
  questions that make people roll their eyes; nobody wants to look like a chicken. But
  asking them is often necessary to immerse yourself in the user's world — **there are no
  naive questions.**
- **The alien / apprentice stance.** Imagine you are an alien, an apprentice in this world:
  you have never been here, everything is new and inexplicable. As a friendly
  extraterrestrial you ask non-biased questions. Respondents open up more, "because the
  role of apprentice is not threatening to the interviewee; on the contrary, [your]
  curiosity motivates potential users to tell us even more."
- **Why empathy is hard now.** Achievement-oriented society exposes us to constant
  pressure; if the interlocutor seems strange, or we have already sorted them by our
  interpretations and conclusions, empathy is hard. Other influencing factors: a stressful
  everyday life, being wiped out, and emotional states such as anger, rage and fear.
  "Behind these emotions you usually find unfilled needs, unconscious beliefs, [and
  patterns]."
- **Mindfulness is a basic capability of the brain** that our ambition to multitask
  suppresses. Focused attention makes perception more precise and presence greater; it can
  be directed inward or outward; it is the basis for enhanced cognitive skills and a vital
  aspect of the design thinking mindset because it stimulates creativity while cultivating
  empathy and emotional intelligence. "The things we experience with great enthusiasm and
  attention, we internalize better." The framing question: **"Full mind or mindful?"**
- **Empathy buttons.** Facebook's five emoticons beyond "Like" (heart = love; smiley =
  funny; wide-eyed = surprise; angry; sad) turn binary Like data into granular emotional
  data. In UX design emoticons matter because many users find written comments too
  burdensome, so content fades on the net unrated; emoticons are easy to enter on smart
  watches and mobile devices and **increase the comment rate considerably**.
- **Simplicity as a need.** Until 2012 dating sites were long registration forms and
  profile pictures in grid design. For millennials (generation Y) that failed demands for
  flexibility and efficiency. Tinder's **swipe right / swipe left** changed the user
  experience; the idea emerged from complaints received about existing dating sites, and
  those users' needs drove the process and the feature selection. Most functions of
  conventional dating platforms were **omitted**, and interaction options reduced.

## Mental Models
- **Experience beats speculation.** "Instead of endlessly speculating on users' everyday
  situations, it is far [better to experience] them ourselves… but be careful: we
  experience only a fraction of them."
- **Our own patterns inhibit us**, because "we innovate not for ourselves."
- **Courage precedes insight.** "It takes courage and strength to step back from known
  standards and views of the world — but without it, needfinding and the empathy with a
  potential user it requires can hardly take place."
- **Simplicity convinces** — and love/passion for a product can convince just as hard in
  the opposite direction (see Lingscars below). Empathy is about resonance with a
  particular user, not about a universal aesthetic.

## Anti-patterns
- Designing for a demographic label ("silver market") instead of a person.
- Euphoric feature demos to a user who has shown a horrified look — pitching instead of
  listening.
- Suppressing naive questions to protect your own status.
- Assuming that because a segment is old it is not digital.

## Reference Tables

**Cognitive vs. emotional empathy**

| Stage | What it gives you | Design consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Cognitive empathy | Recognize what another person feels | Enough to describe a persona |
| Emotional empathy | Feel what the other person feels | Enough to prioritize which experience is critical |
| Strongest form | Suffer with the person mentally and physically | The stance needed for extreme contexts (e.g. the palliative care ward project) |

**Talking stick — costs and benefits**

| Benefit | Cost |
|---|---|
| Better listening; higher willingness to compromise; perspective change; everyone speaks and finishes; one speaker at a time | More meeting time, until the technique is established |

## Worked Example — Priya, the "ImedHeinz," and Anna at the Shady Pine Tree

**The prototype.** Priya builds a prototype smartphone for seniors with a simple interface
to a blood pressure measurement device. It is named the **"ImedHeinz."** It is "a little
clunky, due to the large keys and an analog interface to the blood pressure measurement
device. The enclosure for the smartphone is reminiscent of the smart pocket calculators
from the 1980s."

**The test.** Priya visits the **"Shady Pine Tree" retirement home** and, in the dining
room, meets **Anna**: 70 years old, mentally fit, in a wheelchair after a stroke, which is
why she moved from her townhouse into the home. Priya confronts Anna with the ImedHeinz.

**The result.** Anna's response is *a horrified look*. Priya, trying to generate
enthusiasm, euphorically demonstrates how quickly the blood pressure data transmits to the
Heinz. **Anna shows no enthusiasm whatsoever.**

**The observation that changes everything.** Priya leans back, and her gaze wanders across
the dining room:
- **Richard**, at the back of the room, is playing chess on his tablet.
- **Elizabeth** is exchanging WhatsApp messages with her grandson in New York City on an
  iPhone.
- **Anna** takes Priya's hand and says she is a great iPhone fan too, and that she is
  looking forward to her new, **gilded iPhone that would match her jewelry so well.**

**The lesson.** The design brief Priya inferred from the label "seniors" — large keys,
medical function, 1980s enclosure — was a stereotype. The actual jobs-to-be-done in that
room are play, family connection, and *jewelry-grade aesthetics*. The prerequisite for
empathetic needfinding is immediate proximity to the customers plus "the readiness to
engage with your interlocutor and to try to experience the world through another person's
eyes."

**The counter-example — Lingscars.com.** The book's deliberate opposite of simplicity.
**Ling Valentine** built a car-leasing start-up in the UK; she runs the business (she was
far more capable of operating it than her husband). Her site "radiates powerful colors,
different fonts, and unique [design]," draws hundreds of thousands of new users a month,
was called by *Management Today* **"the most cluttered Web site we've ever seen"** — and
won awards, including recognition for the sheer number of visitors. She lives on viral
marketing and word of mouth, deals with customers directly, and stages stunts (a Chinese
military truck equipped with a huge rocket, a daily attraction for drivers on the
highway). She passes her marketing savings on 1:1 to customers as attractive conditions.
**Emotional relationship, not minimalism, is what her users are hired for.**

## Key Takeaways
- Empathy is a prerequisite, not a nicety; without it you can "deliver a brilliant
  performance with a major flop on the market."
- Never accept the segment label as the design brief. Go to the dining room.
- Be the apprentice/alien; ask the naive question; there are no naive questions.
- Practice mindfulness deliberately — it is the substrate empathy grows on.
- Introduce the talking stick knowing it costs time before it pays.
- In digital products, empathy shows up as emoticon-level emotional data, simplicity, and
  designed moments of happiness (the hook effect).
