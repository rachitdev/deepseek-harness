# Chapter 2 (Book §1.2) — Why Is Process Awareness Key?

## Core Idea
Design thinking has two nested loops. The **micro cycle** is the familiar phase sequence
(Understand → Observe → Define point of view → Ideate → Prototype → Test → Reflect),
run many times. The **macro cycle** is the seven-step arc of an entire innovation project
in which many micro cycles are chained — a deliberately divergent sequence of prototypes
(brainstorm → critical functions → benchmarks → dark horse → funky → **vision prototype**
→ concretization), with the **groan zone** as the pivot between divergence and convergence.

Process awareness means knowing *which* loop you are in, *where* in it you stand, and
therefore which mindset and which tools apply right now. The single hardest judgment call
in the whole discipline is **when to stop diverging**.

## Frameworks Introduced

**The double diamond** — problem space then solution space; each has a divergent
(differentiate) and a convergent (integrate) half. Divergent thinking → convergent
thinking, twice.

**The design thinking micro cycle (the book's canonical seven phases)**

1. **Understand** — the starting point is not a goal but a *persona* with a need or a
   problem. Define the problem statement at the right level of comfort. Two question
   types move the frame: **"WHY?" expands** the creative framework, **"HOW?" narrows** it.
   Answer the six WH questions (who, why, what, when, where, how) for the overall context.
2. **Observe** — mostly qualitative. Document with idea boards, vision boards, daily story
   from photos, mind maps, mood pictures, photos of life situations and people.
3. **Define point of view** — draw upon, interpret and *weight* all findings; the
   facilitator gets every member to talk about their experience so a common knowledge base
   forms. Best done by telling stories that were actually experienced, showing pictures,
   describing people's reactions and emotions.
4. **Ideate** — brainstorming and sketching; goal is as many different concepts as
   possible, visualized. Creativity should increase *step by step per iteration*.
5. **Prototype** — make ideas tangible as early as possible. Motto for the options for
   action: **"Love it, change it, or leave it."**
6. **Test** — after every prototype and every drafted sketch; with colleagues if you must,
   with potential users if you want anything intriguing.
7. **Reflect** — a retrospective on the process and the last iteration. Use an
   **"I like — I wish"** feedback cycle or a structured **feedback capture grid**, and a
   **retrospective board**. Update the personas from what you learned.

**Process variants the book catalogues** (all pursue the same goal with different terms):

| Source | Phases |
|---|---|
| HPI (Hasso Plattner Institute) / most universities | Six steps — the standard depiction Lilly identifies with |
| d.school (Stanford) | Merges "Understand" and "Observe" into **"empathy"** |
| Chair for Global Information Technology, a technical college | Four steps: Empathy — Analysis — Prototype — Co-Creation |
| **IDEO** (original) | **Understand — Observe — Visualize — Evaluate/Optimize — Implement**: understand the task, market, clients, technology, limiting conditions, restrictions, optimization criteria; observe and analyze real people in real situations; visualize first solution drafts (3D, simulation, graphics, drawings); evaluate and optimize prototypes in fast succession with continuous repetitions; implement the new concept in reality (the most time-consuming phase) |
| **Swisscom** (Swiss ICT company) | Simplified three-phase micro cycle: **Hear — Create — Deliver** |
| **ISO 13407** | Planning/process — Analysis/use context — Specifications/user requirements — Prototype (draft of design variants) — Evaluation (of solutions and requirements) |
| **Scrum** | Sprints of 1–4 weeks; product backlog → sprint backlog; requirements as user stories; a tested, ready-to-deliver product at the end of each sprint; process improved in the Retrospective |

**The Swisscom "Hear — Create — Deliver" cycle with its basic tools**

| Phase | Description | Basic tools |
|---|---|---|
| **Hear** | Understand the project; understand the customer problem/need; procure information, internal and external; gather experience directly from the customer | Design challenge; customer interview |
| **Create** | Transform what was learned into potential solutions; generate multiple solutions and possibilities; define solution features; concretize ideas | Core beliefs; target customer experience chain; **NABC** (Need, Approach, Benefit, Competition) |
| **Deliver** | Create and test prototypes; verify, expedite, or reject ideas; gain insights and learn from them | Prototyping plan; self-validation |

## Key Concepts

- **The groan zone** — the transition from the divergent to the convergent phase. The
  phases can be changed at any time; experienced facilitators and innovation champions
  recognize the moment and steer the team into convergence deliberately. Recurs
  throughout the book (see §2.5 on facilitation).
- **Reality distortion field** — Bud Tribble's term at Apple for **Steve Jobs's** ability
  to master the mental leap out of divergence at exactly the right moment. Named after the
  original Star Trek episode "The Menagerie," in which aliens create their own world by
  thought. Jobs "was a master when it came to managing the groan zone optimally."
- **Time pressure as a mindset lever** — "One good influencer that helps us change our
  mindset is a limited period of time." Pull the deadline or the first-prototype date
  forward and the mindset changes automatically.
- **Lay down functionalities early, reuse them at the transition.** Fix candidate
  functionalities and characteristics in an early phase; at the switch to convergence,
  match the mass of ideas against them. That match alone eliminates some ideas and lets
  others be consolidated or clustered.
- **Outsource the vote.** Present remaining ideas to other groups and participants and let
  the community allocate Post-its — "if we only involve our own group, the decision is
  often not objective enough because we always risk falling in love with a certain idea."
- **What to do with rejected ideas** — the book leaves it open: some facilitators have
  participants throw the Post-its on the ground; others keep them as a knowledge reservoir
  until the end of the project.
- **MVP inside the macro cycle** — the functional prototype that carries only the critical
  functionalities is "frequently referred to as minimal viable product (MVP)"; MVPs are
  the foundation you build on, step by step, toward a finished prototype.

## Mental Models
- **"WHY? expands, HOW? narrows."** The single most portable move in the book. Repeatedly
  asking "Why?" expands the creative framework; asking "How?" scales it down. Used to set
  the problem statement at the right level (see Chapter 3).
- **In divergence the number of ideas is infinite.** The skill is not producing ideas; it
  is wrapping up divergence at the right time and focusing on the functionalities that
  lead to an optimal user solution.
- **Chance is not a strategy.** Yes, all sorts of ideas get launched and chance
  contributes to success — "but chance does not often work this way; hence, in the
  process, converging is decisive for success."
- **Prototype maturity ratchets.** "The maturity of the prototypes increases with every
  individual step" — never restart from zero at each iteration.

## Anti-patterns
- Staying in divergence because it is comfortable and fun; the "cavalcade of ideas" never
  becomes a solution.
- Converging inside your own group only — you fall in love with your own idea.
- Treating the seven macro steps as a linear plan rather than as an escalation of creative
  risk that can be re-entered at any time.
- Skipping the Reflect phase; the retrospective is where personas get updated.

## Reference Tables

**The design thinking macro cycle — 7 steps, with the book's own timeboxes**

| # | Step | Purpose | Timebox | Key questions |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Initial brainstorming** | Let the group get all sorts of ideas off its chest and learn how the others think | 20 min | Which ideas come to mind spontaneously? Which solution approaches do others pursue? What can we do differently? Do we all have the same understanding of the problem statement? |
| 2 | **Critical functionalities** | Identify and rank the "important things" the user must have | 1–2 h; draft and test 10–20 critical functions | Which functionalities are mandatory? What experience is absolutely necessary for the user? What is the relationship between function and experience? |
| 3 | **Benchmarks from other industries and experiences** | Break a team that cannot tear itself away from an original concept | 30 min brainstorm + 30 min finding benchmarks + 30 min clustering/combining; then build 2–3 prototypes | Which successful concepts and experiences can be applied to the problem? Which experiences illuminate the problem from another perspective? What is the relation between the problem and other experiences? |
| 4 | **Dark horse** | Lift the borders; strive for maximum success and accept maximum risk | 50 min to build, plus prototyping time | Which radical possibilities have not been considered? Which experiences lie outside anything imaginable? Are there products/services that would expand value creation? |
| 5 | **Funky prototype** | Crank creativity one more notch; maximize learning, minimize time/attention cost; remove all cost and budget restrictions | 1 h | What crazy ideas are super cool? For which idea would you have to ask forgiveness in the end? What does an idea look like that is realized ad hoc and unplanned? |
| 6 | **Vision prototype** | The groan zone — first combination of everything learned | ~2 h; test with **at least three** potential users, capture feedback in detail | Does the vision generate enough attention that a potential user absolutely wants this solution? Does it leave leeway for a user's dreams? Is the value offer convincing? What else would users wish for to make the experience perfect? |
| 7 | **Concretize the vision step by step** | Convergent elaboration of the selected idea | iterative | (see sub-steps below) |

**Dark horse prompts the book gives:** "How would you design an IT service desk without IT
problems?" · "What does a windshield wiper look like without a windshield?" · "What would a
cemetery look like if no one died?" The point is to leave the comfort zone and "do it in
any case, no matter what will occur."

**What the vision prototype combines (step 6):** prior knowledge (*caution is advisable
here*) · best initial ideas · most important critical functionalities · new ideas from
other industries and experiences · initial user experience · intriguing insights (e.g.
from the dark horse) · the simplest possible solution.

**Step 7 sub-stages**

| Stage | What it is | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| **A. Functional prototype** | Concentrate on the critical variables and test them intensively with potential users | Critical functions must be created for critical experiences. Not all functionalities are integrated at the onset — ensure *minimum* functionality so the prototype can be tested under real conditions. These are the **MVPs** |
| **B. Finished prototype** | Crucial for interaction with the user, "because only reality yields truth" | Schedule enough time; integrate the respective functionalities |
| **C. Final prototype** | Excels by the elegance of the thoughts invested in it as well as in its realization | Prototypes convincing with simple functionality are usually also successful on the market. Obtain support from suppliers and partners; using standard components raises the likelihood of success and massively reduces development costs |
| **D. Implementation plan — "how to bring it home"** | Not only product quality is decisive, but its implementation | Know who might obstruct the process and try to influence decisions. Credo: **turn those affected into people who are involved** and create a win-win for all parties (elaborated in Chapter 21) |

**Prototype vision canvas** — used once a prototype has a certain maturity, so that
several visions can be formulated and compared side by side. Fields: *Vision statement;
Target group; Needs; Product; Benefit.*

## Worked Example — Running the macro cycle end to end

A team is given a wicked design challenge. The facilitator does *not* start convergent.

- **20 minutes**: everyone writes ideas on Post-its, says each idea aloud while writing,
  sticks it on the pin board. Purpose is quantity and calibration — the members' levels of
  knowledge about the problem differ wildly, and this exposes that.
- **90 minutes**: the group drafts and tests 10–20 *critical functions*, ranked in the
  context of a critical user experience. This is the step the facilitator must protect —
  the ranking created here is what will later kill ideas objectively in the groan zone.
- **90 minutes**: benchmark round. (a) brainstorm ideas relating to the problem;
  (b) brainstorm industries and/or experiences. Take the three best from each, combine,
  and build two to three physical prototypes to test with the user.
- **50 minutes**: the dark horse. The facilitator removes an element that seemed
  constitutive ("a service desk without IT problems") to force the team out of the
  comfort zone.
- **60 minutes**: the funky prototype. All cost and budget restrictions are lifted.
- **~2 hours**: the vision prototype. Prior knowledge, the best early ideas, the ranked
  critical functionalities, the cross-industry ideas, the first user experience, the dark
  horse insights and the simplest possible solution are fused into one artifact, then
  tested with **at least three** potential users, with feedback captured in detail. Ideally
  those same users — and any known **lead users**, who are highly motivated to satisfy
  their own needs — are then carried into the concretization phase as references.
- **Then convergence**: functional prototype (MVP on critical variables) → finished
  prototype → final prototype → implementation plan.

The whole arc is one macro cycle; every bullet above contains several micro cycles.

## Key Takeaways
- Define the problem statement on the right level; **leave the comfort zone as often as
  possible if you want radical innovations to emerge**.
- Develop awareness of the groan zone — it is decisive for the future success of the
  project.
- Divergence is cheap and infinite; the discipline is the timed exit.
- Fix the critical functionalities early so convergence has an objective filter.
- Test the vision prototype with at least three users before concretizing.
- Reflect after every iteration and feed the result back into the personas.
