# Chapter 7 (Book §1.7) — How to Generate Ideas

## Core Idea
"Without ideas, no new products!" But groundbreaking ideas don't reliably emerge in a
brainstorming session — "sometimes the creative spark leaps while you have a shower or
scribble something on a napkin." Creative companies therefore grant leeway (workdays where
employees may do whatever they want, on the single condition that they report back). Since
most of us cannot tell the boss we'll spend four hours in the shower, we need **methods
and tools of structured ideation** — and the chapter's real subject is the *facilitation
craft* that makes them work: mood, hierarchy suppression, the brain dump, contests, and
the distinction between **requirements and ideas**.

Lilly's credo, which she finds "too banal and childish" but believes anyway:
**"A good mood is the #1 prerequisite."**

## Frameworks Introduced

**The top three brainstorming rules**
1. **Creative confidence** — express every idea that comes into your head, no matter how
   silly. "Maybe the next person can base another idea exactly on our 'silly'
   contribution." Requires the relaxed atmosphere.
2. **Quantity goes before quality** — "very, very important." Fill the hat with as many
   ideas as possible; evaluation comes later. **Resist the temptation of being satisfied
   with the first good idea** — a better one may be five minutes away.
3. **No criticism of ideas** — under no circumstances during this phase; evaluation
   happens later in a separate step.

**The four levels of idea depth** (the ditch model — see Worked Example):
1. What is the problem?
2. The brainstorming question.
3. Possible solution ideas.
4. Idea variants.

**The problem reversal technique.** Lilly's favorite when students have no desire to join
in: reverse the question — *"How would you prevent creativity on your team?"* Then, in a
second step, reverse every negative statement into a positive one. **Trade-off:** less
suitable for finding new product ideas, because the reversed question "What would
something have to be like?" produces a *requirements list* instead of ideas. Works well for
the revision and improvement of **service processes**.

**Quick 'n' dirty prototyping as a creativity technique.** Building an idea as a physical
model. **"The diversity of the provided material determines whether more ideas will emerge
or not. The more odds and ends are available, the better."** A discovered balloon summons
the idea that something could be flexible and stretchable; a piece of cord reminds someone
that the thing might be portable.

**SCAMPER** — a further development of the Osborn checklist (Alex Osborn, brainstorming
expert, with Sidney Parnes, developed one of the first creative problem-solving
approaches). Along with brainstorming it supplies a question list as food for thought.
Usable for products, processes, systems, solutions, services, business models, or
ecosystems. **If individual questions are unsuitable or non-obvious, simply leave them
out.** Method note: "it is important initially to see an example and then go through the
detailed questions."

## Key Concepts
- **Warm up until they laugh.** "Before beginning with any brainstorming, people must
  laugh at least once. A warmup that makes participants smile helps… it's best when they
  smile at one another."
- **Hierarchy is a hindrance.** "An apprentice does not want to make a peculiar impression
  on his boss when expressing a fanciful idea." Countermeasures: state explicitly that the
  assistant, the accountant, the CEO and the marketing officer can each contribute; if
  participants don't know each other, **so much the better — skip the introductions that
  announce who has which role.** "A nonbiased dialog is of great value."
- **The trainee-only team.** With a steep hierarchy, try the reverse approach: form a team
  only from trainees so they can raise their profile and show their creative potential. "In
  the next workshop, the groups will then almost certainly mix at their own initiative."
- **The brain dump.** Peter's opening session: colleagues who arrive with a fixed idea of
  the solution cannot be pulled away from it and generate little new. So he gives everyone
  a session to *dump* their existing ideas first, which opens them to new things.
- **Change the room to change the mood.** "If the meeting takes place week after week in
  the same conference room that is associated with some boring statistics, it is not
  conducive to a good atmosphere. So why not move the workshop to another room, outside,
  or even to the closest bar?"
- **Requirements ≠ ideas.** The chapter's most-used diagnostic (see Reference Tables).
- **Every dumb idea has potential** — "when we know how to reverse the idea successfully
  into something positive, we will gain valuable perspectives with a guaranteed novelty
  value."

## Mental Models
- **The idea-count contest as a diagnostic, not a game.** Stopping at halftime and asking
  each group for its idea count creates catch-up pressure that forces the laggards toward
  wilder ideas — *and* reveals which group is stuck. "Usually, it turns out this group
  has — against instructions — begun to discuss and evaluate the ideas."
- **The brainstorming question sets the size of the solution space.** "What could we lay
  across the ditch?" vs. "How can one overcome a physical barrier such as a ditch?" — the
  formulation "largely determines how many ideas can be generated."
- **When a team is stuck at level 2, make them build.** A physical model forces
  concreteness and pulls them into level 3.

## Anti-patterns
- Introducing everyone by role before ideation — it re-imposes hierarchy.
- Evaluating during divergence (the tell: a group with a very low idea count).
- Accepting requirements as ideas ("ergonomic," "lightweight," "cheaper").
- Being satisfied with the first good idea.
- Using problem reversal to search for new product ideas — you'll get a spec list.

## Reference Tables

**Requirements vs. ideas — Lilly's headset diagnosis**

Problem posed: *How might we communicate in the future without cell phones?*

| What participants wrote | Who wrote it | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| "ergonomic," "lightweight," "user-friendly" | Participants from technical areas | **Requirements** — they do not entail a solution to the problem |
| "cheaper," "cutting-edge design" | Participants from business administration | **Requirements** |
| Electronics implanted under the skin to communicate worldwide | — | **An idea** |
| Integrate communication into accessories and clothing (e.g. Google Glass) | — | **A less abstract idea** |

**Two facilitator tricks for breaking "deadly serious professionalism"**

| Trick | Mechanism | What it reveals |
|---|---|---|
| Halftime idea count across groups | Competitive incentive pushes undermatched groups toward wilder ideas | Which group is stuck — and almost always because it started discussing and evaluating |
| "Present your two best and two dumbest solutions" | Giggles create a positive atmosphere; then a debate starts on whether the "dumb" ideas are actually dumb | Reversible dumb ideas with guaranteed novelty value |

**SCAMPER — the full question list**

| Letter | Prompt questions |
|---|---|
| **S — Substitute** | What can be substituted? What can be used in its place? Who can be involved instead? Which process could be used instead? What other material could be used instead? |
| **C — Combine** | What can be combined? What can be mixed? How might certain parts be connected? Which purposes could be combined? |
| **A — Adapt** | What other ideas are suggested by it? Is there anything that is similar [and adaptable]? Have there been similar situations in the past? |
| **M — Modify** | What modification could be introduced? Can the meaning be changed? How might the color or shape be changed? What can be increased? What can be reduced? What could be modernized? Can it be enlarged? Can it be downsized? |
| **P — Put to other uses** | For what other purposes could it be used? |
| **E — Eliminate** | (what can be removed) |
| **R — Rearrange** | (what order can be changed or inverted) |

The book's worked SCAMPER example on a **vehicle**: *Substitute* → autonomous vehicle,
artificial intelligence instead of human intelligence. *Rearrange* → folding vehicle.
*Eliminate* → 360° turn instead of reverse gear.

## Worked Example — The ditch: four levels of idea depth

Imagine standing in front of a ditch, wanting to get to the other side.

**Level 1 — What is the problem?**
A ditch cuts off this side from the other side. The problem is that we must get to the
opposite side somehow. Brainstorming question: *How can we get to the other side?*
Answers like **"safely," "in one piece," "dry"** come back. **These are not ideas — they
are requirements for the solution, and they don't help us in this situation.**

**Level 2 — The brainstorming question.**
The formulation is crucial and largely determines how many ideas can be generated.
Compare:
- *"What could we lay across the ditch to get to the other side?"* — restricts and
  channels the solution space.
- *"How can one overcome a physical barrier such as a ditch?"* — expands it.

**Level 3 — Possible solution ideas.**
"Fly." · "Build a bridge." · "Beam ourselves." · "Fill the ditch with so much material
that we can walk across it."

**Level 4 — Idea variants.**
Any number of variants can evolve from each level-3 idea. A second brainstorming session
on *"How many ways are there to fly?"* yields: **"With an airplane," "with a jet pack,"
"with wings," "like in the Red Bull ad," "by [balloon]"**…

**The escape hatch:** if a group finds it hard to get away from level 2, have them build
rudimentary models — and make it *mandatory* that requirements be translated into
something physical. "Implementing a physical model… forces the participants [to be
concrete and] engages them in level 3."

**Tips for depth of ideas (the book's own):**
- Formulate the brainstorming question according to the solution space you want to open up.
- You can still adapt the brainstorming question during the workshop.
- Level-3 solutions can be [put into a morphological box]; more variants of partial
  solutions follow from that.
- If a group has a hard time advancing, translating the ideas into a physical model is
  helpful.

**The rubber dog in the prototyping material box.** More or less by accident, Lilly threw
a rubber toy dog into the prototyping box. When participants were tasked with translating
their ideas into physical models, one of them found the toy and was highly amused. He
started to spin ideas — "The dog could do this and that in the machine" — whereupon his
team members joined in and came up with more. The team had a lot of fun with the dog,
which enabled them to break out of their habitual thought patterns and reflect on things
they had thought little about. **Until the very end, the dog contributed materially to the
successful outcome. Since then it has been an integral part of Lilly's prototyping box.**

## Key Takeaways
- Get a laugh before you get an idea; mood is the precondition, not the decoration.
- Suppress hierarchy actively: no role introductions, or all-trainee teams.
- Brain-dump the fixed ideas before searching for new ones.
- Police the requirements/ideas distinction relentlessly.
- Quantity before quality; no criticism; creative confidence.
- Formulate the brainstorming question to size the solution space, and adapt it live.
- Stock the prototyping box with maximum diversity of odds and ends.
