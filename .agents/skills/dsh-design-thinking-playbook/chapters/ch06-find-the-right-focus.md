# Chapter 6 (Book §1.6) — How to Find the Right Focus (Point of View)

## Core Idea
Determining the **point of view (PoV)** is the hardest step in design thinking. It is the
**synthesis** step: collecting, structuring and weighting all insights, identifying
contradictions, and condensing them into **one sentence** that sets the question for the
coming ideation phase. And when the solution must serve not one group but a multitude of
users, the way to get there is a deliberate **360° view** — enumerating stakeholders *and*
non-stakeholder perspectives, then asking each one its own question.

Critical caveat: **"Every PoV sentence is a starting point that will be adapted in the
next iteration lap."**

## Frameworks Introduced

**The four-step PoV procedure (A–D)**

| Step | What you do |
|---|---|
| **A. Analyze information** | Collect, interpret and analyze all information. Summarize and consolidate key findings into insights |
| **B. Infer insights** | Summarize the **10 most important insights**. Infer the design principles or problem clusters from them |
| **C. Formulate possible questions** | Mark possible key themes or questions (e.g. **dot voting** on insights and principles). Choose **three thematic areas** and formulate the question |
| **D. Specify question** | Present a question; refine it |

**The 360° view.** Consider the problem from as many perspectives as possible. A rough
split between **stakeholders** and **other points of view**. The number of possible points
of view is infinite. If a **stakeholder map** already exists (Chapter 21, book p. 258), use
it as the starting point.

**The 9-window tool.** A grid of the dimensions **system** × **time**.
- **System** — the structure of a product or service including its entire environment.
  Zoom *in* to the **subsystem** or zoom *out* to the **super system**.
- **Time** — past / today / future.
It "helps us overcome barriers and see the product or digital service from a different
point of view."

**Daisy map.** Depicts the most important elements as flower petals. Advantage: **items
are highlighted without an automatic ranking — five to eight petals are equal, as it
were.** Use it instead of a list, so the top item isn't automatically privileged. (Often
elements are first prioritized by scoring, then one or several are chosen for the PoV.)

**Three PoV sentence templates** (see Reference Tables).

## Key Concepts
- **Synthesis defined**: "finding the important needs and patterns of users, including
  those that were undiscovered up to now. The result of our synthesis is one condensed
  sentence, the PoV, which determines the question for the coming ideation phase."
- **What the PoV phase focuses on:** recognize patterns in user needs · see opportunities
  where others see problems · understand customer needs at all levels · provide clarity
  about assumptions and hypotheses · immerse in systems and make them tangible ·
  consolidate and interpret information · understand findings and emphasize the most
  important insights · create the starting point and focus for the next ideation.
- **Empathy shapes the questions, not just the community.** Questions should prompt
  interviewees to put themselves into different situations and consider them from
  different points of view.
- **Anonymity is required for negatives.** "Anonymity is not absolutely necessary for
  collecting great ideas — after all, it's fun to talk about things that are pleasing. But
  if you want to know what might bother people, anonymity is a must."
- **Digital vs. physical ideation.** For a working environment a physical workshop is
  advisable "because creativity might suffer in a digital workshop, although feedback can
  be collected quickly in the latter."
- **Other structuring methods the book names**: Venn diagrams, mind maps, system maps,
  cluster analyses, customer journeys.

## Mental Models
- **The reverse point of view.** Deliberately ask "What would be the worst version of
  this?" It surfaces constraints that positive questions never reach.
- **Impossible perspectives are legitimate perspectives.** Another planet (Venus), another
  culture (the family of the Russian Tsar), another time (the Middle Ages) — each is
  chosen for what it *yields*: kitsch and utopian fantasies; cross-cultural inspiration;
  great down-to-earthiness.
- **Zoom out before you zoom in.** The 9-window's super-system row is where market-level
  disruption becomes visible (fiat exchange → autonomous decentralized monetary system).
- **Always develop several PoV questions and choose among them** — never accept the first.

## Anti-patterns
- Jumping to ideation without synthesis; the team then ideates against unweighted findings.
- Presenting needs as a ranked list, which makes the top item win by position rather than
  merit (use the daisy map).
- Asking a friendly audience what they dislike, non-anonymously — you get only the ideas
  they think you want to hear.
- Treating the PoV as final rather than as the input to the next iteration.

**PoV sentence templates**

| Approach | Fill-in-the-blank | Example given |
|---|---|---|
| **How might we** | "How might we help [the user/customer] to achieve [a certain goal]?" or "How many ways are there to achieve [a certain target] for [the user]?" | *How might we help patients keep their health records safe and share them with a doctor at a given time?* |
| **Stanford PoV** | "[User] needs to [need] because [surprising insight]." or "[Who] wants [what] for [need fulfillment] because [motivation]." | *The patient must have the data sovereignty for his health data because he wants to avoid abuse.* |
| **Agile / user stories** | "As a [role/persona] ('who') I would like to [action, destination, wish] ('what'), in order to achieve [benefit] ('why')." | — |

## Worked Example — Lilly and Jonny's wedding party, in full

**Problem statement:** *Lilly and Jonny don't know yet how their wedding party should
look.*

**Derived question:** *"What should Lilly and Jonny's wedding party look like?"*

**Seed observation for the 360° view:** "Suppliers and the budget, for instance, are
important when you plan a wedding."

**The defined points of view, with the intention behind each** — defining the *intention*
is what makes the perspective shareable with other participants:

| Point of view | Explanation of the point of view |
|---|---|
| The couple | Lilly and Jonny stand at the center |
| The parents of the couple | They are very close to their children |
| The witnesses to the marriage | They are close friends of the couple |
| Children | Many families with children are invited |
| Seniors | Many seniors are invited |
| Money: with a lot of money | So dreams can be formulated |
| Money: without money | So the little things that cost nothing surface |
| On another planet: Venus, the planet of love | For some kitsch and utopian fantasies |
| In another culture: the family of the Russian Tsar | Inspiration from other cultures |
| At another time: the Middle Ages | For great down-to-earthiness |
| **Reverse point of view** | To find out what the worst scenario would be |

**One question per point of view** — each forces the respondent into that perspective:

| Point of view | Question |
|---|---|
| The couple | What does the couple wish for the wedding party? |
| The parents of the couple | What do the parents of the couple wish for the wedding party? |
| The witnesses to the marriage | What do the witnesses wish for the wedding party? |
| Children | What do children wish for a wedding party? |
| Seniors | What do seniors wish for a wedding party? |
| Planet Venus | What would a wedding party on Venus look like? |
| Family of the Russian Tsar | What would a wedding party in the family of the Russian Tsar look like? |
| The Middle Ages | What did a wedding party in medieval times look like? |
| Reverse | What would be the worst wedding party? |

**How they collect answers.** Lilly and Jonny won't run a workshop; they'll collect answers
over a shared dinner with friends and via social media/e-mail — but they run *part of the
ideation anonymously*, in writing or with an online tool, precisely so people will say what
bothers them.

**What anonymity bought them.** To *"What would be the worst wedding party?"*:
- Some friends **fear having to sit at the same table with the same people all night long.**
- Some are **horrified at having to wear a suit all day long.**
- **Many families would like to stay overnight locally but cannot afford an expensive
  hotel.**

None of these would have surfaced from the positive questions, and all three are design
constraints: seating rotation, dress code, and affordable local accommodation.

## Key Takeaways
- Find the point of view with a 360° view.
- Look at the situation from different points of view and define the focus for the next
  iteration.
- Use the 9-window tool to explore what happens before and after the use of the product, as
  well as what is happening in the system.
- Present the needs not as a list but as a **daisy map**.
- Change perspective deliberately: "time" (before, after), "money" (with, without), etc.
- Use a fill-in-the-blank PoV text in different variants, changed to suit project,
  maturity, and preferences.
- Start a project with simple WH questions: "How might we…?" or "How many different ways
  are there for…?"
- Always develop various PoV questions and choose the most suitable one.
