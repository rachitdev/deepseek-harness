# Chapter 14 (Book §2.4) — How to Design a Good Story

## Core Idea
"Good stories have accompanied humanity for thousands of years. In olden times, the
profession of storyteller used to exist… **Pretty late it was discovered — and accordingly
used — that products and objects can tell great stories as well.**"

The chapter's philosophical anchor is **Gaetano Pesce**, "one of the icons of Pop Art
design," who "once remarked that **we are separated from objects as long as consuming them
is the only primary reason for their existence.**"

From that follows the chapter's central claim about who designs meaning:

> "Until the 1960s, design was defined by the actual function… It was only in the 1970s
> that some designers challenged this paradigm. They added **'nonfunctional features'** and
> artistic elements and ornaments to their objects… **Without this deeper meaning, a product
> is not perfected in the eyes of the consumer. This means that the consumers became the
> actual designers. They create the meaning of an object through the intimate relationship
> they establish with it.**"

Lilly's observation of the same effect: her younger students "have such a close
relationship with their smartphones that **they give them pet names**."

The commercial stake, per a *Forbes* headline the book cites: **"good storytelling can
heighten customer acquisition by up to 400%."**

## Frameworks Introduced

**The five elements of a good story.** "A good story or a story that is told perfectly
always follows a typical narrative. In so doing, **an arc of suspense** is created, so
listeners remain attentive. This arc of suspense is essential and is built up continuously
from the first moment — right up to the final punch line."
1. **An emotionally significant initial situation.**
2. **A (likable) main character.**
3. **Conflicts and hindrances**, which the main character must overcome.
4. **A recognizable development and change** — the "before and after" effect.
5. **A climax**, including the conclusion or **the moral of the story**.

"Good stories induce emotions in the viewer and convey a message. **To be able to tell a
good story, we must know our target group quite well.** Again, it is of great importance to
have built up empathy beforehand."

**Three types of stories important in the perception of products** (see Reference Tables).

**The Golden Circle (Simon Sinek) — "Start with WHY!"** (see Reference Tables). "Communicate
from the inside to the outside!"

**The Minsky suitcase** — a tool for generating emotional stories (see Worked Example).

**"Wearable dreams"** — the dream-story interview protocol (see Reference Tables).

**Pictures of the Future (Siemens)** — a three-step method that "links realistic current
trends with distant future scenarios to align and direct business activities."
- **Step 1 — "We extrapolate from the world of today."** Start with daily business, look at
  trends, extrapolate the near future. Analyze industry reports and expert interviews;
  "the fastest way to reach our goal is to fall back upon the known trends in an industry,
  like internal trend reports and market analyses… For example, we take the general
  **Gartner Technology Hype Cycle** as a starting point." Compile a provisional list of
  trends, discuss briefly on the team, and note **estimated importance, strength of impact,
  and degree of maturity** of the relevant industry.
- **Step 2 — "We apply strategic visioning."** "We detach ourselves completely from our own
  business focus and our own professional blindness and design various distant future
  scenarios **with a true outside-in perspective**, independent of our own company (**in the
  example, four scenarios have turned out to be ideal**)." Siemens has published Pictures of
  the Future free of charge for many sectors — *energy, digitization, industry, automation,
  mobility, health, finance*. Choose "a positive, constructive, and profitable scenario"
  and ask: **"How might our company make a maximum contribution to this scenario? What would
  we have to do and offer?"** — "We stay in the future in our thoughts and **do not allow
  the processes and structures of our company today to influence us.**"
- **Step 3 — "We 'retropolate' from the world of tomorrow."** "Draw conclusions for the
  present from the 'known' facts of the future scenario. We juxtapose the results from step
  1 with those of step 2, combine them, and infer… what it means, in very specific terms,
  for the alignment and direction of our company today: **In which directions should we
  innovate and do research? What skills must be developed? What personnel should be hired?
  And how should processes be redesigned** so we are prepared for coming challenges and
  opportunities?"

The diagram runs **today → short-term → medium-term → long-term**, with "Extrapolate through
roadmaps" from *ongoing business (products, technologies, customer requirements)* meeting
"Retropolate from scenarios" — *new markets, new customer requirements, new technologies,
new business opportunities* — against **Scenarios for strategic business fields (SBF #1…#4…)**
and the **influencing factors**: *Individual, Society, Politics, Economy, Environment,
Technology, Customers, Competition.*

**Empathic design** — "the development of products and services that are based on **unspoken
customer needs**."

## Key Concepts
- **Desire is a relationship, not an object.** "With all services and products, the ultimate
  art is to **maintain the desire** for them, which is based mostly on the relationship
  between the object and the consumers. **Desire is exactly the condition that the object or
  product does not have** … it is the **presumed relationship** with the object that
  satisfies desire."
- **Daydreaming as a buying stage.** "Any product with hitherto unknown features has the
  potential to fill us with enthusiasm beyond the limits of reality. We can compare this
  state with **daydreaming or the merging of feelings of happiness from fantasy and
  reality**." The dilemma: "the desire to own the object and the fact we don't own it."
- **Use is where the fantasy gets cashed.** "With the actual use of a product, we have the
  opportunity to experience our fantasies and dreams, which we have built up beforehand…
  **Consumers have the power to turn their personal objects into something special.**"
- **In fashion, the story outranks the product.** "For some products, especially when it
  comes to fashion, **a good story is of greater value than the function or the quality of
  the clothes.**" Storytelling's message to the audience: *"Hey, it's really only about
  you!"*
- **Trends are not styles.** "Design trends such as a popular style, and hip combinations or
  colors are **not the real trend**. These attributes are only **the tip of the iceberg**…
  **Changed behaviors, beliefs, and social forces make up a trend.**"
- **Scenarios are hypotheses, not forecasts.** "They aren't forecasts or strategies but are
  more like **hypotheses about various maps of the future**… described in such a way that we
  are able to identify the **risks and opportunities** in terms of certain strategic
  realities." And they should be written as **"captivating and, at the same time, convincing
  stories."**
- **Scale of exposure decides adoption.** "**The more decision makers we introduce to the
  scenarios, the better they recognize their importance.** Moreover, scenarios with easily
  comprehensible contents can be taken quickly into the organization as a whole."
- **Storytelling = story + telling — content and performance.** "We know traditional
  storytelling with a narrator… **Nonverbal reactions help the narrator to assess how well
  the listeners are following him**, so he can react spontaneously. **The digital world has
  none of these nonverbal reactions.** We must use other tools to establish empathy with a
  digital potential audience."
- **Empathy tools that put the designer in the body.** "The use of so-called **third-age
  suits**… allow designers and product managers to experience with their own body the
  limited physical capabilities of seniors." **Geriatric sensitivity training**: "glasses
  that simulate the clouding of the cornea or age-related macular degeneration," plus
  "gloves that simulate limited sensitivity and headphones that replicate a hearing
  impairment."

## Mental Models
- **Start with WHY.** Sinek, quoted: **"People don't buy what we produce — they buy why we
  produce something."** "The same model applies to internal communication (e.g. to digital
  transformation)."
- **Objects must satisfy emotional needs; stories are the transport.** "The idea behind it
  is that objects must satisfy our emotional needs, for one. Also, our **rational** stories
  are the best way to transport these needs."
- **A packed suitcase is a compressed personality.** "It is orderly, chaotic, an imitation,
  an original, it bears traces of past adventures… each of us has exactly the suitcase that
  fits us best and is thus **a mirror image of our life**."
- **Visionary projects galvanize, but are hard to steer.** "Visionary projects not only
  serve to inspire the entire organization and challenge existing technologies; they also
  help to **galvanize individual employees**… however, they are **more difficult to
  orchestrate because they deal with the unknown**."

## Anti-patterns
- Taking a direct user survey at face value. "Respondents in a direct user survey describe
  their own behavior as **an ideal type but do not show their true self**. We ask them about
  their goals and desires, but the reply only consists of **the most obvious insights**."
- Mistaking style attributes for trends.
- Designing future scenarios from inside your own business focus and professional blindness.
- Letting today's processes and structures constrain the step-2 scenario.
- Producing scenarios and then relapsing: "Organizations are often unable to initiate the
  transformation and **fall right back into their daily routines** — not least because the
  future changes had not been sufficiently prepared for." (Siemens' countermeasure:
  publishing *Pictures of the Futures* at regular intervals.)
- Long, non-linear digital stories with no images.

**The Golden Circle**

| Ring | Question | Brain region / faculty | What it holds |
|---|---|---|---|
| **WHY** (center) | "Why are we doing what we're doing? What is the designation, the purpose, or our belief?" | **The limbic system**, "located in the center of the brain and… guided by emotions and images. We're dealing with **behavior and trust, emotions, and decisions** here" | **PURPOSE** — dreams, goals, added value |
| **HOW** (middle) | "How are we doing what we're doing? What is the process of realizing the Why?" | "The How connects the two elements and explains the process of how something is done" | **Unique Selling Proposition, key values, workflow** |
| **WHAT** (outside) | "What are we doing? What are we doing to realize the Why?" | "The logically thinking brain… encompasses **rationality, logic, and language**" | **RESULT** — products, results, services |

"Successful companies keep the Why on center stage with a clear vision. In such companies,
**employees know why they get up in the morning and go to work**. **Spotify**, for example,
has the great mission of bringing music to the world." **Apple** is "another much-cited
example."

**"Wearable dreams" — the dream-story interview protocol**

| Stage | Prompts |
|---|---|
| **Personify the object** | "Imagine that their favorite piece of clothing was a person," then describe its personality: *What is the name of the favorite piece of clothing? How old is it, and what does it do for a living? Is it pretty shy or rather extroverted? Where was it born, and what is its marital status?* |
| **Purpose of that move** | "This way of talking about a product helped the interviewee to think about his favorite item of clothing and thus **transported the object into a social and emotional context**" |
| **Put the person in trouble** | "Imagine the person depicted by the item of clothing **in a difficult situation**; fortunately, the person had **super powers** that got him out of the situation." Describe a situation you would *not* want to get into; choose a specific role and take it on |
| **Set the scene** | *How do the surroundings look? Are there any other people? What items are lying about?* |
| **Output** | "The respondents wrote down a little story they made up about how the person got out of the difficult situation. They were asked **not to reflect too critically on what is possible or impossible in reality**. Ideally, the whole thing was pepped up with drawings. **The length, content, and depth of the story were irrelevant.**" |

Example answer from the book: *"My favorite piece of clothing is a bucket hat. Its name is
Alex. It is 34 years old and a small-time crook!"*

**Digital storytelling — the three-rule mindset**

| # | Rule |
|---|---|
| **1** | **Keep your story or message short!** |
| **2** | **Make sure the content of your story is linear and the narrative is clear.** |
| **3** | **Show, don't tell!** Use images to give the story more content |

Media range: "from multimedia films to audio broadcasts all the way to webinars." Build
**buyer personas** and ask potential customers: *Why buy from us? How do customers find us?
What questions are we asked during the sales process? What motivated the customers to search
for a solution?* "Emotional and intellectual elements associated with our brand are equally
important… flesh out the storytelling with **data and facts**," and consider "encouraging
our users to generate content."

## Worked Example — The Minsky suitcase applied to a soap design challenge

**The tool.** "A tried-and-tested tool for the generation of emotional stories is the
**Minsky suitcase**."

**The setup question:** *"Do we know where our suitcase is at the moment?"* "Most of us are
likely not thinking about our suitcase just now. It is somewhere in the basement or stored
in a closet… Once life has returned to the daily routine after vacation, the memories of
fine dining on the French Riviera or the white sand beaches in the Maldives quickly start to
fade away. **The last memory of the vacation consists of a few grains of sand hidden in the
inner pockets of the suitcase.** For a certain period of time, our suitcase was a synonym
for a different lifestyle, a better life, life as it ought to be: with pleasure, relaxation,
an uncomplicated and free schedule."

**The four types of use of items in a suitcase:**
1. **Things of everyday life** — toothbrush, socks, change of clothes.
2. **Things that are very important to us and do not take much space** — a photograph, a
   lucky charm, or a diary.
3. **Things we want to impress people with** — jewelry, a fashionable scarf, cool
   sunglasses.
4. **Some free space** for things we want to purchase on our trip.

**How to run it.** "We find old suitcases in the attic or buy a new one at the flea market.
In a second step, **we build up a relationship with the suitcase and its contents. Why was
the suitcase forgotten under the roof? What could its story be?** We take some time and
write **a small fictional story** about the object and its possible relationship with its
former owner."

**The filled-in case.** *In the suitcase: an old, heavy winter coat. The design challenge:
create a new soap.* "No restrictions are imposed on our design team in terms of the shape,
smell, color — but **it's not only the soap that is to be designed. The packaging and the
marketing concept must also be created.**"

**The story that emerged from the winter coat:**

> "An old lady is looking outside the window during the winter. She sees the road covered in
> ice while she is preparing for a dinner with her grandchildren. She is really looking
> forward to seeing them again and would like to be **the best host she can be**. However,
> she suddenly realizes that **she forgot an important ingredient**. She feels stressed
> because she will have to go out in the cold winter weather to buy the ingredient…"

**The two resulting product concepts:**
- **"Savon 1890"** — "a very simple, old-fashioned, handcrafted soap in plain packaging."
- **"Soap Crystals"** — "based on the experience with an old walking stick."

**A second filled-in brief — Lego's digital story**

| Field | Content |
|---|---|
| **Problem** | "Lend a new profile to an old children's toy" |
| **Campaign** | 90-minute **"Lego film"** |
| **Agency** | Warner Bros., Hollywood, CA |
| **Solution** | "A good movie for young and old with the message that **we are imaginative builders at any age**." |

**And the object that opens the chapter.** Pesce's **"La Mamma" chair, UP 5, from 1960** —
Peter's admiration for it is the chapter's own storytelling demonstration. "It has the shape
of a woman — hence the name 'La Mamma' — and was inspired by **prehistoric fertility
goddesses**. To implement his idea, Pesce used an innovative technology that allows the
creation of **large foam-molded parts without a load-bearing inner structure**. Thanks to a
vacuum chamber, the piece of furniture could be reduced to **10% of its volume** and thus
shrink-wrapped in air-tight film. This made it easy for the buyer to get the piece of
furniture home. **Only once the film was removed did 'La Mamma' unfold to its original and
final size and shape.**" The unpacking is the story.

## Key Takeaways
- Don't just focus on form and material — **turn the product into an experience**. The goal
  is to stimulate the imagination of the consumers.
- Speak to the various senses to create a holistic experience.
- Use the success factors **focus, simplicity, interactivity, and branding**.
- Create future scenarios for an inspiring framework; they help enshrine and communicate a
  vision.
- Establish empathy with the user — "empathy is the basis for every story because consumers
  want to satisfy their needs. **Arouse fantasies and desires.**"
- Pep the story up "with other people surrounding the user."
- Use tools such as the **Minsky suitcase** for new insights and stories.
- "Thousands of digital words are consumed every day. **Turn digital stories into a pivotal
  medium** in order to heighten user attention."
