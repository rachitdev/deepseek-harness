# Chapter 13 (Book §2.3) — How to Visualize Ideas and Stories

## Core Idea
*"But we don't know how to draw…!"* — "a statement Lilly often hears from her students and
attendees of her design thinking workshops." Her answer is the chapter's premise:
**"Visualizing is not the same as drawing"**; the instruction is to **"think with the
pen."**

"Visualization is a powerful tool for us to bring abstract information and interconnections
as well as data, processes, and strategies into a graphical (i.e. visual) form… **We process
visualized content faster, understand it better, and remember it longer.**"

The bar is deliberately low and the standard is deliberately functional: **"visualization is
neither art nor artistic drawing and it doesn't have to be beautiful, either. The point is
the simple communication and quick transport of content."**

And the licence: "When we recall our childhoods, making drawings was something we did every
day. So, at one time, we were **all courageous**… they are reduced to the essential…
**If we were able to draw as children, we can still do it today!**" The book's examples of
childhood drawings everyone reads correctly: *a house, milk, a mouse, Christmas, a fish, and
a dog.*

## Frameworks Introduced

**The five key design elements of a visualization**
1. **Text**
2. **Graphic elements**
3. **Icons and symbols**
4. **Figures and emotions**
5. **Color**

"We can depict all sorts of things with these design elements: ideas, stories, processes,
diagrams, and so on." (Full guidance per element in Reference Tables.)

**Four properties crucial in the creation of visualizations**
- "We **focus on what's important** — and leave out all that's unnecessary."
- "We are **specific** — we don't create vague drawings."
- "We make our pictures **comprehensible** — and are able to make linkages to the content."
- "We **kindle interest** — it is delightful to look at the pictures."

**The four applications of visualization**

| # | Application | The named practice |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Creative thinking** — "We outline our ideas and show interconnections" | **visual thinking** |
| 2 | **Presentations** — "We want to convey our knowledge to others comprehensibly" | **presenting** |
| 3 | **Documentation** — "We record the knowledge of the group" | **graphic recording** |
| 4 | **Exploring** — "We learn in common by presenting and documenting" | **visual facilitating** |

"Things get most exciting when **everybody in the group** begins to visualize and depict
what they think in pictures; this way, a **common shared image or a vision** can emerge."

**The four WH questions in the preparation phase**

| Question | Prompt |
|---|---|
| **Content** | What do we want to depict? |
| **Goal** | What is the purpose of the depiction? |
| **Target group** | Whom do we want to give information to? |
| **Medium** | What tools do we use? |

"For beginners, it is often difficult to create a good visualization spontaneously. It helps
to concern oneself first with **the core messages to be conveyed**. Think about what symbols
are important and which icons would fit."

## Key Concepts
- **The goals a quick sketch can serve** (the chapter's own list): outline many ideas in a
  brainstorming session · develop a common understanding · make abstract things tangible ·
  create a dialog by collaborative drawing · find surprising solutions with sketches · draw
  the function of a prototype · sketch a customer experience chain and bring it to life ·
  lighten the mood and make content more interesting · shape the story in a lively way.
- **Schematic, not photorealistic.** "If we want to visualize the function of the heart…
  we don't have to depict the exact anatomy photorealistically. It suffices to show the most
  important elements schematically: **where the heart is located and what its function
  is.** Visualizations should be created **fast and to the point**."
- **Direct the eye by omission.** "Good visualizations direct the eye to what is essential.
  **The trick here is to leave out what's nonessential.** This means no art in the sense of
  embellishing, decorating, or designing beautifully."
- **Icons vs. symbols.** "**Icons and symbols are visual abbreviations.** The **icon** is a
  reduced picture of an object. The rule for icons is: **The simpler, the better!** The icons
  are not there to replace or decorate the text. **Symbols** are signs that have **no
  resemblance to the actual object** and stand for something." The book's symbol examples:
  *idea · first aid · radioactive · love*.
- **Write first, then enclose.** For graphic elements (containers, folders, lines, arrows):
  **"Write the text first, then delimit or connect it with the graphical element."**
- **Figures carry the human centre.** "Because design thinking is always based on people,
  we've learned it is useful to be able to draw **stick figures, round figures, and those
  shaped like stars**. If you manage to draw **metaphorical elements**, you bring more
  character and emotion." And: "again, it is important **only to draw what's absolutely
  necessary**."
- **A good pen is half the battle.** "Poor pens will produce poor results, which will
  frustrate us right at the beginning." Media: "from the flip chart to a Post-it, on the
  iPad or just in our notebook."
- **Peter's workaround.** "Peter's handwriting is terrible, but he found his own technique
  of legibly writing on Post-its and adding to it through visualization. At first, he
  thought, **if nobody can guess what the writing implies, chances are better that the idea
  will be shared with others by including the sketch.**" He attended a **scribble course**
  and "learned that you don't have to be a good artist with fine handwriting to visualize
  well."

## Mental Models
- **Visualization is a language, not a talent.** "Visualization is like a language: You have
  to practice and apply it." And Wittgenstein, quoted directly: *"The meaning of a word is
  its use in the language."* "The same is true of symbols and icons — **the context and
  visual cultures in which we visualize play a major role.**"
- **Own your crookedness.** "We have the courage to draw our lines, our own circles and
  ellipses, even if they look terribly crooked sometimes. Because as soon as we have
  practiced a little, we might be able to get a handle on the crookedness and **turn it into
  our trademark**." Be creative with your own symbols and icons rather than relying on a
  general library.
- **Train imagination, not only technique.** "Although we do live in a world of images, we
  have a hard time imagining even very simple things, let alone complex stories and themes.
  This is why we must **not only train the actual activity of visualizing but also our
  imagination, thinking in pictures and images! Every day!**"
- **Drawing crosses language and culture.** "Solve communication problems on
  interdisciplinary teams with drawings; this way, you can **overcome language and cultural
  barriers**."
- **The wall is a shared artifact and needs an author.** "Each team structures the space on
  its own. The facilitator can give hints, so that, at the end, **the 'journey' is
  comprehensible even to outsiders.**"

## Anti-patterns
- Trying to make it beautiful; embellishing, decorating, designing.
- Vague drawings. Specificity is one of the four properties.
- Icons used as decoration or as a replacement for the text.
- Too much color. "For a composition, we recommend using only a few different colors…
  **Too much color can be confusing.**"
- Cramped layout. "We use the space generously. Again, the motto is: **Less is more!**"
- Small writing on a flip chart — the book sets a minimum height.
- Poor or dried-out pens, which frustrate beginners at the outset.
- Following one training course's approach as "the only valid one" instead of drawing.

## Reference Tables

**Guidance per design element**

| Element | The book's rules |
|---|---|
| **(1) Text** | Pay attention to legibility and select **basic font types**; write from left to right, starting **top left**; leave enough space between letters and lines; formulate **short and simple sentences** with familiar words; provide structure by means of **headings and visual blocks**; use stimuli such as different styles and colors. **"WRITE IN PRINT."** Vary line widths and styles — *light · regular · bold · extended*. |
| **(2) Graphic elements** | "Simple graphical elements are **containers, folders, lines, and arrows**. They help to establish interconnections and order." Write the text first, then delimit or connect. |
| **(3) Icons and symbols** | Visual abbreviations. Icon = reduced picture of an object; **the simpler, the better**. Symbol = a sign with no resemblance to the object, standing for something. |
| **(4) Figures and emotions** | Stick figures, round figures, star-shaped figures; metaphorical elements bring character and emotion; draw only what's absolutely necessary. |
| **(5) Color** | Only a few colors; used to **highlight or illustrate interconnections**. "Visualization works best when the important stuff is highlighted, such as by **coloring or adding a border, underlining, or cross-hatching**," or by coloring the background. |

**Chart selection**

| Job | Chart type |
|---|---|
| Transparent depiction of **numbers or figures** | **Bar charts or pie charts** |
| Depicting **structures and processes** | **Organizational charts** (e.g. arrows) |
| Depicting **relative sizes** and positioning objects among one another | **Portfolio charts** |

"Charts can help us compare **numbers, sequences, size ratios, processes, and structures**.
Together with the main lines and connections, they are quite compelling."

**Flip chart technique (expert tip)**

| Rule | Detail |
|---|---|
| Placement | "Depending on the motivation and the goal the theme can be drawn **at the top or in the center**." |
| Pens | "Using high-quality pens and **checking that they all work well** will pay off." |
| Letter size | "**At least 3 to 3.5 cm / 1.5 inches, or even bigger.**" |
| Pen grip | "Set it down **squarely** and write with the **wide side**." |
| Enrichment | Frames, graphical bullet characters, simple symbols; "if chalk is at hand, we can add color afterward." |
| Goal | "Structure the flip chart in a meaningful way and create an attractive composition." · **LESS IS MORE!** |

**Working the big wall.** "In design thinking, we often have very large walls with many
visualizations, such as **life-size personas, insights from customer interviews, images from
surveys together with sketches of ideas, and customer experience chains**… Often, **large
Post-its are sufficient to show where the participants stand in the process**. Our
experience has been good with **connecting elements and structuring grids**, which, in
conjunction with lines and arrows, result in an overall picture."

## Worked Example — Lilly's visualization exercises, and the blood-circulation sketch

**The heart / blood-circulation demonstration.** The book's own worked artifact for the
"schematic beats photorealistic" rule: it presents "an example of **blood circulation** in a
very simple depiction, **so it's a joy to understand the content**" — reduced to *vessels*
and the flow between them. The design decision made explicit: show only *where the heart is
located and what its function is*, not the anatomy.

**Exercise 1 — the child-friendly can opener.** "Lilly motivates her students to use
visualization for specific design challenges. **How would a child-friendly can opener
look**, for instance?" The teaching payoff comes from volume: **"The large number of
sketches quickly show which lines are identified and what symbolism is clear or
irritating."** The exercise is a legibility test of the visual vocabulary, not a design
competition.

**Exercise 2 — the phone call.** "Another exercise is to **put into a picture what we were
just speaking about with somebody on the phone.**"

**Sources of practice the chapter names.**
- *Comics* — "ideal if you seek inspiration for **facial expressions** (e.g. **Calvin &
  Hobbes** or similar comics collections)."
- *Web competitions* — "can be motivating if we want to become better. Usually, they are
  about visualizing individual concepts, which are then published on **Facebook or
  Instagram**."

**The preparation, filled in for the can-opener challenge, using the four WH questions:**

| WH question | Answer for this exercise |
|---|---|
| **Content** | The child-friendly can opener and how a child would hold and use it |
| **Goal** | Test which lines and symbols read clearly and which irritate |
| **Target group** | Fellow students and workshop attendees who must interpret the sketch unaided |
| **Medium** | Pen and paper / Post-its, produced fast and in quantity |

**The expert tip that closes the chapter — "Just do it! Now."** "If you want to learn how to
visualize, you'll find much on offer in books and training courses that are dedicated to
presenting **one specific approach as the only valid one**. We, by contrast, rely on our
design thinking mindset. Our motto is: **Just do it! Now.** … So just do it and begin
immediately sketching and visualizing — **everywhere and every day**, because this is the
only way to immerse ourselves in the new language."

## Key Takeaways
- Establish a culture that is conducive to and **acknowledges the courage to draw**.
- Create simple and clear drawings. **Visualization is not art but a means to an end.**
- Systematically replace key concepts or sentences with images, icons, and stick figures.
- Take advantage of the impact of images on the brain: images leave a deeper impact.
- Solve communication problems on interdisciplinary teams with drawings — overcoming
  language and cultural barriers.
- Use drawings and visualized stories to communicate knowledge in meetings.
- Sketch something instead of cumbersomely writing it down — **because it's faster**.
- Practice and apply it like a language. Just do it! Now.
