# Chapter 18 (Book §3.1) — Why Systems Thinking Helps to Understand Complexity

## Core Idea
"We begin our chapter on designing the future with systems thinking, although the approach
and the mindset are **at least as old as the design thinking paradigm**. We are firmly
convinced, though, that **the basic conditions and interaction of systems must be taken into
account more and more** when we develop our future products, services, and business
ecosystems. **The use of a converging mindset of systems thinking and design thinking will
be pivotal in many areas.**"

The chapter's founding memory is Peter's, from his time as a student at **Munich Technical
University**: a lecture discussion about **the Challenger explosion on January 28, 1986**.
"It was determined at the time that **the system had not been adapted to safety needs**, and
this was why the terrible disaster occurred. Peter often thinks about the disaster. **How
complex are things when a self-driving car is on the road? How many systems must interact
with one another?**"

The definition used: **"We use the term 'system' to describe the interaction of several
components (system elements) in a larger unit and its environment. All these elements fulfill
a specific function or a purpose."** "Many things can be understood as systems: products,
services, business models, processes, and even **our family or the organization in which we
work**." The book uses **"systems thinking" and "systems engineering" synonymously to a large
extent.**

## Frameworks Introduced

**The seven-step systems thinking problem-solving cycle**

| Step | What happens |
|---|---|
| **1. Initial problem** | "A specific initial problem from the real world marks the beginning. With complex problems, the real world is usually **multidimensional, dynamic, and nonlinear.**" |
| **2. Mapping of reality** | "We try to understand the system and map the reality. This mapping, or **system representation**, helps us to understand the situation." |
| **3. Situation analysis** | "Comprehending the situation step by step — **from rough to detail**." Methods: **mathematical models and simulation · experiments and prototypes · abstract thinking · data analytics · summary of findings (e.g. SWOT)**. "Important for finding out where there are gaps with regard to the target state." |
| **4. Goal formulation (decision-making criteria)** | "On the basis of [the SWOT] we formulate the goals to be fulfilled by the solution. This way, we obtain **the decision-making criteria for the assessment of the solution.**" |
| **5. Search for a solution** | "**Only once the problem and the situation are really known** do we begin with the search for a solution." Identify "solutions that actually do fit into **the solution space**"; find several — **thinking in variants**; **synthesis & analysis** generate the solution variants. |
| **6. Evaluation** | "We apply decision-making criteria to the evaluation. Tools and methods such as **evaluation matrix, logical argumentation, simulations, experiments** have proven their effectiveness." |
| **7. Decision / recommendation** | "Based on the evaluation, a recommendation is given and a decision is made. If the solution meets our requirements and solves the problem, that's good; **otherwise, we iterate the process until we have solved the problem completely.**" |

The cycle spans a **problem space** (steps 1–4) and a **solution space** (steps 5–7).
"The output of our representation can be documented as **the operational concept** (ref.
**ISO/IEC/IEEE 291:48**)."

**Feedback loops instead of cause/effect chains.** "**Unlike linear models, which consist of
cause/effect chains (A causes B causes C causes D, etc.), in systems thinking the world is
seen as a connecting unit with various relationships (A causes B causes C causes A, etc.).**
The advantage of a model with feedback is that it **does not just map what happens at what
time, but yields information on how something happens and why it happens.** In this way, we
learn how a system behaves."

Stabilization rule: "Over time, feedback loops increase the response; it can go in both
directions: positive and negative. For this reason, **it is important to stabilize the
feedback loops. Using the feedback only for the optimization of the gap between the target
state and the actual situation is a good way of stabilizing**" — *actual level → corrective
action → desired level.*

**Five core questions for implementing systems**
- Which gaps affect our systems, and to what degree?
- Do we know the gaps and are we able to describe them?
- How do we monitor the gaps?
- What possibilities do we have to close the gaps?
- **How great is the effort to close the gaps, and how much time do we have to do so?**

**The three key questions for understanding systems** (see Worked Example).

**Three basic questions posed to the system environment**
1. **What does the system produce? Is the result desirable?**
2. **How does the interaction of the system with us as human beings work? Does the
   interaction correspond to our needs?**
3. **What happens within the system? How do machines and sensors interact with one another?
   What do we want to achieve?**

## Key Concepts
- **System delimitation is the central modeling task.** "Delimitation of systems is a
  central task of modeling. Especially because **effectiveness and efficiency today are more
  important than ever** for the development of new systems."
- **Complexity multiplies error probability.** "**It is obvious that the error probability of
  complex systems is greater than that of its individual elements.** With the use of
  **modules and sub-elements and the introduction of redundancies**, we attempt to reduce the
  probability of failure of the system as a whole."
- **The boundary defines SWOT.** "We can influence and change the elements **within** the
  system boundaries. **The elements within the system boundaries are the strengths and
  weaknesses known to us. The elements outside these boundaries are the opportunities and
  risks that affect our system.**"
- **Engineered systems exist for a reason.** "They implement a desired or required function.
  For example, we want to build a self-driving car for a stress-free drive from point A to
  point B. As an alternative, we can **integrate the autonomous vehicle in a system of means
  of transportation and won't ever again have to search for a parking space**, because the
  vehicle will permanently be on the road as part of a larger system."
- **Sensor interaction as the concrete case.** "A **rain and cold sensor**, for instance, in
  combination with **a camera or radar**, can provide information on road conditions and thus
  be **an indicator for the speed to be chosen. To achieve this, all components must
  interact.**"
- **Where forecasting breaks.** "With regard to **self-contained technical systems,
  complexity is manageable. But as soon as nature as such and our social systems come into
  play, forecasts are far more difficult.** Traffic will increase when we no longer park our
  autonomous vehicles but have them circulate in the cities. **It's our own motives in a
  system that are difficult to explore and comprehend.**"
- **Complex vs. simple systems.** The book's own classification places **an airport** among
  simple systems and **a nuclear power plant** and **autonomous driving** among complex ones;
  its other named complex systems include **coral reefs**.
- **Stakeholder communication is continuous, not terminal.** "In systems thinking, a strong
  focus is on the **continuous communication with the stakeholders**. This means that **their
  consent can be obtained at an early stage during critical phases of the development.**"

## Mental Models
- **The mindset of a systems thinker** (the chapter's own list):
  - "We always have our eyes on **the big picture**."
  - "We take the time to **penetrate even complex interconnections**."
  - "We search for **the 'key' to the system**."
  - "We consider facts from **various perspectives**."
  - "We think positively of a way to improve the system and **don't complain when it doesn't
    work**."
  - "We accept that **change takes place gradually** and that interconnections also trigger
    changes."
  - "We **identify the effects that are triggered by an action**."
  - "We check the results and **improve the result with every iteration**."
  - "**We reflect on our way of thinking because it affects what will happen.**"
- **Perspective switching is the skill.** "A good systems thinker masters different ways of
  thinking and concentrates correspondingly on the requirements on hand. **He switches the
  perspective, such as from individual parts to the whole, or from structures to
  processes.**"
- **You are inside the system you are designing.** "It makes us design thinkers more aware
  that **we ourselves are a part of a system in its environment**. With our actions, we affect
  the entire system; we can intelligently interact with it; **but we also realize that other
  stakeholders/observers might have a different view of the system as a whole.**"
- **Switching modes changes the focus.** "**With this switch, we change our focus from a
  product-centric to a people-centric approach.**"
- **Both approaches are double diamonds.** "On closer examination, the two approaches are not
  so different after all. **Both follow the double diamond model and switch between divergent
  and convergent ways of thinking.**"

## Anti-patterns
- Modeling a complex problem as a linear cause/effect chain.
- Leaving feedback loops unstabilized so the response amplifies without correction.
- Searching for a solution before the problem and situation are really known.
- Settling on a single solution instead of thinking in variants.
- Treating the first system picture as final rather than as a prototype.
- Holding one view of the system: **"Take different views and perspectives. Only one view is
  surely the wrong one."**
- Long planning instead of doing — the design-thinking corrective this chapter names.
- Assuming that mastering the technical system means mastering the social one; motives are
  the hard part.

## Reference Tables

**Systems thinking vs. design thinking — the complementary mindset**

| Systems thinking | The complementary mindset | Design thinking |
|---|---|---|
| Focus on **the system** | Different focus | Focus on **users and needs of people** |
| **Systematic analytical** problem-solving cycle | Clearly defined but different (problem-solving) process | **Intuitive, circular** problem-solving cycle |
| **White box** view with a focus on **solution space** | Design and architecture of systems | **Black box** view with focus on **problem statement** |
| **Gradual refinement** of the system | Iterative procedure | **Carry out a great many iterations quickly** |
| Create clarity through the consideration of the system and **changes over time** | Create clarity | Create a **common understanding** and clarity |
| Establish clear structures and **anticipate life cycle considerations** | Process understanding important (be mindful of process) | Process understanding is important |
| **Mapping and modeling** of the system | Visualization | **Visualization and prototyping** are important |
| Use of methods from systems thinking | Use of various tools and methods | Use of methods from design thinking |
| **Collaboration and the exchange of information with stakeholders is pivotal** | Interdisciplinary collaboration on the team | **Initiate radical collaboration** |
| **System understanding helps reduce uncertainty** | Positive dealing with uncertainty | **Perform experiments in order to learn** |
| Conduct **project management in a target-oriented way** | Focus on action | **Implementation-oriented and solution-oriented action** |

**What the two share:** "Coverage of the same or similar thematic areas" · "The purpose and
goal is the solution of (complex) problems and the simultaneous definition and expansion of
the solution space" · "It's important to **clarify the critical variables and functionalities
at the onset of the project to reduce risks.**"

**The difference in what iteration is for:** "**Iteration in systems thinking aims at
gradual refinement; in design thinking, many iterations enable us to understand the situation
better and to approximate a potential solution.**"

**Which mindset leads, by development phase**

| Development phase | Lead |
|---|---|
| **Ideas phase → conception phase → technical feasibility** | **Design thinking** is "a strong tool in an early phase (conception and feasibility)… especially when the issue is **simple functionalities or the interaction with a potential user**" |
| **System definition (requirements engineering) → detailed design (modeling, simulation) → production → integration, validation → market launch** | **Systems thinking** "is predestined for many developments" — "for **the interaction of components, the simulation of complex processes, or the engineering of requirements**" |

**What design thinking contributes that is "usually not part of systems thinking"**
- "Arriving at new solution approaches that are **brilliant in their simplicity**"
- "Focusing on **systems-in-systems** with an alignment to individuals or entire groups
  (**360 degrees**) in terms of empathy"
- "The iterative approach in relation to the **building of simple prototypes** during problem
  solving"
- "**Doing it and not planning a long time**"

**"The combination of the two mindsets results in new opportunities and better problem
solutions!"** Its topic split: *systems thinking →* stakeholders, management, requirements
engineering, interaction of components, integration/verification/validation; *shared →*
understanding the problem, analysis of customer needs, problem solution, ecosystem design;
*design thinking →* radical collaboration, interaction with the user and usability, radically
new products, services and business models, conception.

## Worked Example — The Jones family as a system, and the three key questions

**Why a family.** "The system of a family is a good example. **We know the actors of our
family. Living together consists of complex interactions, and we have the possibility of
changing the system through our actions. In addition, people who do not belong to our family
have a different perception of our clan than we have inside the family.**"

The book's own labelled model: **System: The Jones family**, with the observed elements
**Communication · Cohesion · Relationship.**

**The generic system map it instantiates.** A boundary encloses **elements**, each carrying
**variables**; outside sit the **influencers** — *"family member, employer, decision makers,
etc."* Inside the boundary: the strengths and weaknesses we can change. Outside: the
opportunities and risks that act on us.

**The three key questions for the understanding of systems**, with the book's own instruction
for each — note that each ends in a list you are meant to physically write:

| Key question | The question, in full | What you produce |
|---|---|---|
| **Relationships (interconnections)** | "How are the things and elements connected with one another?" | "We compile **a list of the insights and possible solution aspects**." |
| **Perspectives** | "In what different ways can the situation be understood?" | "We compile **a list of variants**." |
| **Framework conditions** | "What is inside the intervention system and what is outside of it?" | "We **delimit the systems from one another and describe them**." |

**Answering them for the Jones family.** *Relationships:* who talks to whom, and which
cohesion and relationship links carry the load — the insight list captures where an
intervention would propagate. *Perspectives:* the inside view versus the neighbours', the
employer's, the school's — the variant list captures each reading of "how this family is
doing." *Framework conditions:* the employer and decision makers sit **outside** the
intervention system, so they are opportunities and risks to be monitored, not levers to be
pulled.

**Then the three environment questions.** *What does the system produce — is the result
desirable? How does its interaction with us as human beings work — does it correspond to our
needs? What happens within it?* The same three questions transfer unchanged from a family to
an autonomous-driving fleet; only the sensors change.

**The switching advice for practitioners.** "We recommend using systems thinking **in tandem
with** design thinking in the case of complex (wicked) problems. How strong the combination
of the mindsets should be depends on the project requirements or personal preferences…
- **To design thinking experts such as Peter**, we recommend **switching the thinking mode
  and trying out the system-oriented problem-solving cycle, especially in phases of
  stagnation or when the overall picture is unclear.**
- **In the case of a strong personal stamp with the systems thinking mindset**, it is as
  important to **confirm the findings at least once by means of the design thinking
  problem-solving cycle and to expand the creative framework. In most cases, this adds new
  insights, which can only find their way into the problem solution in an intuitive
  problem-solving cycle.**"

## Key Takeaways
- Define the system, system boundaries, and factors that influence the system.
- Map out the relationships — **within and outside** the system boundaries.
- Make sure **all stakeholders** in the system are listed.
- Look at the problem holistically; **assume that it is complex per se**.
- Even a complex, multidimensional, nonlinear, dynamic problem can be mapped and modeled as a
  system **in a simplified way**.
- Start with the simple stuff and go **from rough to detail**.
- Begin the search for a solution **once you have understood the problem** (or aspects
  thereof).
- **Always think in variants** when searching for a solution.
- A graphical system representation helps to understand the problem and communicate the
  solution.
- **Consider your first system picture to be a prototype** that will be continuously tested
  and improved.
- Use a wide range of concepts, methods, and tools; take different views and perspectives.
- **Combine systems thinking and design thinking into a common mindset.**
