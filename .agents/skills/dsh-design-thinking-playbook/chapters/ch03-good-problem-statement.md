# Chapter 3 (Book §1.3) — How to Get a Good Problem Statement

## Core Idea
Peter's lesson as a novice facilitator: he wanted to find good solutions, not make
problems worse — until he saw that the problem definition *is* the lever. Three
prerequisites for good solutions:

1. The design thinking team must have **understood** the problem.
2. The design challenge must be **defined to allow useful solutions to be developed**.
3. The potential solution must **fit the defined design space and design scope**.

The chapter gives the calibration tool (WHY expands / HOW narrows), the taxonomy of
problems (well-defined / ill-defined / wicked), the design brief template, and the
three-step procedure for writing a "How might we…" question from a persona.

## Frameworks Introduced

**The WHY/HOW ladder.** Repeatedly asking **"Why?" expands** the creative framework;
asking **"How?" scales it down.** Ask why enough times and you leave your comfort zone
almost immediately and land on earth-shaking, wicked problems.

**Three problem types**

| Type | Structure | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| **Simple / well-defined** | Clear problem → different paths → **one** solution | One correct solution; the solution strategy can vary |
| **Poorly defined / ill-defined** | Unclear problem → different paths → **different** solutions | More than one correct remedy; the search itself can take quite different routes. *Most problems in design thinking and daily work are of this type* |
| **Complex / wicked** | Unknown problem → different paths → **a partial solution that helps make the problem definition clearer** | The problem is the search for the problem; preliminary definitions are used and the understanding of the solution changes the understanding of the problem again — co-evolution |

**The design brief** — "the translation of a problem into a structured task." Creating a
good one "is already a small design thinking project in itself." Sometimes drawn up for
the users, sometimes for the design thinking team; get different, preferably
interdisciplinary opinions and converge through iterations.

**Three-step procedure to formulate the question** (see Worked Example).

## Key Concepts
- **Design thinking suits all problem types** — products, services, processes, individual
  functions, comprehensive customer experiences. What differs is the goal: *a product
  designer wants to satisfy customer needs; an engineer is more interested in defining the
  specifications.*
- **Linear/analytical problem solving fails on wicked problems** — "because the problem is
  the search for the problem, you're pulled every which way." The design thinking
  countermeasures are the "How might we…?" question and the "why" technique.
- **Termination is usually a resource failure, not a method failure.** When no solution is
  found despite design thinking, "limited resources such as money and time are usually the
  reasons for the termination of the process" — hence: devote enough time and energy to
  working out a suitable problem definition.
- **More detail in the brief buys speed but costs radicalism.** "The description of the
  challenge must be seen as a minimum requirement. Further details help to expedite the
  problem solving. The disadvantage here is that the degree of freedom in relation to the
  radicalism of a new solution is limited."
- **When in doubt about scope, just start.** "If the challenge is too narrowly conceived,
  the team will expand the problem in the first iteration. If the challenge is conceived
  too broadly, the team will narrow it down." The ideal starting point is where you leave
  the comfort zone. Frame it as the spectrum from *"improve a detail"* (the cap of a
  ballpoint pen) to *"eliminate paper and save the forests"* / "solve the world's water
  problem."

## Mental Models
- **Ill-defined is the normal case.** Design a process for many-solutions problems, not
  for one-solution problems.
- **Iterate the problem, not just the solution.** With wicked problems there are already
  iterations *inside* the problem definition.
- **Change perspective to find a challenge.** Not "what is broken?" but "are there any
  opportunities where other people only see problems?"

## Anti-patterns
- Setting the design challenge at the level where you already know the answer.
- Over-specifying the brief and then being surprised the output is incremental.
- Applying linear analysis to a wicked problem.
- Agonizing over scope instead of starting the first iteration.

## Reference Tables

**Prompts for finding a design challenge**

| Route | Prompts |
|---|---|
| Improve a daily customer-experience chain | "How might we improve the online shopping experience of a shoe retailer?" · "…the online booking portal for the car ferry from A to B?" · "…customer satisfaction with the ticket app for public transport in Singapore?" |
| Change perspective | What if…? · What might be possible? · What would change behavior? · What would be an offer if business ecosystems connected with each other? · What is the impact of a promotion? · What will happen afterward? · Are there any opportunities where other people only see problems? |
| Interrogate an existing product/service (e.g. buying a music subscription) | What does the music behavior of a user look like? · How does the customer get information on new offerings? · How and where will the customer install it? · How does the customer use it? · How does the customer act when it does not work as expected? · How satisfied is the customer with the entire customer experience chain? |

**Design brief — the six blocks and their core questions**

| Block | Core questions |
|---|---|
| Design space and design scope | Which activities are to be supported, and for whom? What do we want to learn about the user? |
| Existing approaches to the problem | What already exists, and how can elements of it help our own solution? What is missing in existing solutions? |
| Design principles | What are important hints for the team (where more creativity is demanded; which feature potential users should really try out)? Are there limitations, and which core functions are essential? Whom do we want to involve, and at what point in the design process? |
| Scenarios associated with the solution | What does a desirable future and vision look like? Which scenarios are plausible and possible? |
| Next steps and milestones | By when should a solution have been worked out? Are there steering committee meetings from which we can get valuable feedback? |
| Potential implementation challenges | Who must be involved at an early stage? What is the culture like for dealing with radical solution proposals, and how great is its willingness to take risks? |

The brief's short-profile fields the book shows: *Problem · Target customer · Goals ·
Available (resources) · Competition · Budget · Schedule · Next steps.*

**Brainstorming operators for widening or narrowing (the SCAMPER-style matrix)**

| Method | How can we solve our problem? | How can we use an existing solution? |
|---|---|---|
| Minimize | reduce it? | reduce an existing solution? |
| Maximize | expand it? | expand an existing solution? |
| Transform | mentally transfer it to another area? | transfer a solution existing in another area to my problem? |
| Combine | combine it with other problems? | combine several existing solutions? |
| Modify / adapt | modify it? | modify an existing solution? |
| Rearrange / invert | change or invert its internal order? | change or invert the order of an existing solution? |
| Substitute | substitute a partial problem? | substitute a part of an existing solution? |

## Worked Example

**(a) The can opener — the WHY/HOW ladder in both directions.**
Design challenge: *a better can opener that everybody in the family likes using.*

Expand with **WHY?** — and you arrive, very fast, at wicked problems:
- How can we stop hunger in the world?
- How can we prevent so much food from being thrown away?

Narrow with **HOW?**:
- How can the can be opened with a rotating mechanism?
- How can the can be opened without any additional device?

The lesson: the same object sits on a ladder whose rungs range from world hunger to a
rotating mechanism. Pick the rung that leaves the comfort zone but still admits a
buildable prototype.

**(b) Lilly's Post-it problem — the three-step formulation.**

- **Step 1 — Who is the user, and what is the central task?** Lilly holds innovation
  workshops as a facilitator. (Reflect on the created persona; define who the user really
  is and what her needs are.)
- **Step 2 — Apply the WH questions.** *What* does the user want to achieve? (Edit?
  Create? Evaluate? Analyze? Store? Share?) → Lilly wants to **document** the information
  from the workshops. *How?* (Copy? Speech recognition? Text recognition? Image
  recognition? Photographing?)
- **Step 3 — Formulate the question.** → **"How can I digitally capture a Post-it and
  share it?"**

**(c) The BIC ballpoint pen — "minimize" taken to the limit, and where innovation still
lives.** For the BIC, everything unnecessary was left out; three indispensable parts
remain: the refill, the holder, and a cap that also serves as a clip. An ingenious product
unaltered for more than 50 years. Is there room left for innovation? Yes — the hole in the
tip of the cap. It was not always there. It was designed so that a small child who
swallows the cap and gets it stuck in the windpipe can still get sufficient air through
the little hole. BIC caps have had holes for more than 24 years. *Innovation can live in a
job-to-be-done nobody stated (don't kill the user), not in the product's function at all.*

## Key Takeaways
- Question in the form of "Why?" and "How might we?" to grasp and understand the problem.
- Classify the problem first: well-defined, ill-defined, or wicked — the process differs.
- A design brief trades radicalism for speed; choose the trade deliberately.
- Formulate the challenge from the persona: who → WH questions → question.
- If the scope feels wrong, start anyway; the first iteration will correct it.
